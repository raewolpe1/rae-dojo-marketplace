# Rising Strong

## Core Concept
If you are brave enough, often enough, you will fall. Rising Strong is the process of getting back up — and it is the same regardless of the magnitude of the fall.

The process has three stages. **The Reckoning** is walking into your story — recognising that you are in an emotional experience, getting curious about what you are feeling, and understanding how those feelings connect to your thoughts and behaviour. The reckoning requires emotional awareness: noticing the physical signals (tight chest, racing heart, desire to withdraw) and naming the emotion rather than acting it out or numbing it.

**The Rumble** is owning your story — getting honest about the narratives you are constructing about the experience, challenging the confabulations and assumptions, and determining what is truth, what is self-protection, and what needs to change. The central practice is "the story I'm telling myself is..." — surfacing the first draft narrative that your brain has constructed. These first drafts are almost always about blame (someone else caused this), shame (I am fundamentally flawed), or self-protection (I need to withdraw, attack, or numb). The rumble is the discipline of examining these stories with honesty and courage.

**The Revolution** is writing a brave new ending — integrating the learning from the rumble into how you live, lead, and relate. The revolution is not a single dramatic moment. It is the daily practice of choosing courage over comfort, choosing what is right over what is easy, and choosing to show up again despite the risk of another fall.

The key insight is that the process cannot be skipped or shortcutted. Organisations that jump from failure to "lessons learned" without reckoning with the emotion and rumbling with the story produce superficial learning that does not prevent the same failure from recurring. People who bypass the reckoning — who move straight from fall to "I'm fine" — carry the unprocessed emotion into their next interaction.

## How to Apply
1. **Reckon.** When you fall — when something goes wrong, when you are criticised, when you fail — stop. Notice what you are feeling. Name it. Do not act on it yet.
2. **Write your SFD (stormy first draft).** What is the story you are telling yourself about what happened? Write it down. Include the blame, the shame, the self-protection. Get it all on paper without editing.
3. **Rumble.** Challenge the SFD. What is true? What is assumption? What am I making up? What do I not know? What is my part in this? What information am I missing?
4. **Write the brave new ending.** Based on what you learned in the rumble, what will you do differently? How will this experience change how you show up?
5. **Practice.** Rising strong is not a one-time event. It is a daily practice that builds resilience over time.

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant receives harsh criticism from a client about a deliverable. The consultant's first reaction is shame and withdrawal — avoiding the client's calls and over-preparing the next document to the point of paralysis.
**Application:** Reckoning: the consultant recognises the physical shame response (tight chest, desire to disappear) and names it: "I feel ashamed." SFD: "The story I'm telling myself is that I'm not good enough, that the client has lost confidence in me, and that my reputation is ruined." Rumble: examining the SFD — the deliverable had genuine weaknesses (legitimate feedback), but "not good enough" and "ruined reputation" are shame-driven catastrophising. The client gave specific, actionable feedback, not a judgement of fundamental worth. Brave new ending: contact the client, acknowledge the weaknesses, present a revised approach, and ask what the client needs going forward.
**Result:** The consultant moves from shame-driven paralysis to guilt-driven action — owning the weakness, making amends, and showing up again. The relationship is strengthened, not destroyed.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Skip the reckoning.
**Why:** Moving from failure to action without processing the emotion produces "emotional debt" — unresolved feelings that leak into future interactions as defensiveness, resentment, or avoidance.
