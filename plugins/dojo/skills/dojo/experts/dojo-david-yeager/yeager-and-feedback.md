# Yeager and Feedback

## When to Use
- When someone needs to deliver critical feedback and is either avoiding it or delivering it destructively.
- When the wise feedback formula needs explaining or applying to a professional context.
- When feedback practices need redesigning at an organisational level.

## Core Concept
Yeager's wise feedback research, conducted with Walton and Cohen, demonstrated that the frame around critical feedback determines whether it motivates or demoralises. The frame they identified — known as wise feedback — consists of two simultaneous signals: "I have high standards" AND "I believe you can meet them."

The research showed that when critical feedback was accompanied by the wise feedback message — "I am giving you these comments because I have high expectations and I believe you can reach them" — students from marginalised backgrounds (who are most likely to experience belonging uncertainty) responded with significantly more effort and produced significantly better work. Without the frame, the same critical feedback was interpreted through a belonging lens: "They are criticising me because they do not think I belong here."

The wise feedback formula is not a script. It is a genuine stance. If the person delivering feedback does not actually believe in the recipient's capacity, the words ring hollow. Wise feedback requires the deliverer to hold two beliefs simultaneously: this work is not good enough (high standards) and this person can do better (genuine belief in capacity). If either belief is missing, the feedback degrades — into either false praise (low standards, high belief) or harsh criticism (high standards, low belief).

For managers, consultants, and programme leaders, the implication extends beyond individual interactions. Feedback systems — performance reviews, project debriefs, evaluation reports — can be designed to embed wise feedback structurally, so the framing does not depend on individual skill or goodwill.

## How to Apply
1. **Lead with the frame.** "Before delivering criticism, explicitly state: 'I am giving you this feedback because I have high standards for this work and I believe you can meet them.'"
2. **Make it genuine.** "If you do not actually believe the person can improve, the frame is empty. Examine whether you genuinely hold both beliefs."
3. **Be specific in both directions.** "Specify what needs improving AND what the person is doing well. Vague criticism triggers belonging uncertainty; specific criticism enables action."
4. **Build the frame into systems.** "Embed the wise feedback message in feedback templates, review processes, and evaluation reporting standards."

## Examples
**Situation:** A senior consultant needs to give a junior colleague feedback on a report that is significantly below standard. He is torn between being honest (which he fears will crush the junior's confidence) and softening the feedback (which he knows will not improve the work).
**Application:** Yeager would say: "You are stuck in the enforcer-protector trap. The enforcer version: 'This is not good enough. Here is everything that is wrong.' The junior hears: 'I am not capable.' The protector version: 'This is a good start, just a few tweaks.' The junior hears: 'This is fine,' and does not learn. The wise feedback version is different, and it is more honest than either: 'I am going to give you detailed feedback on this report because I hold this work to a high standard and I have seen enough of your thinking to believe you can meet that standard. The current draft has three significant problems: [specific issues]. Here is what each section needs to look like: [specific guidance]. I want you to revise it and bring it back by Thursday. If you get stuck on the methodology section, come to me and we will work through it together.' That version does not soften the criticism. The problems are named clearly. But the frame changes what the criticism means. It is not 'you are inadequate.' It is 'you are capable of better, and I am going to help you get there.' The frame is not a rhetorical trick. You have to actually believe it. If you do not believe the junior can produce good work, you have a staffing decision to make, not a feedback conversation."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use the wise feedback formula as a script without genuine belief.
**Why:** People detect insincerity. "I believe you can meet my high standards" said without conviction is worse than no frame at all.

**Don't:** Avoid critical feedback to protect relationships.
**Why:** Withholding honest feedback is a protector stance. It protects the recipient from discomfort but deprives them of the information they need to grow.
