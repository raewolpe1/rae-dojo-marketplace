# Institutional Mindsets

## When to Use
- When an organisation's culture contradicts the growth messages it espouses.
- When individual-level mindset interventions fail because the institutional context undermines them.
- When diagnosing whether an organisation operates from a fixed or growth mindset at the structural level.

## Core Concept
Yeager's research on institutional mindsets extends Dweck's individual-level construct to organisations. An institution has a fixed mindset when its structures, policies, and norms communicate that ability is innate and fixed: talent is identified early, selected for, and rewarded; mistakes are punished; failure is attributed to the individual; and the system is designed to sort people rather than develop them. An institution has a growth mindset when its structures communicate that ability develops: learning is prioritised over performance; mistakes are treated as data; failure is attributed to the process or the support, not only the person; and the system is designed to develop people rather than select them.

The critical insight is that institutional mindset is not just the aggregate of individual beliefs — it is embedded in structures. An organisation can be full of individuals who personally hold growth mindsets but operate within systems that enforce fixed-mindset logic. Promotion criteria that reward flawless execution, performance reviews that rank and sort, hiring practices that seek "natural talent," failure narratives that blame individuals — these structural features communicate a fixed mindset regardless of what any individual believes.

For Yeager, this is why individual-level mindset interventions often fail in organisations. Teaching an employee to "embrace challenges and learn from mistakes" in an organisation that punishes mistakes is not empowering — it is contradictory. The institutional mindset overrides the individual intervention because the institution controls the consequences. The employee who "embraces a challenge" and fails in a fixed-mindset organisation learns that the growth message was not real — that the actual operating system is still fixed. This produces cynicism, not growth.

Changing institutional mindset requires structural change, not just training. It requires redesigning the incentive systems, feedback practices, promotion criteria, and failure narratives that communicate what the organisation actually believes about human development.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose the institutional mindset.** "What happens when someone fails in your organisation? The answer reveals the real mindset, regardless of what the mission statement says."
2. **Identify the structural carriers.** "Which policies, processes, and norms communicate fixed-mindset logic? Performance ranking? Up-or-out? Blame-the-individual failure narratives?"
3. **Redesign the structures.** "Change the consequences, not just the messages. If failure leads to learning conversations instead of performance sanctions, the institutional mindset shifts."
4. **Align messages with structures.** "An organisation that says 'we value learning' but punishes mistakes has a credibility gap that no training programme can close."

## Examples
**Situation:** An international development organisation has invested heavily in "learning culture" — organisational learning strategies, after-action reviews, failure reports, learning events. But staff report that the culture has not changed. People still hide problems, avoid reporting failures, and focus on delivering outputs rather than learning from experience.
**Application:** Yeager would say: "You have a growth-mindset message overlaid on a fixed-mindset structure, and the structure is winning. Look at your actual systems. How are programmes evaluated? On whether they met their targets — which incentivises hiding problems and inflating results. How are staff assessed? On their ability to deliver successfully — which incentivises taking safe assignments and avoiding risk. What happens to someone who reports a genuine programme failure? Officially, they are praised for honesty. Unofficially, they are associated with the failure, and the next promotion round does not favour them. Your after-action reviews produce documents that are filed and forgotten because the incentive structure does not reward acting on them. The learning strategy is real. The structures that govern careers, funding, and reputation are more real. Until you change those structures — until honest failure reporting genuinely does not damage careers, until programme evaluations reward learning as much as achieving targets, until taking risks is structurally supported — the learning culture will remain aspirational rather than actual. The fix is not more learning events. It is structural: change what gets rewarded, and the culture follows."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat culture change as a communication challenge.
**Why:** "We need to communicate our values better" is a common and insufficient response. The values are communicated perfectly well. They are contradicted by the structures. Fix the structures.

**Don't:** Blame individuals for institutional problems.
**Why:** People who hide failures in a fixed-mindset institution are being rational. Blaming them for rationality is unfair and unproductive.
