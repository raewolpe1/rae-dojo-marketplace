# Yeager and the Field

## When to Use
- When positioning Yeager's work relative to Dweck, Duckworth, Walton, or other motivation researchers.
- When addressing critiques of growth mindset or wise intervention research.
- When someone asks "how does this relate to X?" about adjacent bodies of work.

## Core Concept
Yeager occupies a distinctive position in the field of motivation and intervention science. He is Dweck's primary collaborator but not her clone — his contributions extend and sometimes challenge the implications of her foundational work. Understanding his position requires understanding his relationships with several adjacent thinkers.

**Relation to Carol Dweck.** Dweck established the growth mindset construct and the laboratory evidence for its effects. Yeager's contribution was translation: taking the construct from laboratory demonstrations to real-world, at-scale implementation and testing. The NSLM was the definitive test of whether growth mindset interventions could work at scale, and the answer — yes, but with crucial moderating conditions — moved the field from "does mindset matter?" to "under what conditions does mindset intervention work?" Yeager also introduced the mentor mindset framework, which operationalises how authority figures can support growth mindset in practice.

**Relation to Angela Duckworth.** Duckworth's grit construct focuses on sustained passion and perseverance. Yeager's work is complementary but distinct: where Duckworth asks "what keeps people going?" Yeager asks "what conditions enable people to engage in the first place?" The mentor mindset addresses the preconditions for the kind of engagement Duckworth studies. They share a focus on achievement motivation but differ in their emphasis on individual traits (Duckworth) versus person-environment interaction (Yeager).

**Relation to Gregory Walton.** Walton co-developed the wise intervention framework and conducted foundational belonging intervention research. Yeager extended Walton's work by testing it at national scale and emphasising heterogeneity. Their collaboration produced the wise feedback and synergistic mindsets research. Walton's work tends to emphasise the elegance of brief interventions; Yeager emphasises the conditions for their real-world effectiveness.

**Relation to Alia Crum.** Crum's research on stress mindsets directly informs Yeager's synergistic mindsets work. Crum focuses on how beliefs about stress affect physiological responses; Yeager integrates this with growth mindset to show synergistic effects. Their work is deeply complementary.

**Critiques and responses.** Growth mindset research has faced significant criticism on several fronts. The replication critique — that mindset interventions "do not replicate" — is partially addressed by Yeager's heterogeneity analysis: the average effect may not replicate, but the moderated effect (specific populations, specific conditions) does. The structural critique — that mindset interventions individualise systemic problems — has force, and Yeager partially addresses it through his emphasis on institutional mindset and contextual conditions. But the critique remains valid: a growth mindset intervention in a system that structurally excludes certain groups addresses only one dimension of the problem. The oversimplification critique — that "growth mindset" has been reduced to a slogan — is one Yeager shares; much of his work explicitly combats the pop-psychology version of mindset science.

**Evidence base.** Yeager's specific contributions — the NSLM, synergistic mindsets, mentor mindset — have strong evidence from well-designed, pre-registered, large-scale trials published in high-impact journals (Nature, PNAS). The mentor mindset framework from *10 to 25* synthesises the research into a practice-oriented framework that is well-supported by the underlying evidence but less directly tested as an integrated package. The honest assessment: the component findings are robust; the practical synthesis is well-reasoned but less empirically validated as a package.

## Examples
**Situation:** A colleague says: "Growth mindset has been debunked. The replications failed and it is just a self-help fad."
**Application:** Yeager would say: "That claim overstates the evidence in one direction just as 'growth mindset changes everything' overstates it in the other. Here is what the evidence actually shows. Some replications of small-scale growth mindset studies failed. That is true and it is important — it means the effect is not as universal as early studies suggested. But the NSLM — the largest, most rigorous test — found clear effects for the students who face the most academic challenges, in schools where the culture supports the message. Those moderated findings replicate. What 'debunked' means is: 'the simple version — teach everyone growth mindset and everything improves — is wrong.' That is correct. The simple version is wrong. But the nuanced version — targeted interventions for specific populations in supportive environments — is well-supported. The question is not whether growth mindset is real. It is. The question is whether you can design interventions that are precise enough, contextually appropriate enough, and scalably enough to make a difference in practice. That is a harder question, and the answer is: yes, under specifiable conditions. Dismissing the entire body of work because the pop-psychology version was oversimplified is like dismissing medicine because vitamin supplements do not cure cancer."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present Yeager as having superseded Dweck.
**Why:** Their relationship is collaborative. Yeager extended and tested Dweck's framework at scale; he did not replace it. Presenting it as a correction mischaracterises both the relationship and the science.

**Don't:** Dismiss critiques as misunderstanding.
**Why:** The structural critique of mindset interventions is legitimate. Engaging with it seriously — "yes, and here is what we do about it" — is more productive than defending the territory.
