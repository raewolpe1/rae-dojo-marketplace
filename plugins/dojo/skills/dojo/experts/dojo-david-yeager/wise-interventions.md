# Wise Interventions

## When to Use
- When designing interventions that need to be brief, scalable, and psychologically precise.
- When someone asks how a brief exercise can produce lasting behaviour change.
- When the distinction between information delivery and meaning change needs making.

## Core Concept
Wise interventions — a term coined by Gregory Walton and further developed in Yeager's work — are brief, targeted psychological interventions that produce lasting effects by changing the meaning of a situation rather than delivering new information or skills. The "wise" refers to their precision: they identify a specific psychological barrier (a meaning or interpretation that blocks engagement) and provide a precisely targeted experience that shifts it.

The mechanism is recursive. A wise intervention does not produce a one-time behaviour change. It shifts a meaning, which shifts a behaviour, which shifts the feedback from the environment, which reinforces the new meaning. A student who reinterprets struggle as "developing ability" (growth mindset) engages more with challenging material, which produces better outcomes, which confirms the belief that abilities develop. The intervention triggers a self-reinforcing cycle.

Three conditions are necessary. First, there must be a genuine psychological barrier — a specific meaning that is blocking engagement or performance. If the barrier is structural (inadequate resources, bad teaching, toxic management), a wise intervention will not help. Second, the intervention must be precisely targeted at that specific meaning — not a generic message but a tailored experience. Third, the context must support the new meaning. If the intervention says "struggle means growth" but the environment punishes struggle, the intervention's message is contradicted by experience and the effect disappears.

Wise interventions are not a substitute for structural change. They operate in the space between what the environment provides and how the individual interprets it. When the environment is adequate but the interpretation is limiting, wise interventions can be transformative. When the environment itself is the problem, they are insufficient.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the specific psychological barrier.** "What meaning or interpretation is blocking engagement? Be precise — 'low motivation' is not specific enough."
2. **Design for meaning change, not information.** "The intervention should create an experience that shifts interpretation, not a lecture that delivers facts."
3. **Ensure context supports the shift.** "Will the environment reinforce the new meaning? If not, fix the environment first."
4. **Build in the recursive loop.** "Design the intervention so the initial shift creates behaviours that generate confirming feedback."

## Examples
**Situation:** A development programme is struggling with attrition in its community health worker (CHW) training. CHWs complete the training but drop out within six months of deployment. Exit interviews suggest they feel unprepared and overwhelmed by real-world conditions.
**Application:** Yeager would say: "The feeling of being unprepared and overwhelmed is probably not a training quality problem — your training may be excellent. It is a meaning problem. The CHWs interpret the gap between training and reality as evidence that they are not good enough, rather than as the normal experience of anyone transitioning from structured learning to unstructured practice. This is a wise intervention opportunity. During the final week of training, design a brief exercise — forty-five minutes, not a full module — where experienced CHWs share their stories of the first six months. Not success stories. Stories of difficulty: the confusion, the mistakes, the feeling of being unprepared. Frame these stories with a simple message: 'Every CHW who is now effective went through exactly this period. The difficulty is not a sign that you are failing — it is the normal transition from learning to practice.' This reframes the meaning of difficulty from 'I am inadequate' to 'this is how it works.' Then — crucially — build a support structure that reinforces the message: monthly check-ins where CHWs can share challenges without judgement, a mentor CHW for the first three months. The intervention shifts the meaning. The support structure confirms the new meaning through experience."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat wise interventions as a substitute for fixing broken systems.
**Why:** If the environment is genuinely harmful — inadequate support, abusive management, structural barriers — a wise intervention is a band-aid on a structural wound.

**Don't:** Assume any brief exercise is a wise intervention.
**Why:** The "wise" is about precision of targeting, not brevity. A brief, poorly targeted exercise is just a waste of time.
