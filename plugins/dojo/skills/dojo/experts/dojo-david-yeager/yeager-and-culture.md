# Yeager and Culture

## When to Use
- When team or organisational culture is blocking the growth of individuals within it.
- When peer norms are more powerful than formal training or policy.
- When designing culture-level interventions rather than individual-level ones.

## Core Concept
Yeager's NSLM research revealed a finding that shifted the conversation about mindset interventions: the growth mindset intervention worked in schools where the peer culture supported challenge-seeking and failed in schools where the peer norm devalued effort. The individual intervention could not override the cultural context. Culture was the moderator.

This finding — that peer norms moderate individual-level psychological interventions — has broad implications. In any group setting, the prevailing norms about effort, struggle, failure, and help-seeking shape what individuals believe is appropriate, regardless of what any formal training tells them. A new team member who joins a culture where admitting mistakes is normal will admit mistakes. The same person joining a culture where mistakes are hidden will hide them — regardless of their personal beliefs about growth.

Culture change, in Yeager's framework, is not about messaging or values statements. It is about creating norm-setting experiences — moments where people see that the new norm is real. When a senior leader publicly discusses their own failure and what they learned, that single moment shifts norms more effectively than a year of "learning culture" presentations. When a team adopts a practice of opening meetings with "what did not work this week," the norm of honest failure reporting becomes real through repetition.

The critical design principle: norms are set by visible behaviour of respected members, not by policy documents. The culture of an organisation is whatever the most respected members actually do, regardless of what the organisation says it values.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose the actual norms.** "Not what the organisation says it values. What actually happens when someone fails, asks for help, or admits uncertainty?"
2. **Identify norm-setters.** "Who are the people whose behaviour others watch and imitate? These are not always the most senior — they are the most respected."
3. **Create norm-setting moments.** "Design experiences where respected members visibly model the desired behaviour — admitting mistakes, seeking feedback, embracing challenge."
4. **Sustain through repetition.** "One moment does not change culture. Repeated, visible, respected-member modelling of the new behaviour, reinforced by structural supports, changes culture."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consulting firm wants to become more learning-oriented — less focused on appearing expert and more willing to acknowledge uncertainty and learn from projects that did not work. They have run workshops, published values statements, and created a "learning library." Nothing has changed.
**Application:** Yeager would say: "Workshops and values statements do not change culture because culture is not set by messages — it is set by the visible behaviour of respected members. Here is what changes culture: next Monday, at the all-hands meeting, the most senior, most respected partner in the firm stands up and says: 'I want to tell you about a project I led last quarter that did not work. Here is what went wrong, here is what I missed, and here is what I learned.' Then another senior person does the same. Then you create a standing agenda item — monthly, not annual — where senior people discuss not their successes but their failures and learnings. The junior staff will be watching. What they learn is not from the words but from the act: 'The people I respect and want to be like openly discuss their failures. Therefore, failure is safe to discuss. Therefore, I can discuss mine.' That is norm-setting through visible behaviour. It works because it is credible — it involves real vulnerability from real people with real reputations. Your learning library was not credible because it was impersonal. Your values statement was not credible because it was not enacted. Culture shifts when behaviour shifts, visibly, repeatedly, by the people others are watching."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Try to change culture through training alone.
**Why:** Training changes knowledge. Culture changes through visible modelling, structural reinforcement, and repeated enactment.

**Don't:** Ignore whose interests the current culture serves.
**Why:** Fixed-mindset cultures often serve incumbent interests — they protect the status of those who have already succeeded and make it harder for newcomers to challenge them. Culture change threatens incumbents, and they may resist.
