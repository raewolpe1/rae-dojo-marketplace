# Yeager and Measurement

## When to Use
- When designing evaluations of psychological, behavioural, or social interventions.
- When evaluation results show variable or null average effects and need interpreting.
- When heterogeneity analysis, pre-registration, and moderation need integrating into evaluation practice.

## Core Concept
Yeager's approach to measurement and evaluation represents a paradigm shift from "does it work?" to "for whom, under what conditions, and why?" This is not a rhetorical reframing — it requires fundamentally different evaluation design.

The conventional evaluation asks: what is the average treatment effect? This makes sense for pharmaceutical trials where the mechanism is biological and relatively uniform. For psychological and social interventions — where the mechanism involves meaning-making, context, and individual variation — the average effect can be deeply misleading. An intervention with an average effect of zero may have strong positive effects for one subgroup and strong negative effects for another. Reporting only the average tells you nothing useful.

Yeager's methodological contributions include several practices that evaluators in development and social programming should adopt. First, pre-registration of moderation hypotheses: before collecting data, specify which subgroups and conditions you expect to moderate the treatment effect, and why. This prevents the post-hoc data-dredging that produces false findings. Second, measurement of contextual moderators: collect data not just on the intervention and the outcomes but on the conditions under which the intervention is delivered. School culture, facilitator quality, participant demographics, implementation fidelity — these are not covariates. They are the most important variables. Third, transparent reporting of heterogeneity: present the moderated findings alongside the average. If the intervention works for lower-achieving students in supportive environments, say so clearly. Do not bury the moderation in a supplementary table.

For development evaluators, this approach transforms how findings are communicated. Instead of "the programme had a modest average effect," the finding becomes "the programme had strong effects for [population] under [conditions], suggesting that scaling should target those conditions." This is more useful for decision-making and more honest about the reality of complex interventions.

## How to Apply
1. **Pre-register moderation hypotheses.** "Before data collection, specify who you expect to benefit most and what conditions should moderate the effect."
2. **Measure the context.** "Collect implementation fidelity, environmental conditions, and participant characteristics alongside outcomes."
3. **Analyse for heterogeneity.** "Run your moderation analyses. Present them as primary findings, not supplementary material."
4. **Communicate for decisions.** "Frame findings in terms of 'works for whom under what conditions' rather than 'works or does not work.'"

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluator is designing the impact evaluation for a national teacher training programme. The standard design is a cluster-randomised trial with average treatment effect as the primary outcome.
**Application:** Yeager would say: "Your standard design will give you an average effect that is almost certainly modest and almost certainly masks enormous variation across sites. Before you start, do three things. First, hypothesise the moderators. Based on what you know about the programme, which factors should make it more or less effective? My guesses: school leadership support, teacher experience level, existing pedagogical culture, class size, and implementation fidelity. Write these down, register them publicly, and commit to analysing them. Second, measure those moderators. Add short surveys for school principals (leadership support), observe or rate implementation fidelity at each site, collect teacher experience data. This is not expensive — it is a few additional instruments. Third, when you report results, lead with the moderation. Not 'the programme had an average effect of d = 0.10.' Lead with: 'The programme produced effects of d = 0.25 in schools where [conditions], and effects near zero in schools without those conditions. The conditions for impact are: [list]. We recommend scaling to contexts that meet these conditions and redesigning the programme for contexts that do not.' That finding is ten times more useful than the average, and it costs almost nothing extra to produce."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Run unplanned subgroup analyses and present them as primary findings.
**Why:** Post-hoc data-dredging inflates false positive rates. Pre-register your moderation hypotheses or clearly label analyses as exploratory.

**Don't:** Treat heterogeneity as a problem to explain away.
**Why:** Variable effects are the most useful finding for programme improvement. They tell you where to focus and what conditions to create.
