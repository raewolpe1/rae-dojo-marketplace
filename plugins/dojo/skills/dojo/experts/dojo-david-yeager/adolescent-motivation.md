# Adolescent Motivation

## When to Use
- When working with or designing programmes for young people aged roughly 10-25.
- When someone describes youth disengagement and attributes it to laziness, apathy, or defiance.
- When the developmental science of adolescent motivation needs translating into practice.

## Core Concept
Yeager's 2024 book *10 to 25* reframes adolescent motivation through a developmental lens. The conventional view treats adolescent disengagement as a problem of character (they are lazy), attitude (they do not care), or rebellion (they are defiant). Yeager's research shows it is primarily a problem of status and meaning.

During adolescence, the brain undergoes a dramatic reorganisation. The sensitivity to social status, respect, and autonomy increases sharply. Adolescents are not seeking comfort or ease — they are seeking to be taken seriously. They want to contribute, to have their competence recognised, to feel that their actions matter. When programmes, schools, or workplaces treat young people as passive recipients — as problems to be fixed, mouths to be filled, behaviours to be corrected — they trigger a status threat that produces exactly the disengagement they are trying to prevent.

The implication is practical and counterintuitive: the way to motivate young people is not to make things easier, not to offer more rewards, and not to demand more compliance. It is to offer genuine respect — through challenging and meaningful work, through authentic responsibility, through treatment as contributors rather than beneficiaries. A young person who is asked to mentor a younger peer is more engaged than one who is told to complete a worksheet. Not because mentoring is easier — it is harder — but because it signals: "You have something valuable to offer."

This applies beyond adolescence. Young professionals in their twenties respond to the same mechanisms. And the framework illuminates why so many youth development programmes fail: they are designed with a protector mindset (make it easy, do not challenge, keep them comfortable) or an enforcer mindset (demand compliance, punish failure, weed out the uncommitted). Neither addresses what young people actually need: the experience of being taken seriously while being supported to grow.

## How to Apply
1. **Reframe disengagement as information.** "When a young person is disengaged, do not ask 'what is wrong with them.' Ask 'what is their experience of this environment telling them about their status and value?'"
2. **Design for contribution.** "Create roles where young people are contributors, not recipients. Ask them to teach, lead, create, solve — not just absorb."
3. **Offer meaningful challenge with support.** "The challenge must be real — young people detect condescension instantly. The support must be specific — not generic encouragement."
4. **Respect autonomy.** "Wherever possible, offer choice. Not unlimited choice — structured choice within meaningful constraints."

## Examples
**Situation:** A youth employment programme reports high drop-out rates among 18-24-year-olds. The programme offers soft skills training, CV writing workshops, and interview preparation. Staff describe participants as "unmotivated" and "not taking the opportunity seriously."
**Application:** Yeager would say: "Look at your programme through the eyes of a twenty-year-old who is already sceptical about institutions. You are telling them: 'You lack skills. We will teach you how to write a CV, how to present yourself, how to behave in an interview.' Every element communicates: 'You are deficient and we are going to fix you.' That is a protector programme with an enforcer message — the combination most likely to trigger disengagement. The twenty-year-old does not experience it as an opportunity. They experience it as a judgement. Redesign with status in mind. Instead of teaching them to write CVs, partner them with local employers to solve real business problems. Instead of mock interviews, create project presentations where they present work they actually did to people who actually care about the results. Instead of soft skills training, give them roles where the skills develop through authentic use — peer mentoring, community projects, micro-enterprise. The content is similar. The meaning is entirely different. In your current programme, they are patients. In the redesign, they are contributors. That shift in meaning — from recipient to contributor — is worth more than any curriculum improvement."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Attribute disengagement to character.
**Why:** "Unmotivated" is a description of a mismatch, not a diagnosis of a person. The same young person who seems apathetic in your programme may be deeply engaged in something you know nothing about.

**Don't:** Conflate respect with low expectations.
**Why:** Respecting young people means treating them as capable of rising to genuine challenges, not shielding them from difficulty.
