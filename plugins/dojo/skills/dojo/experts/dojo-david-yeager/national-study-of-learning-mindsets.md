# National Study of Learning Mindsets

## When to Use
- When someone asks about the evidence for growth mindset at scale.
- When evaluation findings show variable effects and the evaluator needs a framework for interpreting heterogeneity.
- When the methodological design of rigorous psychological intervention trials needs discussing.

## Core Concept
The National Study of Learning Mindsets (NSLM) was the largest pre-registered randomised controlled trial of a psychological intervention ever conducted in education. Yeager led the study, which delivered a brief online growth mindset intervention to over 12,000 ninth-grade students across 65 U.S. schools. The results, published in *Nature* in 2019, were simultaneously modest and revolutionary — depending on how you read them.

The average effect on GPA was small. If you stopped there — as many media reports and sceptics did — the conclusion would be: "Growth mindset interventions have small effects." But the average effect was never the point. Yeager designed the study specifically to examine heterogeneity — to ask not "does it work?" but "for whom and under what conditions?"

The moderation findings were clear: the intervention had meaningful effects for lower-achieving students — the students who faced the most academic risk — and particularly in schools where the peer culture supported challenge-seeking behaviour (what the study called "school-level growth mindset norms"). In schools where the dominant peer norm devalued effort and challenge, the intervention had no effect. The seed required appropriate soil.

This finding transformed the debate. The question was no longer "does growth mindset work?" — a binary that satisfies nobody. The question became: what are the conditions under which brief psychological interventions can unlock potential that structural barriers have constrained? And the answer — the interaction between individual belief change and institutional culture — opened a research programme that continues to shape intervention science.

For evaluators and programme designers, the NSLM offers a methodological template: design your evaluation to examine heterogeneity from the start. Pre-register your moderation hypotheses. Do not report only the average. The average masks the finding. The moderation IS the finding.

## How to Apply
1. **Report heterogeneity, not just averages.** "When your evaluation shows a null or modest average effect, disaggregate. The action-relevant information is in the variation."
2. **Pre-register moderation hypotheses.** "Before the data come in, specify which subgroups and conditions you expect to moderate the effect. This prevents post-hoc data-dredging."
3. **Characterise the 'soil.'** "Measure the environmental conditions — organisational culture, peer norms, implementation fidelity — that you expect to moderate the intervention's effect."
4. **Communicate moderated findings clearly.** "To funders: 'The intervention works for [population] under [conditions]. We now know how to target resources for maximum impact.'"

## Examples
**Situation:** An education NGO has completed a rigorous evaluation of a teacher training programme. The average effect on student learning is statistically significant but small (d = 0.08). The funder considers this a poor return on investment.
**Application:** Yeager would say: "A d of 0.08 across the full sample tells you one thing: the programme does not work equally well everywhere. It does not tell you whether the programme works. You need to do what we did in the NSLM: examine the heterogeneity. Break the sample by school-level variables: implementation fidelity, school leadership support, teacher experience level, student demographics. My prediction: you will find a subset of schools — probably those with moderate resources and supportive leadership — where the effect is two to four times the average. And you will find another subset where the effect is zero or negative — probably schools where the training contradicted the existing culture or where implementation was poor. Present this to the funder differently: 'In schools with conditions X, the programme produced effects of d = 0.20-0.30, which is a strong return. In schools without those conditions, the programme did not work. We now know the conditions for impact, and we can target the next phase accordingly.' That is a much more useful finding than d = 0.08, and it is a much more honest one."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use the average to dismiss a heterogeneous finding.
**Why:** An average of zero can contain subgroups with large positive and large negative effects. The average cancels meaningful variation.

**Don't:** Present heterogeneity as a weakness.
**Why:** Variable effects are not a flaw in the intervention — they are information about implementation. Embrace them as the most practically useful findings.
