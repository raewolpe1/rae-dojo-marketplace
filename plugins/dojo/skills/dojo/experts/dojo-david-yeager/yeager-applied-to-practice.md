# Yeager Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When someone needs to apply Yeager's research across multiple professional situations.
- When building a comprehensive approach to motivation, development, or culture change.
- When translating the academic research into actionable professional practice.

## Core Concept
Yeager's body of work synthesises into six practical principles that translate across professional contexts — management, programme design, consulting, evaluation, and youth development.

The first principle is the mentor mindset as default stance. In every interaction involving authority and development — managing staff, teaching students, designing programmes for participants, coaching clients — adopt the stance that combines high standards with high support. Diagnose when you default to enforcer (demanding without supporting) or protector (supporting without demanding), and correct.

The second principle is context before intervention. Before recommending or designing any psychological or behavioural intervention, audit the environment. Does it support the change you are trying to create? If not, fix the environment first. An intervention in a hostile context is worse than no intervention — it teaches people that growth messages are hollow.

The third principle is heterogeneity as the primary question. When evaluating any programme or policy, do not ask "does it work?" Ask "for whom, under what conditions, and why?" Design your measurement to answer the moderated question from the start.

The fourth principle is status and respect as design parameters. Every programme, every management practice, every feedback system communicates something about the status and capacity of the people it serves. Audit these signals. Design for contribution, not remediation. Treat participants as capable people in challenging circumstances, not as deficient people who need fixing.

The fifth principle is meaning over information. The mechanism of lasting change is not knowledge transfer — it is meaning change. When you design a training, a curriculum, or a communication, ask not "what information am I conveying?" but "what meaning am I shaping about difficulty, ability, belonging, and stress?"

The sixth principle is scalability as a design constraint. If your approach only works in a pilot with perfect conditions, it does not really work. Design for real-world variation from the start. Assume imperfect implementation, diverse populations, and variable environments. The approaches that survive this assumption are the ones worth scaling.

## How to Apply
1. **Start with diagnosis.** "Before prescribing, diagnose. What is the specific barrier? Is it a mindset barrier, a context barrier, a status barrier, or a belonging barrier?"
2. **Apply the mentor mindset.** "In your own practice — as a manager, consultant, or programme designer — adopt the high-standards-plus-high-support stance consistently."
3. **Design for the context.** "Every recommendation should include the environmental conditions that must be in place for it to work."
4. **Measure heterogeneity.** "Build moderation analysis into every evaluation. Present findings as 'works for whom under what conditions.'"

## Examples
**Situation:** A development consultancy is redesigning its approach to staff development. Junior consultants feel overwhelmed by client demands and undersupported. Senior consultants feel frustrated by junior performance and overloaded with correction. The firm has tried various training programmes with limited effect.
**Application:** Yeager would say: "You have a system-level problem that training cannot solve. Let me diagnose using the frameworks. First, mentor mindset: your senior consultants are oscillating between enforcer ('this is not good enough, do it again') and protector ('I will just do it myself'). Neither develops capacity. They need training not in technical skills but in the mentor stance — how to set high standards and provide specific support simultaneously. Second, institutional mindset: does your firm's culture treat mistakes as learning or as failures? If junior consultants hide their struggles because admitting difficulty is career-threatening, you have a fixed-mindset institution. Fix the structure: create structured check-ins where early-stage work is reviewed as a normal part of the process, not as evidence of inadequacy. Third, status and respect: how do juniors experience their role? If they feel like production units whose value is measured entirely by billable output, they are experiencing a status threat that produces exactly the defensive, hide-problems behaviour you are seeing. Create roles where juniors contribute to intellectual leadership — method development, client insight sessions, internal research — so they experience themselves as developing professionals, not labour. Fourth, wise feedback: build the wise feedback frame into your formal review processes. Every piece of critical feedback should explicitly signal: 'This is because I have high standards AND I believe you can meet them.' Not as a script. As a genuine organisational stance."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Apply the frameworks mechanically.
**Why:** Each context is different. The frameworks are diagnostic tools, not recipes. Diagnosis must precede prescription.

**Don't:** Promise guaranteed results.
**Why:** Yeager's research shows strong effects under specific conditions. If the conditions are not met, the effects will not appear. Be honest about conditionality.
