# Mentor Mindset

## When to Use
- When someone is struggling with how demanding or supportive to be — with staff, students, or programme participants.
- When the enforcer/protector/mentor framework needs explaining or applying.
- When designing management approaches, feedback systems, or programme delivery models.

## Core Concept
The mentor mindset is Yeager's central practical framework, developed most fully in his 2024 book *10 to 25*. It identifies three stances that people in positions of authority — teachers, managers, parents, programme designers — adopt when working with others, and argues that only one produces sustained motivation and growth.

The **enforcer mindset** prioritises high standards without sufficient support. "Sink or swim." "If they cannot handle it, they are not ready." The enforcer gets short-term compliance and weeds out those who struggle. The cost: those who are weeded out are disproportionately those who had the least structural advantage, and those who survive learn to hide difficulty rather than seek help.

The **protector mindset** prioritises support without sufficient standards. "They are not ready for that level of challenge." "Let us make it easier so they do not get discouraged." The protector creates a comfortable environment but communicates low expectations. The cost: people who are protected from challenge do not develop the capacity to meet challenge. They feel safe but stagnant.

The **mentor mindset** holds both simultaneously. "I have high expectations for you because I believe you can meet them, and I am going to support you specifically in getting there." This is not a middle ground between enforcer and protector — it is a qualitatively different stance. It requires the mentor to genuinely believe in the person's capacity (not just say so), to set standards that are genuinely high (not lowered to avoid discomfort), and to provide support that is genuinely specific (not generic encouragement).

The mentor mindset is not a personality trait. It is a learnable stance. And it is possible to shift between stances unconsciously — many people are mentors on their good days and enforcers or protectors under pressure. The practice is making the stance conscious and deliberate.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose the current stance.** "Are you currently operating as enforcer, protector, or mentor? The clue is what you do when someone struggles."
2. **Identify what is missing.** "If you are enforcer: what support are you not providing? If you are protector: what standards are you not holding?"
3. **Design for both simultaneously.** "The structure — feedback processes, onboarding, performance management — should embed both high standards and specific support."
4. **Check under pressure.** "Everyone defaults to a stance under stress. What is your default? Knowing it lets you correct before it does damage."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consulting firm partner is frustrated with junior consultants. He says: "I give them challenging assignments and they come back with mediocre work. Then I have to redo it. They are just not performing at the level we need." He oscillates between giving harsher feedback (enforcer) and doing the work himself (a form of protector — protecting them from the consequences of their inadequacy by substituting his own competence).
**Application:** Yeager would say: "You are oscillating between enforcer and protector, and both are failing. When you give challenging assignments without sufficient support — without checking their understanding of the task, without intermediate check-ins, without modelling what excellent looks like — you are in enforcer mode. You have set the bar and walked away. Some will clear it. Many will not, and you will conclude they are not good enough. When you redo their work, you are in protector mode — you are shielding the client from their performance gap, but you are also shielding them from the learning that would come from working through the gap with support. The mentor mindset version looks like this: assign the challenging work. Then structure the support. 'Here is an example of what excellent looks like for this type of deliverable. Here is where I think the hard parts will be. Let us check in at the midpoint so I can see where you are and give you specific guidance. If you get stuck, here is how to think about it.' That takes more time upfront. It saves time downstream because the junior consultants develop capacity rather than developing dependence or learned helplessness."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present mentor mindset as being warm or encouraging.
**Why:** Mentor mindset includes genuinely high standards. If the person hears "be nicer," they will become a protector. The message is: be demanding AND supportive, simultaneously.

**Don't:** Ignore the structural dimension.
**Why:** An individual can adopt a mentor mindset, but if the organisation rewards enforcer behaviour (up-or-out cultures, public ranking) or protector behaviour (avoiding difficult conversations, grade inflation), the individual's stance will be undermined.
