# The Cognitive Model

## When to Use
- When someone needs to understand the theoretical foundation of cognitive therapy.
- When the relationship between thoughts, feelings, and behaviour needs to be explained.
- When someone is confused about what CT actually proposes.

## Core Concept
The cognitive model holds that people's emotions and behaviours are influenced by their perception of events. It is not a situation itself that directly determines what people feel but rather the way they interpret and make meaning of that situation. Two people in the same situation may respond entirely differently because they construct different meanings from the same event.

Beck developed this model after years of psychoanalytic practice. Studying depressed patients, he noticed that their emotional states were consistently associated with identifiable patterns of biased thinking — not with unconscious conflicts as psychoanalytic theory predicted. These biased thoughts were accessible to the patient's awareness, could be examined collaboratively, and could be modified through systematic questioning and evidence-gathering.

The model proposes three levels of cognition. At the surface level are automatic thoughts — rapid, evaluative, often image-based cognitions that stream through consciousness. Below these are intermediate beliefs — rules, assumptions, and attitudes that organise thinking ("If I make a mistake, people will reject me"). At the deepest level are core beliefs or schemas — fundamental, usually unarticulated convictions about oneself, others, and the world ("I am incompetent," "People are untrustworthy," "The world is dangerous").

Core beliefs develop through early experience and act as filters — biasing attention, memory, and interpretation in ways that confirm the belief. A person with the core belief "I am incompetent" will notice failures, dismiss successes, interpret ambiguous feedback as criticism, and remember past mistakes more readily than past achievements. The cognitive system is self-confirming unless deliberately examined.

Cognitive therapy works by teaching patients to identify their automatic thoughts, examine the evidence for and against them, recognise the cognitive distortions at work, and develop more balanced, evidence-based interpretations. Over time, this process also modifies the deeper beliefs and schemas that generate the surface-level thoughts.

## How to Apply
1. **Explain the model.** Situations → Thoughts → Emotions/Behaviour. The thought mediates between the event and the response.
2. **Demonstrate with a concrete example.** Use the patient's own experience to show how different interpretations of the same event produce different emotional responses.
3. **Introduce the three levels.** Automatic thoughts (surface), intermediate beliefs (middle), core beliefs (deep).
4. **Begin with automatic thoughts.** They are the most accessible and the entry point for intervention.

## Examples
**Situation:** A patient says, "My colleague didn't say hello this morning and I felt terrible all day."
**Application:** Beck would explore the cognitive mediation: "When your colleague didn't say hello, what went through your mind?" The patient might say: "She's ignoring me. She must be angry about something I did." These are automatic thoughts — interpretations of the event, not descriptions of it. The event is neutral (colleague didn't say hello). The interpretation (she's angry at me, I did something wrong) is what produced the emotional response. Were there alternative interpretations? She was distracted. She didn't see you. She was having a bad morning. Any of these interpretations would have produced a different emotional response. The question is not which interpretation is "positive" but which is most supported by evidence.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present the cognitive model as "mind over matter."
**Why:** The model does not claim that thinking can override reality. It claims that biased thinking adds unnecessary suffering to real difficulties. Bad events are genuinely bad. Biased thinking makes them worse.

**Don't:** Ignore the role of behaviour and environment.
**Why:** The cognitive model is interactional — cognition, behaviour, and environment all influence each other. CT addresses cognition primarily but includes behavioural interventions and environmental considerations.
