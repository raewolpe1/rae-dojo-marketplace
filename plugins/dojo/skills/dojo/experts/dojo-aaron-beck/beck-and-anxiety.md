# Beck and Anxiety

## When to Use
- When anxiety is the presenting problem.
- When the cognitive model of anxiety needs to be explained or applied.
- When safety behaviours are maintaining the anxiety cycle.

## Core Concept
Beck's cognitive model of anxiety centres on threat appraisal. While depression involves a cognitive bias toward loss and failure, anxiety involves a cognitive bias toward danger and vulnerability. The anxious person systematically overestimates the probability and severity of threat while underestimating their capacity to cope. This dual miscalculation — danger is everywhere and I cannot handle it — produces the subjective experience of anxiety and drives the behavioural responses of avoidance, escape, and safety-seeking.

The model distinguishes between the content of anxious cognition and its process. The content varies by disorder: social anxiety involves threats to social standing ("They will judge me"), generalised anxiety involves diffuse threats across multiple domains ("Something bad will happen"), panic disorder involves catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations ("This heart pounding means I am having a heart attack"), and obsessive-compulsive disorder involves inflated responsibility for preventing harm ("If I don't check, the house will burn down"). The process is consistent across all anxiety presentations: overestimation of threat plus underestimation of coping resources.

Safety behaviours are a central concept in the cognitive model of anxiety. These are actions the person takes to prevent the feared catastrophe: the socially anxious person rehearses every word before speaking, the panic patient sits near the exit, the health-anxious person checks their body repeatedly. The problem is that safety behaviours prevent disconfirmation. If the person rehearses and the presentation goes well, they attribute the success to the rehearsal, not to their competence. The belief "I cannot manage without preparation" remains intact. Dropping the safety behaviour is therefore essential — it allows the person to discover that the feared outcome does not occur even without the protective ritual.

Beck and his colleagues — particularly David Clark and Adrian Wells — developed disorder-specific cognitive models for panic, social anxiety, GAD, OCD, health anxiety, and PTSD, each identifying the specific maintenance cycles that keep the anxiety going. These models are among the most well-validated in clinical psychology and form the basis of the NICE-recommended treatments for anxiety disorders in the UK.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the threat appraisal.** What is the feared outcome? How probable does the person believe it is? How catastrophic?
2. **Identify the coping appraisal.** How does the person rate their ability to cope if the feared outcome occurred?
3. **Identify safety behaviours.** What is the person doing to prevent the feared outcome?
4. **Examine the evidence.** Is the probability estimate accurate? Has the catastrophe actually occurred before?
5. **Drop safety behaviours and test.** Design behavioral experiments where safety behaviours are reduced or eliminated.
6. **Reappraise coping capacity.** Help the person recognise their ability to manage difficult situations.

## Examples
**Situation:** A project manager avoids speaking in team meetings because he is certain he will "go blank and everyone will see I'm incompetent." He prepares obsessively detailed notes and reads from them word for word.
**Application:** The threat appraisal is clear: "I will go blank" (probability overestimation) and "Everyone will see I'm incompetent and I will be finished" (catastrophe overestimation). The coping appraisal is low: "I cannot recover if I lose my place." The safety behaviour is the detailed notes — they prevent him from discovering whether he can speak without them. Beck would first examine the evidence: "How many times have you actually gone blank? ... Twice in ten years? And what happened? ... You paused, found your place, and continued? So you did cope." Then design a behavioral experiment: next meeting, bring only three bullet points instead of a full script. Prediction: "I will go blank and lose credibility." Observe: what actually happens? Most patients discover they can speak more naturally with fewer notes, and colleagues respond more positively to a conversational style than to a person reading a script. The safety behaviour was maintaining the problem it was designed to solve.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reassure the person that their fear will not happen.
**Why:** Reassurance is a form of safety behaviour. It provides short-term relief but prevents the person from developing their own evidence-based confidence. Help them test the belief, not rely on your reassurance.

**Don't:** Assume all anxiety is distorted.
**Why:** Sometimes the threat is real. A person who is anxious about a genuinely unstable job situation may be appraising accurately. The therapist's job is to help examine the evidence, not to assume the appraisal is always wrong.
