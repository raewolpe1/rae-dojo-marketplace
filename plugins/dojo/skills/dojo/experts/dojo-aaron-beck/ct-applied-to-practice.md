# CT Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When the practical structure of cognitive therapy sessions needs to be explained.
- When homework and between-session practice need to be understood.
- When the principle of making the patient their own therapist needs to be emphasised.

## Core Concept
Cognitive therapy is structured, time-limited, and skill-building. Each session follows a predictable format, and the overall therapy follows a predictable arc from symptom relief to relapse prevention. The structure is not bureaucratic — it serves a therapeutic purpose. Structure ensures that sessions are productive, that important topics are not avoided, and that the patient learns a process they can replicate independently.

The standard CT session structure has several components. It begins with a mood check — a brief standardised measure like the Beck Depression Inventory or Beck Anxiety Inventory to track progress objectively. Next is the bridge from the previous session: "What has happened since last time? How did the homework go? Were there any important reactions to last session?" Then collaborative agenda-setting: "What would be most useful to work on today?" The therapist and patient agree on one or two priorities. The main body of the session works on those priorities using cognitive and behavioural techniques. Before closing, the therapist assigns homework — a continuation of the session's work into the real world. The session ends with a summary and feedback: "What did you take away from today? Was there anything I said that bothered you?"

Homework is not optional in CT. It is arguably the most important component. Therapy sessions provide approximately one hour per week. The remaining 167 hours are where the patient actually lives. If the skills practiced in session are not applied between sessions, little changes. Homework typically involves thought records, behavioral experiments, activity scheduling, or reading. The therapist reviews homework at the start of the next session — if homework is assigned but never reviewed, the patient learns that it does not matter.

The overarching goal of CT is to make the patient their own therapist. Every technique is taught with this end in mind. The therapist does not just restructure thoughts — they teach the patient how to restructure thoughts. They do not just design behavioral experiments — they teach the patient how to design them. By the end of therapy, the patient should be able to identify an automatic thought, examine the evidence, generate a balanced alternative, design a test, and draw conclusions — all without a therapist present. This is why CT has lower relapse rates than medication for depression: the skills persist after treatment ends, whereas the medication effect does not.

## How to Apply
1. **Structure every session.** Mood check → bridge → agenda → work → homework → summary → feedback.
2. **Assign homework every session.** Make it specific, doable, and directly connected to the session's work.
3. **Review homework every session.** Failure to review signals that homework is unimportant.
4. **Teach the process, not just the content.** The patient should learn how to catch thoughts, examine evidence, and test beliefs.
5. **Plan for termination from the start.** Taper session frequency as the patient develops competence. Relapse prevention is built in, not added on.

## Examples
**Situation:** A patient is in session eight of twelve planned sessions for moderate depression. She has learned to use thought records and has shown improvement on the BDI.
**Application:** The session follows structure. Mood check: BDI down from 28 to 14. Bridge: "The thought record on Wednesday was hard — I caught the thought 'Nobody cares about me' after a friend cancelled dinner, but I couldn't find evidence against it." Agenda: work on this thought; begin discussing what happens after therapy ends. Main work: examine the evidence for "Nobody cares about me." Evidence for: friend cancelled. Evidence against: friend rescheduled for the following week, another friend called to check in, husband asked about her day. Balanced thought: "My friend cancelled one dinner. Multiple people in my life show care regularly. One cancellation does not mean nobody cares." Homework: this week, keep a log of evidence of care from others — not to force positivity but to counter the mental filter that attends only to perceived rejection. Summary: "We're noticing a pattern — when one person does something that feels like rejection, you generalise to 'nobody cares.' The mental filter screens out the evidence that contradicts it." Feedback: "That was helpful. I hadn't noticed the mental filter pattern across different situations before." The session has moved the work forward, reinforced a skill, and begun preparing for independence.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Skip the structure when a session feels emotional.
**Why:** Structure is especially important when emotions are high. It prevents the session from becoming an unstructured venting session that feels cathartic but does not produce change. The structure can be applied flexibly, but it should not be abandoned.

**Don't:** Do all the cognitive work for the patient.
**Why:** If the therapist identifies the thought, labels the distortion, generates the balanced alternative, and designs the experiment, the patient has learned nothing except that the therapist is clever. The goal is to teach, not to perform.
