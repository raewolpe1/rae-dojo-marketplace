# Automatic Thoughts

## When to Use
- When someone needs to learn to identify their automatic thoughts.
- When a person treats their thoughts as facts rather than interpretations.
- When the concept of automatic thoughts needs to be distinguished from deeper beliefs.

## Core Concept
Automatic thoughts are the rapid, evaluative cognitions that flow through consciousness in response to situations. They arrive without deliberate effort — hence "automatic." They are often brief, sometimes verbal ("I'm going to fail"), sometimes imagistic (a mental picture of being humiliated). They feel completely true in the moment, which is what gives them their emotional power. Most people do not notice them as thoughts — they experience them as reality.

Beck discovered that patients could learn to catch these thoughts if they knew what to listen for. The key signal is a shift in affect. When your mood changes — when you suddenly feel anxious, sad, angry, or ashamed — there was almost certainly an automatic thought immediately before the shift. The thought may have been so rapid that you did not register it consciously, but with practice, you can learn to identify it.

Automatic thoughts have several characteristic features. They are rapid and usually brief. They feel plausible and are accepted as true without examination. They are associated with specific emotions. They are often biased in predictable ways (see cognitive distortions). And they are modifiable — once identified, they can be examined, tested, and restructured.

The skill of catching automatic thoughts is the foundational skill of cognitive therapy. Without it, nothing else works. The thought record — a structured tool for identifying the situation, the automatic thought, the emotion, the evidence for and against the thought, and a balanced alternative — is the primary homework tool for building this skill.

## How to Apply
1. **Teach the concept.** Automatic thoughts are rapid interpretations, not facts. Everyone has them. In emotional disorders, they are systematically biased.
2. **Use affect as a signal.** "When did your mood change? What went through your mind at that moment?"
3. **Capture them in writing.** The thought record is the essential tool. Situation → Automatic thought → Emotion → Evidence for/against → Balanced thought.
4. **Normalise the experience.** Everyone has automatic thoughts. The goal is not to eliminate them but to examine them.

## Examples
**Situation:** A therapist asks a patient when they started feeling anxious during the week. The patient says, "Tuesday afternoon, at my desk, out of nowhere."
**Application:** Beck would say, "Anxiety rarely comes from nowhere. Something happened — perhaps something subtle — and your mind generated a thought about it. Can you take yourself back to that moment? What were you doing? ... You were reading an email from your manager requesting a meeting. What went through your mind when you read that?" The patient: "I thought, 'She's going to tell me my work isn't good enough.'" That is the automatic thought. It arrived so quickly that the patient experienced the anxiety as coming "out of nowhere." But it did not come from nowhere — it came from an interpretation of an ambiguous event (a meeting request) through a biased lens (my work isn't good enough). Now the thought can be examined: What is the evidence? Has the manager given feedback before? What else might the meeting be about?

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss automatic thoughts as trivial.
**Why:** They are the entry point for all cognitive work. Missing them means missing the mechanism that maintains the emotional disorder.

**Don't:** Assume the first thought reported is the key thought.
**Why:** Ask "And what did that mean to you?" to find deeper layers. The surface thought often points to a more important underlying interpretation.
