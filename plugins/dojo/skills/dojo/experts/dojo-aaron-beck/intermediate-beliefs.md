# Intermediate Beliefs

## When to Use
- When someone operates according to rigid if-then rules that restrict their life.
- When the connection between core beliefs and automatic thoughts needs the middle layer.
- When compensatory strategies are maintaining the problem despite surface-level cognitive work.

## Core Concept
Intermediate beliefs sit between core beliefs and automatic thoughts in the cognitive hierarchy. They take the form of rules ("I must always be prepared"), assumptions ("If I make a mistake, people will reject me"), and attitudes ("Mistakes are unacceptable"). They are the operating system that translates core beliefs into everyday cognition and behaviour.

A person with the core belief "I am incompetent" might develop the intermediate belief "If I prepare exhaustively, I can avoid failure" (compensatory rule) and "If I fail at anything, it proves I'm incompetent" (conditional assumption). These beliefs drive behaviour — in this case, overpreparation and avoidance of risk — that temporarily manages the core belief's anxiety but ultimately reinforces it. The overpreparation prevents the person from discovering that they can cope with imperfect preparation. The avoidance prevents them from learning that failure is survivable.

Intermediate beliefs are more stable than automatic thoughts but more accessible than core beliefs. They often emerge when the therapist asks: "If that automatic thought were true, what rule are you operating by?" or "What would it mean about you if that happened?" The answer often takes the form of a conditional: "If X, then Y."

Modifying intermediate beliefs involves identifying the rule, examining its origins and function, testing it through behavioral experiments, and developing a more flexible alternative. The rigid rule "I must never show weakness" might be replaced with "It is sometimes useful to show vulnerability, and doing so does not make me weak."

## How to Apply
1. **Listen for rules and conditions.** If-then statements, must/should language applied to behaviour, attitudes about what is and is not acceptable.
2. **Map the connection.** How does this rule connect to the core belief below it and the automatic thoughts above it?
3. **Examine the function.** What does this rule protect the person from? What does it cost them?
4. **Test with a behavioral experiment.** What happens if the rule is violated? Is the predicted consequence accurate?
5. **Develop a more flexible alternative.** Not the abolition of the rule but its relaxation.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man never delegates at work, works seventy-hour weeks, and is close to burnout. He says, "If I don't do it myself, it won't be done right."
**Application:** The intermediate belief is: "If I don't control everything, things will go wrong, and that will prove I'm incompetent" (connecting to a core belief of incompetence, paradoxically). The compensatory strategy — doing everything himself — temporarily manages the anxiety but produces burnout and prevents him from discovering that colleagues can do competent work. Beck would explore the belief Socratically: "What evidence do you have that others can't do the work? Have you tested it? What would happen if you delegated one small task this week and observed the outcome?" The behavioral experiment — delegating and observing — provides evidence that the rigid rule can be relaxed without the feared consequence materialising.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Try to eliminate all rules.
**Why:** Some rules are adaptive. "I should prepare for important presentations" is reasonable. The problem is rigidity and absolutism, not the existence of rules.

**Don't:** Skip intermediate beliefs.
**Why:** Working only on automatic thoughts without addressing the rules that generate them is like treating symptoms without addressing the condition. The thoughts will recur until the rules change.
