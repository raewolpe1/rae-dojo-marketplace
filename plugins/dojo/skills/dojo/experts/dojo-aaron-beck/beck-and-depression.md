# Beck and Depression

## When to Use
- When depression is the presenting problem.
- When the cognitive model of depression needs to be explained or applied.
- When the person needs to understand how their thinking maintains their depression.

## Core Concept
Beck's cognitive model of depression was his foundational contribution to psychology. Trained as a psychoanalyst, Beck set out in the early 1960s to validate Freud's theory that depression was anger turned inward. Instead, he found something different: depressed patients' thinking was dominated not by anger but by systematic negative biases — about themselves, their experiences, and their future. This observation became the cognitive triad, and the therapeutic method Beck developed to address it became cognitive therapy.

The cognitive model of depression proposes that early experiences lead to the formation of negative schemas — organised knowledge structures containing core beliefs about the self as defective, the world as hostile, and the future as hopeless. These schemas remain dormant until activated by a relevant life event, typically one involving loss, failure, or rejection. Once activated, the schemas bias all subsequent information processing: the person attends selectively to negative information, interprets ambiguous events negatively, recalls negative memories more easily, and has difficulty generating positive expectations. This biased processing produces the automatic thoughts that drive the emotional, behavioural, motivational, and physiological symptoms of depression.

The behavioural component of depression is critical and often needs to be addressed first. Depression produces withdrawal, inactivity, and loss of pleasure, which in turn reduce the sources of positive reinforcement in the person's life, which deepens the depression. Beck's behavioural activation strategies — activity scheduling, graded task assignment, and mastery-and-pleasure ratings — break this cycle by reintroducing activity and providing evidence that contradicts the depressive belief that nothing is enjoyable or achievable.

Cognitive therapy for depression was the first psychotherapy to be tested in a randomised controlled trial against medication (Rush et al., 1977). The results showed CT was at least as effective as imipramine for moderate depression, with lower relapse rates. This study changed the field. It demonstrated that a structured, time-limited psychotherapy could match pharmacotherapy for a condition that psychiatry had considered primarily biological.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess severity.** Severe depression may require medication as an adjunct. CT alone is appropriate for mild to moderate depression.
2. **Start with behavioural activation.** When someone is barely functioning, cognitive work is premature. Get them moving first.
3. **Introduce the cognitive model.** Help the person see the connection between their thoughts and their mood.
4. **Use the thought record.** Identify, examine, and restructure the automatic thoughts that maintain the depression.
5. **Address the cognitive triad.** Map the negative views of self, world, and future and examine each.
6. **Build toward relapse prevention.** Teach the person to be their own therapist.

## Examples
**Situation:** A woman in her forties presents with depression following her children leaving home. She says she does almost nothing during the day, feels no pleasure, and believes her life has no purpose anymore.
**Application:** Beck would first address the behavioural withdrawal. Activity scheduling: plan specific activities for each day, starting small. Rate each for mastery (sense of accomplishment) and pleasure (enjoyment), even if the ratings are low. The purpose is to counter the belief "Nothing gives me pleasure" with data. Often patients discover that some activities do produce mild pleasure or satisfaction — they had predicted zero and found a three. Once some behavioural activation is underway, move to the cognitive work. The automatic thought "My life has no purpose" reflects the cognitive triad — negative view of self (I am empty without children), world (there is nothing worthwhile), future (it will always be this way). Examine each: "Your purpose was defined by parenting. That chapter has changed. Does that mean you have no capacity for purpose, or that you haven't yet found what comes next? Those are very different conclusions."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Jump to cognitive restructuring with severely depressed patients.
**Why:** When someone cannot get out of bed, asking them to complete thought records is unrealistic and may produce failure experiences that worsen the depression. Start with behavioural activation.

**Don't:** Treat depression as purely cognitive.
**Why:** Depression is a syndrome with cognitive, emotional, behavioural, motivational, and physiological dimensions. The cognitive model explains the maintenance cycle but does not claim that depression is "just thinking." Biological vulnerability, life circumstances, and interpersonal factors all play roles.
