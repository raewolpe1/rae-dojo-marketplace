# Cognitive Distortions

## When to Use
- When someone's thinking exhibits systematic bias that can be named and examined.
- When the taxonomy of cognitive distortions needs to be taught.
- When identifying the specific distortion helps the person see their thinking pattern.

## Core Concept
Beck and his colleagues identified a set of systematic thinking errors — cognitive distortions — that are characteristic of emotional disorders. These are not random errors but predictable patterns of bias that distort interpretation in consistent ways. Naming the distortion helps the patient recognise the pattern, which is the first step toward correcting it.

The major cognitive distortions include: all-or-nothing thinking (seeing things in absolute categories — complete success or total failure, with nothing in between), overgeneralisation (drawing broad conclusions from a single event — "I failed this exam, so I'll fail at everything"), mental filter (focusing exclusively on negative details while ignoring positives), disqualifying the positive (dismissing positive experiences as flukes or exceptions), jumping to conclusions (mind-reading — assuming others' thoughts without evidence; fortune-telling — predicting the future negatively without evidence), magnification and minimisation (exaggerating negatives and shrinking positives), emotional reasoning (assuming that feelings reflect reality — "I feel incompetent, therefore I am incompetent"), should statements (rigid rules about how things ought to be), labelling (attaching a fixed label to oneself or others based on specific behaviours), and personalisation (assuming responsibility for external events without evidence).

These distortions are not the same as irrational beliefs in Ellis's model, though they overlap. Ellis focused on the demand structure underlying disturbance. Beck focused on the processing errors — the systematic ways in which the cognitive system mishandles information. Both approaches are valid and complementary.

The distortions are tools for self-awareness, not accusations. Telling someone "You're catastrophising" as a dismissal is unhelpful. Helping them see for themselves that their prediction involves magnification and fortune-telling — and that these patterns have been wrong before — is therapeutic.

## How to Apply
1. **Teach the distortions as a vocabulary.** Give the patient a list. Ask them to identify which ones they recognise in their own thinking.
2. **Identify the distortion in specific thoughts.** Not abstractly but in the automatic thought they just reported.
3. **Use the distortion label as a signal, not a verdict.** "It sounds like there might be some mind-reading here — can we check the evidence?"
4. **Show the pattern over time.** As therapy progresses, patients begin to see that the same distortions recur across different situations.

## Examples
**Situation:** A student receives a B+ on a paper and says, "I'm a terrible student."
**Application:** Multiple distortions are operating. All-or-nothing thinking: anything less than an A is failure. Overgeneralisation: one grade defines her entire academic identity. Labelling: attaching "terrible student" as a fixed label based on one outcome. Disqualifying the positive: the B+ is objectively a strong grade but is treated as evidence of failure. Beck would help her see each distortion: "You received a B+. In what system of evaluation is that 'terrible'? What grade would you need to not be terrible? ... An A? So anything below perfection equals terrible? That sounds like all-or-nothing thinking — there's no middle ground between perfect and terrible. And you've taken this one grade and turned it into 'I'm a terrible student' — that's overgeneralisation and labelling. What would you say to your best friend if she got a B+ and called herself terrible?"

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use distortion labels as weapons.
**Why:** "You're just catastrophising" said dismissively invalidates the person's experience. The label is a tool for collaborative exploration, not a conversation-stopper.

**Don't:** Assume the person's thinking is always distorted.
**Why:** Sometimes the negative interpretation is accurate. The job is to examine the evidence, not to assume every negative thought is wrong. CT is about accuracy, not optimism.
