# Micro-Empirical Policy

## When to Use
- When building a policy stance on a question that has been studied empirically.
- When converting an ideological debate into specific, researchable questions.
- When designing a programme that should be informed by the accumulated evidence base.

## Fails When
- **The question is structural rather than intervention-specific.** Industrial strategy is not a micro-empirical question.
- **The evidence base is thin.** Pretending to evidence-based policy when the evidence does not exist is worse than admitting uncertainty.

## Core Concept

Micro-empirical policy is the stance that most policy debates can be substantially advanced by better evidence about specific interventions, even where the structural questions remain contested. The orientation is pragmatic: take the question that is being asked, identify the empirical sub-questions whose answers would change a reasonable person's view, and gather the evidence.

The approach has several characteristics. It is **question-driven, not method-driven** — it uses RCTs where appropriate, quasi-experimental designs where RCTs are infeasible, and qualitative methods to understand mechanism. It is **modular, not monolithic** — large policy questions decompose into smaller researchable questions whose answers accumulate into useful policy understanding. It is **cumulative** — each study adds to the base of what is known; meta-analyses and replications matter as much as new studies.

The contribution micro-empirical policy makes is not to settle every debate (it cannot) but to narrow the range of defensible positions. Before the RCT evidence on cash transfers, the debate about cash vs in-kind was conducted with strong claims on both sides and weak evidence on either. After the evidence, the defensible positions narrowed substantially — cash usually outperforms in-kind for most outcomes; in-kind matters for specific cases (e.g. school meals as nutritional supplement). Reasonable people can still disagree about emphasis; they can no longer disagree about whether cash transfers produce harm (they do not) or whether they produce broad poverty reduction (they reduce poverty depth, not eliminate poverty).

The pushback that micro-empirical policy is "small bore" misunderstands the scale. An intervention that reaches a million households and improves outcomes by 10 per cent affects a hundred thousand lives. Across the J-PAL portfolio of evidence, the cumulative human impact runs into the hundreds of millions. The contemptuous framing of "just RCTs" misses what is at stake.

## How to Apply

1. **Take the policy question seriously, including its structural dimensions.** Then identify the sub-questions where evidence can be brought to bear.
2. **Survey the existing evidence base** before designing new studies. Often the question has been studied.
3. **Specify which evidence would change your mind.** A policy claim that no evidence could disconfirm is ideology, not policy.
4. **Combine micro evidence with structural analysis where the structural questions are unavoidable.** The two are complements.

## Examples

**Situation:** A government is designing a school feeding programme. The political debate is heated; the empirical questions are less so.

**Application:** Identify the sub-questions: what is the effect of school meals on attendance, learning, nutrition, household food security? What design features matter — meal composition, timing, cost-sharing, targeting? What does the existing global evidence say? Where the evidence is strong, use it; where it is thin, pilot to learn.

**Result:** A programme designed on what is known about what works, with iterative learning built in.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat micro-empirical work as the only legitimate input to policy.
**Why:** Structural questions require structural analysis. Methodological pluralism is the honest position.

**Don't:** Use existing evidence to deflect from new questions.
**Why:** Many policy contexts are not covered by existing evidence. Saying "we have evidence elsewhere" is sometimes sufficient and sometimes evasive. Be honest about the gap.
