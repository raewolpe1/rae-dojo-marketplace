# Policy Trial Iteration

## When to Use
- When designing a new programme that will be rolled out broadly.
- When building learning capacity into an existing programme.
- When choosing between competing design options where evidence is thin.

## Fails When
- **The programme is acute emergency response.** Some interventions cannot afford iteration time.
- **The political window for iteration is narrow.** Sometimes single-launch is the only feasible option.

## Core Concept

Most programmes that work are the result of multiple design iterations. The "design the whole thing in advance and roll it out at scale" model wastes resources because the design is not optimised — small design choices that matter a lot (frequency, recipient, framing, delivery mode) are rarely well-specified in advance.

The trial-iteration approach has several elements:

**Start with a pilot** — small enough to learn from, large enough to generate credible evidence on the key design questions.

**Identify the design choices that matter most** and structure the pilot to test them. The pilot is not just "does this work?" — it is "what version of this works best?"

**Build in evaluation from day one.** Programmes that try to evaluate after the fact rarely produce credible evidence. Pre-registration, baseline data collection, control comparisons must be designed at the outset.

**Iterate on design.** Use the pilot findings to redesign before scaling. Multiple rounds of small-scale iteration are usually better than one large rollout with built-in irreversibility.

**Scale incrementally with continued evaluation.** Even after scale-up, build in monitoring and evaluation to confirm that pilot effects hold at scale. Where effects fade, redesign.

**Accept that programmes will fail.** Some pilots show no effect or negative effects. The honest response is to redesign or stop, not to defend the initial design.

The cultural challenge in many development bureaucracies is that iteration looks like indecision or wasted effort. Build the case explicitly: iteration produces better programmes that deliver more impact per unit of resource, even if it appears slower upfront.

## How to Apply

1. **Build evaluation into the programme architecture from the start** — not as an add-on.
2. **Identify the highest-leverage design choices** and structure the pilot to test them.
3. **Plan multiple iteration rounds.** A single pilot followed by scale-up is rarely enough.
4. **Build political space for iteration** — be transparent with stakeholders about why iteration matters.

## Examples

**Situation:** Designing a new graduation programme for extreme-poor households in South Africa.

**Application:** Phase 1: pilot in selected districts with random assignment, testing 2–3 versions of the asset transfer (cash vs livestock vs choice), 2 versions of consumption support duration (12 vs 18 months), 2 versions of training intensity. Evaluate at 18 and 36 months. Phase 2: redesign based on findings, pilot the refined design in additional districts. Phase 3: scale based on the refined design with continued M&E.

**Result:** A programme architecture built for learning rather than for upfront completeness.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the pilot as a confirmation exercise.
**Why:** The pilot exists to find out what works, including the possibility that the planned design doesn't.

**Don't:** Lock in design choices that the pilot was supposed to inform.
**Why:** If political or budget commitments lock in the design before the pilot reports, the pilot is theatre.
