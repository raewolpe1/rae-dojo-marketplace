# Microfinance Evidence

## When to Use
- When evaluating proposed microfinance interventions or programmes.
- When responding to claims that microfinance either ends poverty or causes harm.
- When summarising the evidence for policy decisions on financial inclusion.

## Fails When
- **The discussion is committed to a prior position.** Evidence rarely changes ideologically committed minds.
- **The specific institutional context** matters more than the global evidence — engage with the specifics.

## Core Concept

Microfinance — primarily microcredit in its classical form — has been one of the most studied interventions in development economics. Six independent RCTs (India, Ethiopia, Mexico, Morocco, Mongolia, Bosnia) and many smaller studies have produced a remarkably consistent picture.

**What microfinance does.** Modest positive effects on business outcomes — business creation, expansion, sometimes profits. Consumption smoothing across shocks. Some evidence of investment in productive assets. Effects concentrated among the small subset of borrowers who use credit productively.

**What microfinance does not do.** It does not produce broad-based poverty reduction at the population level. It does not transform women's empowerment in the ways early advocates claimed. It does not lift large numbers out of poverty. Average effects on consumption are small or zero.

**What microfinance does not cause.** Despite vocal critique, the evidence does not support claims of widespread debt traps, destitution, suicides, or harm at the population level. Specific cases of harm exist (predatory lending in particular contexts), but they are not the dominant pattern.

The conclusion: microfinance is a moderately useful tool in the financial inclusion toolkit, alongside savings products, payment systems, agricultural insurance, and consumer credit. It is not a magic bullet, not a fraud. The polarised public debate reflects the unfortunate tendency of development policy discussion to swing between hype and denouncement.

For policy: microfinance has a place where there is genuine credit demand among potential entrepreneurs (a smaller subset of the poor than enthusiasts claim), where institutional design is sound (group lending where group dynamics work, individual lending where they don't, reasonable interest rates that cover costs without predation), and where complementary services (savings, training, insurance) are available. Where these conditions are not met, microfinance is unlikely to achieve much.

## How to Apply

1. **Distinguish the average effect from the heterogeneous effects.** Microfinance helps a subset of borrowers substantially; the average effect is small because most borrowers experience small effects.
2. **Combine with other financial services.** Credit alone is a partial tool; savings, insurance, payments matter more for most poor households.
3. **Design carefully** — interest rates, group dynamics, collection schedules, default management.
4. **Resist both the enthusiast and denouncer framings** — both are empirically unsupported.

## Examples

**Situation:** A donor is proposing a major scale-up of microfinance access in rural South Africa as a poverty reduction strategy.

**Application:** The proposal is overstated. Microfinance does not deliver poverty reduction at the scale the proposal implies. A more useful financial inclusion strategy would emphasise savings products (commitment savings, mobile money), payment infrastructure (mobile money, banking), insurance (agricultural, health), and consumer credit on regulated terms — alongside, not instead of, microfinance. The portfolio approach matches the evidence better than the single-instrument approach.

**Result:** A redesigned financial inclusion programme that reflects what the evidence actually supports.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Adopt the early enthusiast framing.
**Why:** It is empirically wrong. Microfinance is useful but not transformative.

**Don't:** Adopt the later denouncer framing.
**Why:** It is also empirically wrong. Microfinance does not cause widespread harm.
