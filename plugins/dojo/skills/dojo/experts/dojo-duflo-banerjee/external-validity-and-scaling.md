# External Validity and Scaling

## When to Use
- When considering whether findings from one country/context apply to another.
- When planning to scale a successful pilot from a small randomised study to a national programme.
- When assessing the policy relevance of evidence from elsewhere.

## Fails When
- **The scaling environment differs in ways that change the mechanism.** Findings that depend on contextual features do not transport.
- **The scale itself changes the intervention.** What works in a 1,000-person pilot may not work in a 10-million-person rollout — equilibrium effects, capacity constraints, political dynamics.

## Core Concept

External validity is the question of whether findings from a specific study apply elsewhere. Internal validity (does the study show what it claims to show in the study population?) is established by good design. External validity (does the finding apply to a different population or context?) is established by replication, mechanism understanding, and careful adaptation.

Three patterns characterise external validity in development RCTs.

**Some findings transport widely.** Cash transfers reduce poverty depth across many contexts. Free distribution beats cost-sharing for preventive health products in many contexts. Information about school quality shifts choices in many contexts. These findings have been replicated enough times that the external validity claim is robust.

**Some findings do not transport.** Community monitoring effects on school quality work in Kenya, fail in India. Microcredit average effects vary across contexts (small in most, near-zero in some). Conditional cash transfer effects depend on the conditioned behaviour and the baseline rate.

**Some findings transport only with adaptation.** Teaching at the right level works across many countries when the core elements (diagnostic assessment, ability grouping, focused instruction) are preserved but the specific materials, training, and language adapt to local conditions.

Scaling carries its own challenges beyond external validity. The pilot effect may not hold at scale because: implementation quality typically declines as programmes grow; political dynamics that supported the pilot may not support the scale-up; market equilibrium effects (when many villages get the intervention, market prices and behaviours adjust differently than when one village does); and the population reached by the scale-up may differ from the pilot population.

The honest position on scaling: pilot, evaluate, scale incrementally, re-evaluate at each scaling step, and be willing to redesign or stop if the effects fade.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the mechanism** through which the intervention worked. External validity depends on whether the mechanism is plausible in the new context.
2. **Check the contextual conditions.** What in the original context might have been necessary for the effect? Is it present in the new context?
3. **Plan replication or local piloting** before full scale-up. Don't assume; verify.
4. **Build evaluation into scaling.** Scale in waves, evaluate as you go, adjust based on findings.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African NGO wants to adopt the BRAC graduation approach, based on the strong international evidence base from six RCTs.

**Application:** The evidence is strong, but the contexts (Bangladesh, Ethiopia, Ghana, Honduras, India, Pakistan, Peru) differ in important ways from rural South Africa — labour market structure, existing social grant architecture, available productive assets. The recommendation: pilot in selected South African districts with random assignment, adapt the asset choice and consumption support design to local conditions, evaluate over 24+ months. The pilot will resolve external validity for the South African context.

**Result:** A responsible scaling plan that learns from the international evidence without assuming uniform transportability.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume findings from one developing country apply to another.
**Why:** Developing countries differ from each other as much as they differ from rich countries. "Africa" or "developing countries" is not a context.

**Don't:** Scale without re-evaluating.
**Why:** Programmes change at scale. The evidence at scale must be regenerated.
