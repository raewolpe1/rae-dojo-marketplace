# Poverty Traps

## When to Use
- When designing interventions for the chronically poor or extreme poor.
- When evaluating "graduation programmes" or integrated anti-poverty bundles.
- When debating whether transfers alone or transfers-plus-services are appropriate.

## Fails When
- **You apply "poverty trap" framing to all poverty.** Some poverty is trap-based; some is the result of a recent shock that will pass; some is chronic but not self-reinforcing.
- **You assume a single intervention will release the trap.** The evidence suggests integrated bundles work better than single instruments.

## Core Concept

A poverty trap is a situation in which the poor face constraints whose effects reinforce each other and reproduce poverty across time. The classic poverty-trap dynamic: low income limits investment in nutrition, health, or schooling; the limitation reduces productive capacity; reduced capacity reproduces low income. Several specific traps are well-documented: nutritional traps (low calorie intake limits work capacity which limits earnings), credit traps (lack of collateral prevents access to credit which prevents investment), information traps (lack of exposure to opportunity sets limits aspirations and choices), bandwidth traps (the cognitive load of managing scarcity reduces decision quality which compounds the scarcity), location traps (geographic isolation limits market access which limits returns to productive effort).

The empirical evidence is mixed. Some traps are real and demonstrable through experiments where a one-time intervention produces sustained improvement. Others are not — for many poor households, persistent poverty reflects ongoing low returns to effort rather than a discrete trap that could be released by a one-time push.

The most successful trap-release intervention is the **graduation approach**, pioneered by BRAC in Bangladesh and replicated in many contexts. It combines: a productive asset transfer (typically a cow, goats, or shop inventory); consumption support during the productive build-up period; training; coaching; access to savings; and health support. Randomised evaluations across six countries (Ethiopia, Ghana, Honduras, India, Pakistan, Peru) found substantial and sustained effects on consumption, assets, food security, and mental health, persisting three to seven years after programme end.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose which constraints are binding** for the target population. Not all traps are present in all contexts.
2. **Address the specific binding constraints** with integrated instruments — asset, consumption support, skills, services.
3. **Plan for the duration of build-up.** Graduation effects take 18–24 months of intensive support to materialise.
4. **Measure long-run effects** — the value of trap-release is sustained, not immediate.

## Examples

**Situation:** Designing a programme for extreme-poor households in rural South Africa where Child Support Grants reach but extreme poverty persists.

**Application:** A graduation-style bundle — productive asset (livestock, small-scale agricultural inputs), 18 months of consumption support layered on CSG, agricultural training, savings group enrolment, and case-worker support — adapted to the South African rural context. Pilot, evaluate, refine.

**Result:** A trap-release intervention complementing the existing transfer architecture.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use "poverty trap" rhetorically to justify any intervention.
**Why:** The concept has specific empirical content. Use it precisely.

**Don't:** Assume one-time transfers will permanently change trajectories.
**Why:** The evidence on one-time transfers shows fade-out absent the support bundle. Integration is the lesson.
