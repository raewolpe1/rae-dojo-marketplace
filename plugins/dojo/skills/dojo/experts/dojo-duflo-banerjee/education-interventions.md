# Education Interventions

## When to Use
- When designing interventions to improve learning, attendance, or transition rates.
- When evaluating proposed pedagogical or structural reforms.
- When weighing different uses of limited education-sector resources.

## Fails When
- **The system lacks basics.** No teachers, no schools, no materials — the evidence on intervention design assumes these are present.
- **The question is system-level governance reform** rather than within-school intervention.

## Core Concept

The evidence on education interventions in developing countries clusters around a few robust findings. The most important: **learning is not the same as schooling**, and many interventions that increase enrolment do not increase learning. Once basic enrolment is achieved (which most developing countries have), the binding constraint shifts to learning quality.

What raises learning at modest cost:

**Teaching at the right level.** Pratham's "Teaching at the Right Level" (TaRL) approach groups students by current ability rather than grade, focuses on foundational skills, and has substantial evidence from multiple RCTs of large effects at low cost. The most policy-ready learning intervention in the global evidence base.

**Information about school quality.** Providing parents with credible information about school performance shifts choices and creates pressure on schools. Effects are robust where school choice exists.

**Targeted instruction technology** — computer-assisted learning calibrated to student ability — works in specific contexts where it can be sustained.

**Teacher accountability through community monitoring.** Mixed evidence — works in some contexts (Kenya), fails in others (India). Context-specific.

What does not raise learning, despite policy enthusiasm:

**Reducing class size at the margins** — modest effects relative to cost. Larger class size reductions have larger effects but are expensive.

**Providing inputs (textbooks, materials)** absent changes in pedagogy or teacher engagement — limited effects.

**Conditional cash transfers tied to attendance** — raise attendance but not learning, since attendance was already high in most contexts.

**Generic teacher training** — small effects unless tightly linked to specific pedagogical changes (e.g. TaRL training).

The lesson: improving education quality is harder than improving enrolment, and the politically attractive interventions (more resources, smaller classes, more materials) often produce smaller effects than the less obvious pedagogical changes (teaching at the right level, focused instruction, accountability).

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose the binding constraint** — enrolment, attendance, learning quality, or transition. Different problems require different interventions.
2. **Survey the evidence base** before designing — many education questions have multiple RCT answers.
3. **Prioritise pedagogy over inputs** where the basics are in place.
4. **Build evaluation into the intervention** to confirm context applicability.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial education department wants to improve learning outcomes in foundation phase (Grades R–3).

**Application:** The strongest evidence supports a TaRL-style intervention — diagnostic assessment, ability grouping for portions of the day, focused instruction in foundational reading and numeracy skills, teacher training and ongoing support. Pilot in selected schools with random assignment to enable evaluation, then scale based on findings.

**Result:** A pedagogical intervention with the strongest international evidence base, adapted to the South African context.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Default to input-based interventions (textbooks, smaller classes, more teachers) without checking the evidence on effect size.
**Why:** These are politically attractive but often produce smaller effects than pedagogical changes.

**Don't:** Generalise without piloting.
**Why:** Education interventions are particularly context-sensitive. Pilot before scaling.
