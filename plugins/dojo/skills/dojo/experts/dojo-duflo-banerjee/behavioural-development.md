# Behavioural Development Economics

## When to Use
- When the design of a transfer, service, or programme depends on how beneficiaries respond.
- When debating defaults, framing, reminders, commitment devices, or social norm interventions.
- When responding to claims that the poor are poor because of irrational choices.

## Fails When
- **You use behavioural design as a substitute for structural change.** Nudges around savings cannot solve the problem of low income. The behavioural toolkit is complement, not substitute.
- **You assume universal cognitive patterns.** Behavioural responses vary by context, gender, age, and the specific framing — calibrate, don't extrapolate.

## Core Concept

The poor face the same cognitive constraints as everyone else — present bias, attention limits, social influence, default effects, framing effects. What differs is the cost of small mistakes. A wealthy person who forgets to save can correct it next month; a poor person who forgets to save in advance of the school-fee period may have to take a child out of school. The high cost of small errors makes behavioural design more consequential for the poor than for the rich.

Several behavioural findings are robust across contexts. Defaults shape behaviour powerfully — automatic enrolment in a savings programme produces dramatically higher participation than opt-in. Reminders work — text-message reminders for vaccination appointments, medication adherence, or school attendance increase compliance at trivial cost. Commitment devices work — a savings product that restricts withdrawal until a specified date or amount produces higher accumulation than a flexible alternative. Information shapes choice — exposure to information about school quality, returns to schooling, or job opportunities shifts decisions substantially. Social influence is strong — interventions that change what people see others doing change what people do.

The behavioural lens is not about "fixing" the poor. It is about designing programmes that work given how people actually behave. A cash transfer to a woman delivered in cash on a fixed date with no information about how to use it has a particular impact. The same transfer to the same woman delivered to a mobile account, with a savings default, with light-touch information about productive uses, has a different and usually larger impact. The intervention is "the same" only in the lazy formulation; in real terms, behavioural design changes what the intervention is.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the behavioural mechanism** the intervention is meant to operate through.
2. **Design for actual behaviour** — defaults, reminders, framing, social context.
3. **Test variations** — small design changes can produce large effect differences; pilot to learn which design works best.
4. **Avoid the "rational agent" assumption** that beneficiaries will optimise across the available choices without support.

## Examples

**Situation:** Designing a savings product for low-income agricultural households whose income arrives at harvest but expenses are spread across the year.

**Application:** Default enrolment in a commitment savings product at the moment of harvest payment; automatic transfer of a specified percentage; restricted withdrawal until a chosen date (e.g. start of planting season for input purchase). Light-touch SMS reminders. The behavioural design transforms the savings product from a generic option to a tool calibrated to the actual cognitive and budget pressures of the population.

**Result:** Substantially higher savings accumulation than the same product without the behavioural design features.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Confuse behavioural design with manipulation.
**Why:** Designing for actual behaviour is not manipulation; it is acknowledgment that the textbook rational agent is a pedagogical fiction. The poor benefit from defaults and reminders precisely as the wealthy do.

**Don't:** Use behavioural framing to avoid structural change.
**Why:** Behavioural design improves programmes but does not replace the income or service the programme provides.
