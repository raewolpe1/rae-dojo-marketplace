# Growth Scepticism

## When to Use
- When the discussion assumes that economic growth will predictably solve poverty, inequality, or social problems.
- When responding to "rising tide lifts all boats" or trickle-down arguments.
- When advocating humility about what macro growth advocacy can deliver.

## Fails When
- **The question is about industrial transformation** rather than rich-country growth. The scepticism is about mature-economy growth, not about catch-up development.
- **You over-state into denial.** Growth matters; the argument is about overclaiming its effects.

## Core Concept

The economic growth experience of rich countries since 1980 has produced substantial macro-level GDP growth alongside stagnant or falling living standards for many sub-populations, rising inequality, and limited progress on social outcomes that growth advocates had promised would follow. The macro-empirical record on growth as a solution to development problems is much weaker than the discipline's confidence about it suggests.

Several specific findings underpin this:

**Aggregate growth does not predictably reduce poverty** within countries, particularly where inequality is rising. The poverty elasticity of growth depends on the distributional pattern; growth concentrated at the top reduces poverty slowly or not at all.

**The link between growth and social outcomes is weaker than assumed.** Health, education, and life satisfaction outcomes have improved in some growing economies and stagnated in others. The relationship is mediated by specific policies and institutional arrangements, not by growth itself.

**Cross-country growth determinants are not well-identified.** Decades of cross-country growth regressions have produced contradictory findings, sensitive to specification and sample. The "we know what causes growth" position is poorly grounded.

**At the micro level, what works is much clearer than what works at the macro level.** Specific interventions (bed nets, deworming, conditional cash transfers, teaching at the right level, free preventive health) have well-identified effects. The macro-level policy advice (liberalise, stabilise, reform institutions) has much weaker empirical foundations.

The growth-scepticism position is not anti-growth. It is anti-overclaiming. Growth matters; but the discipline should be much more humble about predicting it, much more focused on what specific policies can do for specific outcomes, and much more open to direct interventions on the outcomes that matter (poverty, health, education) rather than relying on growth to deliver them downstream.

This argument is developed at length in *Good Economics for Hard Times* (Banerjee and Duflo, 2019).

## How to Apply

1. **Resist the framing that growth is the master solution.** The evidence does not support it.
2. **Focus on specific outcomes** — poverty, health, education, employment — with the specific instruments that affect them.
3. **Take growth seriously where it is achievable** — but as one instrument among several, not as the sole goal.
4. **Be honest about macro uncertainty.** Predicting growth is hard; producing specific interventions effects is more tractable.

## Examples

**Situation:** A policy debate frames South Africa's unemployment problem as fundamentally a growth problem — "if we grew at 5% rather than 1%, the unemployment would solve itself."

**Application:** The framing is partially right and partially misleading. Higher growth would reduce unemployment, but the relationship is not automatic — labour-intensive growth absorbs labour, capital-intensive or finance-led growth does not. The question is what kind of growth, not just whether to grow. Direct labour market interventions (active labour market policies, public employment programmes, training matched to demand, sectoral expansion in labour-intensive industries) deserve weight alongside the growth strategy.

**Result:** A more honest framing in which growth is one instrument and direct interventions are another, with both required.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use growth scepticism to oppose all growth-promoting policies.
**Why:** Some growth-promoting policies (especially industrial-policy-led growth) directly address poverty and employment. The scepticism is about overclaiming, not opposition.

**Don't:** Substitute slogans for evidence.
**Why:** "Growth doesn't reduce poverty" is as misleading as "growth solves everything." Both are slogans; the evidence is nuanced.
