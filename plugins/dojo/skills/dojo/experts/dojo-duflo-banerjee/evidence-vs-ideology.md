# Evidence vs Ideology

## When to Use
- When a policy debate is being conducted in ideological framings ("cash transfers create dependency", "the market will solve it", "the state cannot deliver") that admit of empirical answers.
- When advocating evidence-based policy as a counterweight to ideologically-driven positions.

## Fails When
- **The question is genuinely a values question** — distributional priorities, what kind of society to be — where evidence is input but not determinative.
- **The evidence base is too thin** to displace the ideological position.

## Core Concept

Most development policy debates are conducted partly empirically and partly ideologically. The empirical part is where evidence can settle the question; the ideological part is where values, distributional preferences, and political identities are at stake. The mistake is to confuse the two — to treat ideological positions as if they were empirical claims, or to treat empirical claims as if they were ideological preferences.

The Duflo-Banerjee orientation is to be aggressive on the empirical questions and modest on the ideological ones. The empirical questions: does cash transfer create dependency (no, evidence is overwhelming)? Does microfinance reduce poverty at scale (modestly, the evidence is clear)? Does free distribution beat cost-sharing for preventive health (yes, robustly)? Do conditional cash transfers outperform unconditional (depends on what you condition; usually small marginal effects)? These have answers.

The ideological questions: how generous should the safety net be? How should we balance growth and equality? What is the appropriate role of the state? These are political-philosophical, not empirical. Evidence informs but does not settle them.

The discipline of separating the two is the discipline of evidence-based policy. The failure mode of evidence-based policy is to pretend that all questions are empirical, which both inflates the discipline's reach and produces poor policy when the genuine values dimensions are ignored.

The corresponding failure mode of ideology-driven policy is to pretend that empirical questions are settled by ideology — which produces policies that do not work because they were designed by reference to preferred theories rather than to what the evidence shows.

The plumber-economist stance combines: clarity on the empirical questions, where evidence has accumulated; modesty on the ideological-political questions, where evidence is input but not determinative; and explicit acknowledgment of which is which.

## How to Apply

1. **Decompose the policy debate** into empirical sub-questions and values sub-questions.
2. **Settle the empirical sub-questions** with the available evidence.
3. **Engage the values sub-questions** transparently as political-philosophical, not technical.
4. **Resist conflation** in both directions — neither inflate values into empirical claims nor reduce values to empirical questions.

## Examples

**Situation:** A debate about expanding the South African Child Support Grant. One side argues "transfers create dependency"; another argues "transfers are a right".

**Application:** "Transfers create dependency" is an empirical claim. The evidence is overwhelming that they do not — recipient households work as much or more than comparable non-recipient households, invest in children's outcomes, and use transfers productively. This claim is settled. "Transfers are a right" is a values claim — it is a position about what justice requires. The evidence does not settle it; political philosophy does. Both sides should be clearer about which kind of claim they are making.

**Result:** A more honest debate. The empirical claim against transfers is unfounded and should be dropped. The values claim for transfers stands or falls on its political-philosophical merits, separate from the empirical record.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat values claims as merely ideological.
**Why:** They are genuine and important. Evidence-based policy does not displace political philosophy.

**Don't:** Treat empirical claims as merely values claims.
**Why:** Many policy claims that masquerade as values claims are actually empirical claims that the evidence has settled — and the evidence should be deployed.
