# Cash Transfers and Social Protection

## When to Use
- When designing or reviewing cash transfer programmes — conditional or unconditional, targeted or universal, in cash or via mobile money.
- When debating cash versus in-kind transfers.
- When evaluating the "cash plus" approach (cash combined with services).

## Fails When
- **The country lacks delivery infrastructure** (no national ID, no mobile money, no banking penetration in the target areas). Design must work with available infrastructure.
- **The political economy will not permit transfers at scale** — design questions become moot.

## Core Concept

The evidence on cash transfers is now substantial and converges on several findings. Cash transfers reduce poverty depth (the gap between household consumption and the poverty line), particularly for the deepest poor. They smooth consumption across shocks. They modestly increase investment in productive activities, education, and health. They do not cause widespread "lazy" behaviour, dependency, or expenditure on temptation goods — the evidence on this is overwhelming and convergent.

The design choices that matter:

**Conditional vs unconditional.** Conditions (school attendance, health visits) shift behaviour modestly toward conditioned outcomes but raise implementation costs and exclude the most marginalised who cannot reliably comply. Conditions are most valuable where the conditioned behaviour would not otherwise occur (e.g. girls' secondary schooling in resistant cultural contexts). Where the behaviour is already widespread (universal primary attendance), conditions add little.

**Targeting.** Categorical targeting (e.g. households with children) is administratively simple and politically robust. Means-testing is more accurate but expensive and excludes households just above the threshold. Community-based targeting works in some contexts and fails in others (capture by local elites). The best targeting choice depends on administrative capacity and political context.

**Recipient.** Transfers to women shift household allocation toward children's outcomes more than transfers to men, on average. The effect is robust but the magnitude varies. Where intra-household dynamics are central to the desired outcome, choose the recipient deliberately.

**Frequency and timing.** Monthly transfers are easier to budget but produce less investment than lump-sum. Lump-sum transfers timed to productive cycles (planting, school enrolment) produce more investment but require more sophisticated household financial management.

**Cash vs in-kind.** Cash is almost always more cost-effective per outcome than equivalent in-kind transfers. In-kind is preferable in specific cases — school meals where the goal is nutrition during the school day, immediate emergency response where markets have failed, supplementary feeding for malnourished children where the target is the specific child.

**Cash plus.** Cash combined with services (childcare, training, case management, savings, financial inclusion) substantially outperforms cash alone for transformative outcomes. The "cash plus" literature is one of the most policy-relevant evidence accumulations of the past decade.

## How to Apply

1. **Specify the policy goal** — poverty depth reduction, asset accumulation, behaviour change, emergency response. Different goals favour different designs.
2. **Choose the design choices deliberately** — conditionality, targeting, recipient, frequency, cash vs in-kind, complementary services — based on evidence and context.
3. **Pilot and iterate** on the choices where evidence is thin or context-specific.
4. **Monitor delivery integrity** — the largest source of programme failure is delivery, not design.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's Child Support Grant — universal categorical targeting, unconditional, monthly cash via bank or post office, paid to caregiver (usually woman).

**Application:** The design is largely well-supported by the evidence. The CSG reaches its target population effectively, smooths consumption, supports children's outcomes. Marginal design improvements — explore "CSG plus" pilots integrating early childhood development services, case management for vulnerable households, complementary financial inclusion — could add transformative value at modest cost.

**Result:** A defensibly designed programme with specific opportunities for cash-plus integration.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Add conditions automatically.
**Why:** Conditions impose costs and exclude vulnerable households. The case for conditions should be made on specific evidence for the specific behaviour being conditioned.

**Don't:** Treat in-kind as inferior automatically.
**Why:** In-kind has specific advantages in specific cases. Use evidence on the specific outcome, not blanket preference.
