# Health Interventions

## When to Use
- When designing the delivery of preventive health products (bed nets, deworming, immunisation).
- When debating cost-sharing for health services or products.
- When evaluating community health worker programmes or other delivery innovations.

## Fails When
- **The question is system-level** (health financing, hospital reform) rather than intervention delivery.
- **Basic health infrastructure is missing** in ways that prevent the intervention from being delivered.

## Core Concept

The evidence base on health interventions has produced several findings that have substantially shaped global health policy.

**Free distribution beats cost-sharing for preventive health products.** The Cohen-Dupas studies on bed nets, and parallel studies on deworming tablets, chlorine for water purification, and other preventive products, show that even small prices dramatically reduce uptake — far beyond what the standard "they value what they pay for" intuition predicts. Free distribution achieves much higher coverage at not much higher cost (because the per-unit cost is small relative to the impact gain from coverage).

**Behavioural design matters as much as the product.** A bed net distributed at a clinic without instruction produces lower use than the same bed net distributed at the home with a brief installation visit. Immunisation campaigns with small incentives (a kilo of lentils per visit), reminders, and convenient timing produce dramatically higher coverage than campaigns without these features.

**Community health workers** are effective for specific tasks — case-finding, referral, follow-up for chronic disease management — but require careful design (selection, training, supervision, incentives). Generic CHW programmes without these features under-perform.

**Conditional incentives for utilisation** work in many contexts — small payments or in-kind rewards for completing immunisation schedules, attending antenatal care, or completing TB treatment courses produce substantial improvements in completion rates at modest cost.

**Information shapes uptake** less than design and access do. Information campaigns about handwashing, vaccine safety, or HIV prevention have smaller effects than interventions that change the costs and convenience of behaviour.

The general lesson is that the standard "build a programme, raise awareness, charge a small price for sustainability" model under-performs. Free, convenient, behaviourally-designed delivery dominates on cost-effectiveness.

## How to Apply

1. **Default to free for preventive products** unless there is specific evidence that cost-sharing in the context is sustainable and high-coverage.
2. **Invest in delivery design** — point of distribution, timing, installation support, reminders, incentives.
3. **Match the CHW model to the specific tasks** — generic CHW programmes are weaker than task-specific ones.
4. **Combine information with behavioural design** — information alone rarely changes behaviour at scale.

## Examples

**Situation:** A district health authority is designing a bed net distribution programme for malaria prevention.

**Application:** Free distribution, paired with home-based installation visits to ensure correct use, with light-touch follow-up at 3 and 6 months. Cost-sharing schemes (even at very low prices) should be rejected based on the evidence on price elasticity.

**Result:** Coverage and use rates substantially higher than would be achieved through commercial or subsidised sales models.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use cost-sharing for "sustainability."
**Why:** The evidence shows cost-sharing dramatically lowers coverage. Programme sustainability is a financing question, not a price-on-recipient question.

**Don't:** Treat information campaigns as substitutes for behavioural design.
**Why:** People often know the right thing to do but don't do it. Design for behaviour, not just for knowledge.
