# Plumber Economists

## When to Use
- When discussing the appropriate role of economists in policymaking.
- When defending the unglamorous design-detail work against the lure of grand strategy pronouncements.

## Fails When
- **The question is genuinely strategic-structural** — the architect's job, not the plumber's. Hand it on.

## Core Concept

Duflo, in her 2017 Ely Lecture "The Economist as Plumber," argued that economists' most useful contribution to policy is often in the design details — the equivalent of a plumber's specific expertise on fittings, water pressure, and drainage — rather than in the grand strategic pronouncements that economists are often called upon to make.

The argument: the big strategic choices in policy (whether to have a programme, what its goals should be) are largely political questions where economists' technical expertise has little distinctive value. The design choices (how the programme works, who is eligible, when payments are made, what the conditions are, how delivery is structured) are technical questions where careful empirical and design work makes substantial differences to outcomes.

Many of the largest improvements in development programmes over the past two decades have come from plumbing-level details: changing the recipient of a cash transfer; moving from paper to mobile delivery; adding reminder SMSes; changing the default enrolment for a benefit; restructuring a programme around behavioural insights. None of these are grand strategic choices. All of them have moved outcomes substantially.

The plumber-economist stance is humble about disciplinary expertise on the big political-strategic questions and confident about disciplinary expertise on the design questions. The orientation pushes economists toward in-government practical roles, embedded research, and the unglamorous work of getting programmes to work better — and away from the op-ed-and-conference circuit of grand strategic pronouncements.

The stance does not mean economists cannot engage with strategic questions; it means they should be honest about which contributions come from disciplinary expertise and which come from political-philosophical preference.

## How to Apply

1. **Distinguish strategic-political questions from technical-design questions.** Engage at the appropriate level on each.
2. **Invest in the design details.** Small changes in how programmes operate often matter more than large changes in their stated goals.
3. **Embed with implementers.** The plumber learns more from the building than from the architecture textbook.
4. **Be honest about disciplinary modesty.** The big strategic claims usually rest more on preference than on disciplinary expertise.

## Examples

**Situation:** An economist is asked to advise on whether South Africa should expand its Child Support Grant.

**Application:** The strategic question (should the grant be expanded?) is largely political — its answer depends on fiscal priorities, distributional preferences, and political coalitions. The plumbing questions (if expanded, how should it be designed? at what age cutoffs? with what delivery mode? with what complementary services?) are technical and have evidence-based answers. The economist's distinctive contribution is at the plumbing level. The strategic recommendation should be flagged as more political than technical.

**Result:** A more honest advisory stance, with clearer claims on the technical questions and more humility on the political ones.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Avoid the plumbing because it lacks prestige.
**Why:** This is where economists' expertise actually adds the most value. Embrace the work.

**Don't:** Pretend strategic advice is technical.
**Why:** Inflating political preferences into technical claims weakens the credibility of the genuinely technical contributions.
