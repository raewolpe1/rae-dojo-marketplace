# Arendt and Development

## When to Use
- When examining the development industry through Arendt's analytical framework — as a specific form of institutional governance with particular relationships to action, thinking, plurality, and freedom.
- When considering whether development as currently practised creates or destroys political life.
- When a practitioner wants to understand how Arendt's concepts apply to their daily work.

## Core Concept
Arendt did not write about development. But her analysis of the modern condition — the triumph of the social over the political, the rise of bureaucratic governance, the displacement of action by administration — describes the development industry with uncomfortable accuracy.

Development is overwhelmingly an administrative enterprise. It identifies needs through assessment, designs interventions through planning, delivers services through implementation, and measures results through evaluation. Each of these activities is administrative — it follows procedure, applies methodology, and produces outputs. None of them is action in Arendt's sense — none involves appearing before others, revealing who one is, or initiating something unpredictable.

Development also exemplifies the social question: it is concerned primarily with necessity — with feeding, healing, educating, and economically empowering populations whose material needs are urgent. This concern is legitimate. But when it consumes all available institutional energy — when every programme, every strategy, every conversation is about delivery — the political dimension disappears. Nobody asks who decides, whose voice is heard, what kind of society is being built. Everyone asks how many were reached, what outcomes were achieved, and whether the targets were met.

Development institutions also exemplify bureaucratic rule by nobody. The logframe decides what counts. The procurement system decides who provides. The M&E framework decides what is measured. The financial management system decides how money flows. These are all political decisions — but they present themselves as technical requirements, embedded in systems that nobody can change because nobody made them.

The result is an industry that administers material life with increasing sophistication while the political life of the populations it serves either stagnates or deteriorates. This is not a conspiracy. It is the logic of the social question consuming the political, exactly as Arendt predicted.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess the programme's relationship to political life.** "Does this programme create conditions for people to act as citizens — to deliberate, to shape decisions, to hold institutions accountable? Or does it position them solely as recipients of services?"
2. **Identify where thinking has been replaced by procedure.** "In the programme design, the evaluation, the reporting — where has genuine thinking been replaced by the application of methodology? Where has someone stopped to ask whether what is being done is right, not just whether it is being done correctly?"
3. **Map the rule by nobody.** "Which decisions in this programme are made by systems rather than by persons? The targeting criteria, the eligibility rules, the indicator framework — who decided these, and who bears responsibility for what they produce?"
4. **Create political space within administrative programmes.** "Even within a programme designed for service delivery, political space can be created: community decision-making about priorities, participant oversight of implementation, public accountability for results. These are not add-ons — they are the difference between administration and political life."

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor-funded health programme has achieved impressive results: reduced maternal mortality, increased vaccination coverage, improved nutrition indicators. But a political economy analysis reveals that the programme has undermined the government's own health system by creating parallel structures, paying higher salaries that attract government staff, and bypassing government planning processes. The government's health governance capacity has arguably weakened during the programme's operation.
**Application:** "The programme has brilliantly administered the social question — it has saved lives and improved health. But it has destroyed political life in the health sector. The government — the political institution that should govern health — has been bypassed, weakened, and rendered irrelevant. The programme makes the decisions: what priorities, what methods, what standards. The government implements what the programme decides. This is an inversion of the political relationship — the foreign institution governs, the national government administers. When the programme ends, the government will be weaker than when the programme began, and the political capacity to govern health — to make decisions, to set priorities, to be accountable to citizens — will have deteriorated. The programme has traded political life for administrative success. Arendt would recognise this immediately: the social question (health needs) has consumed the political question (who governs health), and the result is a population that is healthier but less politically capable of sustaining its own health. The administration was excellent. The politics was destroyed."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Argue that development should abandon service delivery for political engagement.
**Why:** Material needs are real and urgent. The point is not that development should stop administering but that it should also attend to the political conditions — governance, accountability, civic participation — that make the administration sustainable and legitimate.

**Don't:** Treat Arendt as providing a development methodology.
**Why:** Arendt provides analytical concepts, not programme designs. She tells you what to look for and what questions to ask. She does not tell you how to build a logframe or design a theory of change. That translation is the practitioner's work.
