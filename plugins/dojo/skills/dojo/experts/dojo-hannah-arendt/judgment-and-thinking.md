# Judgment and Thinking

## When to Use
- When a situation demands judgment — a response to a particular case that cannot be resolved by applying a general rule.
- When examining whether thinking is occurring within an institution, or whether procedure has replaced reflection.
- When developing the capacity for "representative thinking" — considering a situation from standpoints other than one's own.

## Core Concept
Arendt's unfinished work on judgment, drawn primarily from her *Lectures on Kant's Political Philosophy* (1982, posthumous), proposes judgment as the political faculty par excellence. Judgment is the capacity to assess a particular situation without subsuming it under a general rule. It is what we do when no rule covers the case — when the situation is unprecedented, ambiguous, or morally complex and we must respond anyway.

Arendt distinguishes thinking from knowing. Knowing deals with truths that can be verified — facts, data, evidence. Thinking deals with meaning — what things signify, what they imply, what they demand of us. Knowing can be delegated to systems: databases, indicators, algorithms. Thinking cannot. It is the internal dialogue of a person with themselves — the "two-in-one" that constitutes conscience. When this dialogue stops — when a person ceases to examine what they are doing and why — the capacity for evil is unleashed.

The key concept is "representative thinking" or the "enlarged mentality" (drawn from Kant). This is not empathy — it is not feeling what others feel. It is the disciplined effort to consider a situation from the standpoints of all those affected, to understand how it appears from perspectives other than one's own, and to reach a judgment that takes account of this plurality. Representative thinking does not produce certainty — it produces judgment, which is always provisional, always open to revision, always based on the best understanding available.

For development practice, the judgment/thinking framework challenges the dominant culture of evidence-based decision-making. Evidence is knowing — it tells you what is. Judgment tells you what it means and what to do about it. No amount of evidence can replace the act of judgment that decides which evidence matters, how to interpret it, and what it demands. The evaluation that produces data without judgment produces information without meaning. The decision-maker who relies entirely on evidence without exercising judgment is functioning, not thinking.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask whether thinking is occurring.** "Is anyone in this institution actually reflecting on what they are doing and why — not just calculating how to do it more efficiently? If the answer is no, thinking has been replaced by functioning."
2. **Practise representative thinking.** "Before making a decision, deliberately consider how this situation appears from the standpoints of all those affected: the beneficiaries, the excluded, the staff, the community, the government. Not to agree with all perspectives — but to take them into account."
3. **Distinguish judgment from methodology.** "A scoring matrix, a decision tree, a cost-benefit analysis — these are tools. They do not exercise judgment. Judgment is what you do with the result of the tool — the human act of assessing what the numbers mean and what should be done."
4. **Protect the space for thinking.** "Institutions that fill every moment with meetings, reports, and deliverables leave no time for thinking. Thinking requires stopping — requires the willingness to pause the production of outputs and reflect on what is being produced and why."

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluation team has collected extensive quantitative and qualitative data on a health programme. The data shows mixed results: some indicators improving, others declining, with significant variation across sites. The team must produce a judgment about whether the programme is working. The donor wants a clear answer: is it succeeding or failing?
**Application:** "The donor's demand for a binary judgment — succeeding or failing — is the demand to subsume a particular situation under a general rule: programmes either work or they do not. But this particular programme is not a specimen of a general category. It is a specific intervention in a specific context producing specific, mixed, contradictory results. Judgment here requires resisting the binary. It requires the team to think — to consider what the mixed results mean, why some sites succeed and others do not, what the variation reveals about the programme's theory of change and the contexts in which it operates. It requires representative thinking — considering the results from the standpoints of the communities served, the health workers implementing, the government partners, and the donor. These standpoints do not yield the same assessment. The community may value something the indicators do not measure. The health workers may understand a constraint the data does not capture. Judgment means producing an assessment that holds these perspectives together — that says, honestly, 'this is what we found, this is what it means from multiple standpoints, and this is our best assessment of what should be done.' This is harder than a binary verdict. But it is thinking rather than processing."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat judgment as subjective and therefore unreliable.
**Why:** Judgment is not arbitrary opinion. It is the disciplined exercise of considering particulars from multiple standpoints and reaching the best available assessment. It is fallible — but so is every other form of knowing. The alternative to judgment is not objectivity. It is thoughtlessness.

**Don't:** Present thinking as a luxury for academics.
**Why:** Thinking is a moral necessity for anyone who exercises power — which includes every development practitioner who designs, implements, or evaluates programmes. The claim that there is no time for thinking is precisely the institutional condition that Arendt identifies as dangerous.
