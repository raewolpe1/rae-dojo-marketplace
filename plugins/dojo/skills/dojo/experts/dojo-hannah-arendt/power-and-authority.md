# Power and Authority

## When to Use
- When analysing how governance operates in a development context — whether through genuine authority (recognised legitimacy), power (collective capacity), or violence (instrumental force).
- When examining why programmes that rely on compliance fail to produce sustained change.
- When the question is about what makes governance legitimate and what happens when legitimacy collapses.

## Core Concept
Arendt insists on precise distinctions among concepts that political theory — and everyday language — routinely conflates. Power is not what a ruler exercises over subjects. Power is the capacity that emerges when people act together in concert. It belongs to the group, not to the individual. A leader has power only insofar as the group empowers them. When the group withdraws its support, power vanishes — no matter how much violence the leader commands.

Violence is the opposite of power. Violence is instrumental — it uses tools (weapons, institutions, sanctions) to compel obedience. It is deployed precisely when power has failed — when consent can no longer be obtained. The more violence a regime requires, the less power it possesses. A government that maintains itself through force alone has lost power and replaced it with its functional opposite.

Authority is a third thing — neither power (collective capacity) nor violence (instrumental force) but a form of recognition. Authority is obeyed because it is recognised as legitimate — because people grant it a right to rule that does not depend on either persuasion or force. Arendt locates the paradigm of authority in the Roman tradition: the authority of the senate derived not from force but from the founding — from a continuous connection to the origins of the republic. When authority collapses — when neither tradition, religion, nor political founding provides a basis for recognition — societies face a crisis in which they resort to either violence or manipulation.

For development practice, these distinctions illuminate governance dynamics that are otherwise puzzling. A traditional leader who governs through authority (recognised legitimacy) exercises a different kind of governance from a government official who governs through bureaucratic power (institutional mandate) or a security force that governs through violence. Development programmes that strengthen "governance" without distinguishing these modalities may strengthen authority, power, or violence — with very different consequences.

## How to Apply
1. **Classify the governance modality.** "Is this institution or leader governing through authority (recognised legitimacy), power (collective capacity), or violence (instrumental force)? The answer determines the character of the governance and the appropriate intervention."
2. **Ask where power resides.** "Power belongs to the group. Does this institution derive its capacity from the collective support of its constituency, or does it maintain itself through bureaucratic mandate or force? If the latter, its power is fragile."
3. **Watch for the violence-power substitution.** "When an institution increases its use of sanctions, conditionality, or coercion, it is likely because its power — its capacity to generate consent — has declined. The escalation of enforcement signals the erosion of legitimacy."
4. **Assess the authority foundation.** "What gives this institution or leader authority? Tradition? Democratic election? Professional expertise? Religious sanction? Each foundation of authority has different implications for how it operates and when it collapses."

## Examples
**Situation:** A local government in a post-conflict country has been supported by a governance programme for five years. The programme has trained officials, strengthened financial management systems, and established community accountability mechanisms. But communities still prefer to take disputes to traditional leaders, tax compliance remains low, and community participation in local government processes is minimal.
**Application:** "The programme has built institutional capacity — bureaucratic power — but it has not established authority. The local government can process applications, manage budgets, and produce reports. But communities do not recognise it as legitimate. They recognise the traditional leaders — who derive authority from lineage, custom, and continuous connection to the community's founding. The local government derives its mandate from national legislation and international support, neither of which generates local authority. The programme's assumption was that capacity produces legitimacy — that a government that functions well will earn the community's recognition. Arendt would argue the reverse: authority is not earned through performance but through recognition, and recognition depends on a connection between the institution and the community's understanding of legitimate governance. The traditional leader has this connection. The local government, however competent, does not. The programme has strengthened power (institutional capacity) while the authority (community recognition) remains with different institutions. Until the programme addresses this gap — not by displacing traditional authority but by finding a relationship between it and formal governance — capacity will not translate into legitimacy."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that democratic election automatically confers authority.
**Why:** Elections confer procedural legitimacy but not necessarily authority in Arendt's sense. A democratically elected government that lacks connection to the community's traditions and self-understanding may have procedural mandate without recognised authority.

**Don't:** Treat power as something that individuals possess.
**Why:** Power belongs to the group. A leader exercises power only as long as the group supports them. The moment the group withdraws, the leader's "power" is revealed to have been the group's all along.
