# Arendt and Bureaucracy

## When to Use
- When diagnosing institutional situations where "the system" makes decisions, nobody bears responsibility, and harmful outcomes are produced without anyone intending them.
- When examining why large development organisations, government departments, or international institutions produce outcomes that no individual within them would choose.
- When the question is about how to reintroduce personal judgment into institutional processes designed to eliminate it.

## Core Concept
Arendt calls bureaucracy "rule by nobody" — a form of governance in which decisions are made by procedures, systems, and guidelines rather than by identifiable persons exercising judgment. Rule by nobody is not no rule — it is the most tyrannical form of rule because there is no one to whom the governed can appeal, no one who bears responsibility, no one who can be held accountable. The system decides, the system implements, the system evaluates — and the system has no conscience.

Bureaucracy does not eliminate power. It conceals it. Every system, every procedure, every guideline embodies decisions about what matters, how resources flow, and who benefits. But these decisions — once embedded in systems — become invisible. They present themselves as neutral procedure rather than as political choices. The targeting algorithm, the eligibility criteria, the reporting format — each encodes decisions that would be contestable if made by a person but become unquestionable when embedded in a system.

For development practice, rule by nobody is the default condition. The logframe decides what counts as an outcome. The procurement system decides who can provide services. The M&E framework decides what is measured and therefore what matters. The financial management system decides how money flows. In each case, the system decides — and nobody is responsible for the decisions the system makes. When the logframe excludes outcomes that matter to communities, nobody decided this — the format requires specific types of outcomes. When procurement rules exclude local providers, nobody chose this — the system requires competitive bidding. When M&E measures outputs rather than transformation, nobody preferred this — the indicators were designed that way.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify rule by nobody.** "Who decided this? If you cannot name a person who made the decision and bears responsibility for it, you are facing rule by nobody. Name it."
2. **Make the embedded decisions visible.** "Every system encodes decisions. The logframe decides what counts. The eligibility criteria decide who is included. These are political decisions disguised as technical formats. Make them visible and contestable."
3. **Create accountability to persons.** "Systems do not bear responsibility — persons do. For every critical decision that the system makes, identify a person who is responsible for the decision and accountable for its consequences."
4. **Preserve space for judgment within systems.** "Design systems with discretionary space — points at which a person must exercise judgment rather than follow procedure. The case that does not fit the categories, the situation that the protocol does not cover — these require judgment, not escalation."

## Examples
**Situation:** A large international organisation has an internal policy requiring that all programmes above a certain budget must use competitive international procurement. A country office wants to purchase supplies from local vendors to support the local economy and reduce delivery times, but the procurement system requires international competitive bidding. The local vendors cannot compete on price against international suppliers. The procurement team explains that "the policy requires it."
**Application:** "The policy decides. Nobody decides. The person who wrote the procurement policy made a decision — that competition on price should override local economic development — but that person is long gone and the decision is now embedded in the system. The procurement team follows the policy. The country director follows the procurement team. The local vendors are excluded. The local economy is not supported. And nobody is responsible because everybody followed the rules. This is rule by nobody operating as it typically does: a political decision (price competition matters more than local economic impact) disguised as a technical requirement (competitive bidding is 'best practice'), implemented by people who follow procedure without exercising judgment about whether the procedure serves the programme's purposes. The solution is not to break the rules — it is to make the decision visible. Somebody, somewhere, decided that price competition matters more than local economic impact in every situation above a certain budget. That decision should be identifiable, attributable, and contestable. The person in the country office who exercises judgment — who says 'in this context, the policy produces a harmful outcome' — is thinking. The system that requires compliance regardless of context is not."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Argue that all procedure is harmful.
**Why:** Procedure exists for reasons: consistency, fairness, accountability, efficiency. The problem is not procedure per se but the substitution of procedure for judgment — the point at which following the rules becomes more important than achieving the purpose the rules were designed to serve.

**Don't:** Romanticise personal discretion as automatically better than systems.
**Why:** Personal discretion without accountability is arbitrary power. The goal is not to eliminate systems but to maintain space for judgment within them — and to ensure that when systems make decisions, someone is responsible.
