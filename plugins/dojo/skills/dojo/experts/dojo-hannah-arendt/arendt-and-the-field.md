# Arendt and the Field

## When to Use
- When positioning Arendt within broader intellectual debates and identifying productive pairings.
- When assessing the limits and blind spots of Arendt's analytical framework.
- When building a multi-framework approach that combines Arendt with other thinkers.

## Core Concept
Arendt is one of the twentieth century's most original political philosophers, but her work has significant critics and genuine limitations. Understanding these is essential for using the framework productively.

**Three serious critiques of Arendt:**

First, the social question problem. Arendt's insistence on separating the social from the political has been challenged by feminist, Marxist, and postcolonial scholars. The claim that politics should not be consumed by necessity overlooks the fact that for most people — particularly women, the poor, and the colonised — necessity is the political question. The distinction between the social and the political can function as a way of excluding the concerns of the dispossessed from the political realm. Arendt's idealisation of the Greek polis, where politics was possible only because slaves handled necessity, reinforces this critique.

Second, the race and colonialism gap. Arendt's treatment of race and colonialism in *The Origins of Totalitarianism* has been criticised for treating colonial violence as primarily a precursor to European totalitarianism rather than as a catastrophe in its own right. Her analysis of African colonialism in particular has been challenged for reproducing certain colonial assumptions. Her treatment of the Little Rock desegregation crisis revealed a troubling insensitivity to the lived experience of racial domination.

Third, the elitism charge. Arendt's conception of the public realm — modelled on the Greek polis and the American founding — can seem to privilege a specific, historically narrow form of political participation. The citizens who act in Arendt's public realm are educated, articulate, and free from material constraint. This raises the question of whether her political philosophy speaks to or about ordinary people, particularly in the Global South.

**Productive pairings:**

*Michel Foucault* provides what Arendt lacks: an analysis of how power operates through everyday practices, institutions, and knowledge systems rather than through grand political structures. Foucault shows the micro-politics that Arendt's macro-political analysis misses.

*Achille Mbembe* extends Arendt's analysis into colonial and postcolonial contexts. Where Arendt analyses totalitarianism as the destruction of political life in Europe, Mbembe analyses necropolitics as the administration of death in the colony. The pairing addresses Arendt's colonial blind spot.

*Amartya Sen* provides the capability framework that can operationalise Arendt's conception of freedom. Sen's "development as freedom" addresses the social question that Arendt acknowledges but resists engaging with directly, while Arendt provides the political dimension that Sen sometimes underweights.

*Michael Quinn Patton* provides practical evaluation methodology that can embody Arendt's insistence on judgment. Patton's developmental evaluation — responsive, emergent, judgment-centred — is closer to Arendt's conception of judgment than conventional evaluation methodologies.

*Pierre Bourdieu* provides the political economy of everyday life that Arendt's grand political theory lacks. Bourdieu's habitus, field, and capital show how structural inequality reproduces itself through daily practice — the mechanisms that Arendt's framework does not specify.

*Frantz Fanon* provides the postcolonial political theory that Arendt's Eurocentric framework requires. Fanon's analysis of violence, liberation, and the psychological dimensions of colonial domination complements Arendt's analysis of totalitarianism.

## How to Apply
1. **Use Arendt for the political diagnosis.** "Arendt's strength is identifying whether political life exists — whether people can act, think, and judge. Start with her diagnosis of the institutional and political conditions."
2. **Supplement with social analysis.** "Once the political diagnosis is established, bring in thinkers who address the social dimensions Arendt resists: Sen on capabilities, Bourdieu on everyday power, feminist scholars on the politics of necessity."
3. **Address the colonial dimension.** "In postcolonial contexts, pair Arendt with Mbembe or Fanon to address the colonial genealogy that Arendt acknowledges but does not fully develop."
4. **Connect to method.** "Arendt does not provide methods. Pair with Patton for evaluation, with Chambers for participatory practice, with deliberative democracy scholars for institutional design."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant is developing a conceptual framework for evaluating a governance programme that aims to strengthen citizen participation in budget processes across several African countries.
**Application:** "Begin with Arendt for the core political questions: does the programme create a genuine public realm where citizens can act — not merely participate in a structured process? Does the budget process involve deliberation among citizens with distinct perspectives, or does it administer a predetermined methodology? Is anyone thinking about what the budget means for the community's future, or is everyone calculating allocations? Layer Mbembe for the postcolonial dimension: does the governance structure reflect the commandement — personalised authority exercising sovereignty through spectacle — or genuine institutional governance? Are the budget decisions actually implemented, or do they exist as simulacra — performances of governance that do not translate into resource allocation? Add Bourdieu for the political economy: whose forms of capital — economic, social, cultural — enable participation in the budget process? Are the participants those with existing power reproducing their advantage through the participatory format? Use Patton for the evaluation methodology: developmental evaluation that exercises judgment about each country context rather than applying standardised indicators across all of them. The result is a framework that asks the right political questions (Arendt), situates them in the postcolonial context (Mbembe), specifies the power dynamics (Bourdieu), and evaluates with judgment (Patton)."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present Arendt as universally applicable without modification.
**Why:** Arendt's framework was developed from European experience — particularly the catastrophe of totalitarianism. Its application to non-European contexts requires adaptation, supplementation, and critical engagement with its blind spots.

**Don't:** Dismiss Arendt because of her limitations.
**Why:** Every thinker has blind spots. Arendt's contributions — the analysis of thinking as a moral activity, the concept of the banality of evil, the distinction between power and violence, the insistence on plurality — remain indispensable. Use them critically, not uncritically or not at all.
