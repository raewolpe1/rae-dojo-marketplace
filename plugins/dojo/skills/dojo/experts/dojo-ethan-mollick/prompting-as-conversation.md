# Prompting as Conversation: Beyond Prompt Engineering

## When to Use

- You are frustrated with AI outputs and believe better prompts would fix the problem.
- You are searching for prompt templates or formulas and need to shift toward a more productive approach.
- You are training colleagues on AI use and need to teach interaction technique rather than prompt recipes.

## Core Concept

The "prompt engineering" frame — the idea that there exists a perfect incantation that unlocks the best AI output — is wrong in a way that actively impedes effective AI use. Mollick argues for replacing it with a conversational frame: effective AI interaction is iterative dialogue, not one-shot instruction.

The prompt-engineering mindset leads people to spend twenty minutes crafting the perfect prompt, submit it, evaluate the output, and either accept or start over. This is like sending a single email to a colleague and expecting them to deliver a perfect product without any conversation. No working relationship functions that way. You would iterate, clarify, push back, refine, and collaborate through dialogue.

The conversational frame changes the practice. You start with a reasonable prompt — good enough, not perfect — and then you work with the output. "This is good but the tone is too academic — make it more accessible for municipal officials." "You have missed the fiscal dimension entirely — add analysis of how this programme interacts with the inter-governmental fiscal framework." "Your third recommendation is not feasible given the institutional capacity constraints I described — propose alternatives." Each exchange improves the output and deepens the AI's understanding of what you actually need.

This approach is both more effective and more accessible than prompt engineering. You do not need to learn a technical syntax or memorise template structures. You need to develop the same skill you already have for working with human colleagues: the ability to give clear direction, provide useful feedback, and iterate toward a good outcome.

For development consultants, the conversational frame maps naturally onto existing professional practice. You already know how to brief a junior consultant, review their draft, provide feedback, and guide them to a better product. AI interaction is the same process — faster, more patient, and available at midnight when you are working against a deadline — but the same fundamental dynamic of direction, review, and refinement.

Three practical principles anchor the conversational approach. First, give context before instruction: who you are, what this is for, what good looks like, what constraints apply. Second, react to output rather than just evaluating it — tell the AI what is wrong and why, not just that it is wrong. Third, build on what works rather than starting over — if 70% of the output is good, refine the 30% rather than regenerating from scratch.

## How to Apply

1. **Start with a context-rich opening, not a perfect prompt.** "I am a development consultant preparing a policy brief on youth unemployment for the Western Cape provincial government. The audience is senior officials who are technically literate but time-poor. The brief needs to be evidence-based, actionable, and no longer than four pages. I want to start with the problem statement section." This is not a prompt template — it is a briefing, the same kind you would give a colleague.

2. **Respond to output with specific, directional feedback.** Not: "This is not good enough." Instead: "The problem statement is too broad — narrow it to the specific demographic (18-25, not in education, employment, or training) and the specific geography (Cape Town metropolitan area, not all of Western Cape). Also, the tone is too alarmist — these officials respond better to measured, evidence-based framing." AI adjusts to specificity the way a good colleague does.

3. **Use follow-up questions as refinement tools.** "What evidence exists that contradicts the causal claim you made in paragraph two?" "If I had to cut this from four pages to two, which sections would you remove and why?" "What would a Treasury official object to in this brief?" These questions are not prompts — they are the same questions you would ask a colleague whose draft is 80% there.

4. **Save and reuse conversational openings, not prompt templates.** Build a library of context-setting openings for your recurring tasks. Not rigid templates but flexible briefings that you adapt to each project. Over time, these become faster to deploy and more refined — just as your briefing style for human colleagues improves with experience.

## Examples

**Situation:** Rae is drafting terms of reference for an external evaluation of a rural water supply programme. He opens AI and tries to write the perfect prompt. After fifteen minutes of prompt-crafting, he has not yet started the actual work.

**Application:** Rae abandons the prompt-engineering approach and starts a conversation instead. "I need to draft terms of reference for an external mid-term evaluation of a rural water supply programme in the Eastern Cape. The programme is funded by a bilateral donor, implemented through district municipalities, and is in its third year. I have done this many times — I know what good TORs look like. Help me draft this, starting with the background section."

The AI produces a competent background section. Rae responds: "Good structure, but you are describing a generic water programme. This programme has a specific feature: it uses community-based management committees rather than municipal operations. That changes the evaluation questions significantly — the key question is whether the management committees are functional and sustainable, not just whether the infrastructure was built. Revise with this in mind."

The AI revises. Rae continues: "Now draft the evaluation questions. I want five main questions, each with two to three sub-questions. The questions should cover relevance, effectiveness, efficiency, sustainability, and institutional arrangements. For sustainability, focus specifically on the management committee model and fiscal sustainability of operations and maintenance."

Two more rounds of iteration, and Rae has a solid draft TOR in forty-five minutes. Not because the first prompt was perfect, but because the conversation converged on what he actually needed through the same iterative process he would use with any colleague.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Spend more time crafting the prompt than you would spend briefing a human colleague on the same task.
**Why:** If you would brief a colleague in three sentences and expect them to come back with questions, do the same with AI. The conversational approach is fast because it front-loads direction and refines through iteration, rather than trying to encode everything into a single instruction.

**Don't:** Start over from scratch when AI output is partially good.
**Why:** Regenerating from zero discards the context and progress of the conversation. If 60% of the output is usable, build on it. "Keep sections 1 and 3 as they are. Rewrite section 2 with the following changes..." This is how you work with human colleagues, and it is how you should work with AI.
