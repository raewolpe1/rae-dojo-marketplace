# The Four Rules for Working With AI

## When to Use

- You are introducing someone (or yourself) to AI use and need a memorable, actionable starting framework.
- You are writing guidelines for how a team or organisation should approach AI in their work.
- You need a quick diagnostic checklist when an AI workflow is not producing good results.

## Core Concept

Mollick distils his practical framework into four rules that function as both adoption principles and daily operating heuristics. They are deceptively simple, but each encodes a deeper insight about how human-AI collaboration actually works.

**Rule 1: Always invite AI to the table.** Before starting any knowledge work task, ask: what happens if I bring AI into this? Not "should I" — the cost of asking is zero and the cost of not asking is unknown compounding opportunity loss. This rule fights the default inertia where people continue working as they always have because adopting AI feels like a separate project. It is not a separate project. It is a habit.

**Rule 2: Be the human in the loop.** AI generates; you verify, judge, and decide. This is not optional quality control — it is the structural architecture of effective human-AI work. AI hallucinates with confidence, confabulates references, and produces outputs that look indistinguishable from correct work even when they are wrong. The human in the loop is not a safety check bolted on at the end; it is the design requirement that makes the whole system work.

**Rule 3: Treat AI like a person, but tell it what kind of person.** This is Mollick's most counterintuitive rule. AI performs dramatically better when you assign it a role, a persona, and a context. "You are a senior M&E specialist with 15 years of experience in sub-Saharan Africa, reviewing a theory of change" produces fundamentally different (and better) output than a bare instruction. The "but tell it what kind of person" caveat matters: without it, the AI defaults to generic, often mediocre performance.

**Rule 4: Assume this is the worst AI you will ever use.** Current models are a floor, not a ceiling. Anything that seems like a fundamental limitation today may be a solved problem in twelve months. Build workflows that accommodate improvement. Do not lock yourself into workarounds for today's constraints — build modular systems where the AI component can be upgraded without redesigning the whole workflow.

For development consultants, these four rules translate directly into practice. Inviting AI to every task means it is present when you draft terms of reference, design evaluation questions, structure interview guides, and write policy briefs. Being the human in the loop means you never submit an AI-generated output without applying your contextual expertise. Telling AI what kind of person it is means you front-load context about your sector, your client, and your standards. And assuming improvement means you do not build your firm's processes around current limitations.

## How to Apply

1. **Make Rule 1 a default, not a decision.** Put a sticky note on your monitor or a recurring calendar reminder: "Have I invited AI to this task?" The goal is to make inclusion automatic and exclusion deliberate. When you exclude AI, know why — "this requires confidential client data I cannot share" is a reason; "I have always done it this way" is not.

2. **Operationalise Rule 2 with verification checklists.** For each type of output AI produces in your workflow, define what verification looks like. For a literature review: check three cited sources actually exist and say what AI claims. For an indicator framework: confirm indicators are measurable with available data. For a stakeholder map: verify against your actual knowledge of the institutional landscape.

3. **Build a role library for Rule 3.** Create a set of role descriptions you reuse across projects. "You are an experienced development economist reviewing a programme design for a bilateral donor." "You are a qualitative researcher designing an interview guide for community-level beneficiary assessment." "You are a public finance specialist analysing municipal budget data in South Africa." Reuse and refine these over time.

4. **Apply Rule 4 through quarterly re-testing.** Every three months, revisit three tasks where AI previously failed or underperformed. Test again with current models. Document what changed. Share findings with your team. The frontier moves faster than your assumptions about it.

## Examples

**Situation:** Rae's consulting team is developing internal guidelines for AI use across their M&E practice. The team ranges from AI-enthusiastic juniors to sceptical senior consultants.

**Application:** Rae structures the guidelines around the four rules, making each concrete for M&E work. Rule 1 becomes a mandatory checklist item in project kickoff meetings: "AI integration considered for: literature review, framework design, data analysis, report drafting, quality assurance." Rule 2 becomes a verification protocol: every AI-assisted output requires sign-off from a domain specialist, with specific checkpoints for factual accuracy, contextual appropriateness, and indicator feasibility. Rule 3 becomes a shared prompt library on the team's intranet, with tested role descriptions for common M&E tasks. Rule 4 becomes a quarterly "frontier review" meeting where team members share what AI can now do that it could not do last quarter. The sceptical seniors engage because the rules explicitly centre human judgment; the enthusiastic juniors engage because the rules explicitly encourage experimentation. The framework gives everyone a shared vocabulary without imposing a single way of working.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Apply the rules rigidly as a compliance checklist rather than as thinking habits.
**Why:** The rules are heuristics, not regulations. The point is to shift default behaviour — to make AI inclusion and human verification habitual — not to create bureaucratic overhead. If filling out a four-rule checklist takes longer than the task itself, you have missed the point.

**Don't:** Interpret Rule 4 as "do not invest in current workflows because everything will change."
**Why:** Temporal humility does not mean paralysis. Build workflows that work today and can be upgraded tomorrow. The rule says "assume improvement" — it does not say "assume obsolescence." Your workflow architecture (human-AI collaboration patterns, verification protocols, role libraries) will outlast any specific model.
