# Co-Intelligence: Working With AI, Not Delegating To It

## When to Use

- You are starting a new piece of knowledge work and want to integrate AI from the beginning rather than using it as an afterthought.
- You are struggling with the mental model — treating AI either as a magic oracle or a dumb autocomplete — and need a more productive frame.
- You want to redesign a recurring workflow (report writing, data analysis, stakeholder engagement) to be genuinely AI-collaborative.

## Core Concept

The central argument of Mollick's Co-Intelligence is a reframe: AI is not a tool you use, it is a co-worker with alien intelligence. This distinction matters because it changes everything about how you interact with it.

When you treat AI as a tool, you give it an instruction and evaluate the output. Pass or fail. When you treat it as a co-intelligence, you engage in a working relationship: you provide context, it generates, you react, it adjusts, you push back, it offers alternatives, and the final product is genuinely joint — neither purely yours nor purely the machine's.

For development consultants, this reframe is practically significant. A theory of change, a stakeholder analysis, or a programme evaluation framework is not a document you extract from a system. It is a thinking process. The value of AI as co-intelligence is not that it produces the document for you — it is that it produces a draft that makes your thinking visible to yourself. When you read AI's first pass at your theory of change and think "no, that causal pathway is wrong because..." — that reaction is the work. AI provoked it. You could not have had it staring at a blank page.

This is what Mollick means by co-intelligence: the AI contributes structural competence (it knows what a theory of change looks like, what indicators are standard, what frameworks exist in the literature), and you contribute contextual judgment (you know this specific programme, these specific stakeholders, this specific political economy). Neither is sufficient alone. Together, they produce work that is faster, more structurally complete, and more thoroughly interrogated than either could produce independently.

The practical implication is that you should never be "using AI" as a discrete step in your workflow. You should be working with AI as a continuous presence — the way you might work with a junior colleague sitting next to you, generating ideas, catching oversights, and drafting sections while you steer, correct, and decide.

## How to Apply

1. **Start every task by inviting AI in.** Before you open a blank document, open a conversation with AI. Give it context about who you are, what you are working on, and what good looks like. "You are helping me, a development consultant with 20 years of experience in Southern Africa, draft a mid-term evaluation framework for a youth livelihoods programme. The client is [description]. Good output looks like [description]."

2. **React, don't just evaluate.** When AI produces output, do not just score it pass/fail. Engage with it. "This indicator framework misses the demand-side factors entirely — what would you add if the key constraint is employer willingness to hire, not youth skills?" Your reactions sharpen both the output and your own thinking.

3. **Let AI challenge your assumptions.** Ask explicitly: "What assumptions am I making that might be wrong? What causal pathways am I not considering? What would a sceptical reviewer say about this theory of change?" AI is surprisingly good at generating challenges — not because it understands your context, but because it has pattern-matched across thousands of similar frameworks and can surface the standard critiques.

4. **Design for iteration, not extraction.** Do not try to get the perfect output in one prompt. Design a three-round workflow: Round 1 — AI generates a structural first draft. Round 2 — you annotate what is wrong, missing, or context-inappropriate, and AI revises. Round 3 — you and AI co-refine the final product together, section by section.

## Examples

**Situation:** Rae is developing an M&E framework for a municipal capacity-building programme. The programme aims to strengthen financial management in three district municipalities. He has the programme document, baseline data, and a tight deadline.

**Application:** Instead of building the M&E framework from scratch — which he has done dozens of times — Rae opens a conversation with AI, gives it the programme context and the role of "experienced M&E specialist familiar with South African local government," and asks for a first-draft logical framework with outcome indicators, means of verification, and data collection frequencies. The AI produces a competent structural draft within minutes. Rae immediately spots three problems: the AI has proposed indicators that require data the municipalities do not collect, it has missed the inter-governmental fiscal transfer dimension entirely, and the outcome indicators are too high-level for mid-term measurement. He feeds these reactions back. The AI adjusts, proposes alternative indicators, and adds the fiscal dimension. Rae refines further, adding context about the specific municipalities' capacity constraints. The final framework took 90 minutes instead of the two days it would have taken from a blank page — and it is more structurally thorough because the AI surfaced standard indicator categories Rae might have overlooked from habit.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Generate an AI output and submit it without substantive human engagement.
**Why:** This is delegation, not co-intelligence. The output will lack contextual judgment, miss local specifics, and potentially contain hallucinated references or indicators. The point is not to have AI do your work — it is to do your work better, together.

**Don't:** Use AI only at the end of a process to "polish" a document you have already written.
**Why:** This captures about 10% of the value. The largest gains come from AI collaboration at the beginning — when your thinking is most uncertain and a structural scaffold is most valuable. By the time you are polishing, the major decisions are already made.
