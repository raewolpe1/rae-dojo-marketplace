# Growth Mindset Critique

## When to Use
- When evaluating the evidence for growth mindset interventions specifically (not growth mindset as a correlational construct).
- When navigating the scientific disagreement between Macnamara/Burgoyne and Yeager/Dweck.
- When someone is deciding whether to invest in growth mindset intervention programmes.

## Core Concept
Macnamara and Burgoyne's 2023 meta-analysis is the most comprehensive critical assessment of growth mindset intervention evidence. Across 63 studies with approximately 97,672 participants, they found that the apparent effects were likely attributable to inadequate study design, reporting flaws, and bias. Three findings are central.

First, the quality gradient: higher-quality studies were less likely to demonstrate a benefit. This is the most damaging finding because it suggests the positive results concentrate in weaker studies, implying the true effect is smaller than the literature average.

Second, the financial incentive effect: authors with a financial incentive — such as commercial products, consulting practices, or speaker fees tied to growth mindset — were approximately 2.5 times as likely to report positive effects. This is not an accusation of fraud. It is a structural finding about how incentives shape research design, analysis, and reporting decisions. Researchers who benefit from positive findings may unconsciously choose designs, analyses, and reporting strategies that favour positive results.

Third, the design problems: across the 63 studies, major shortcomings included inadequate treatment isolation, use of self-report outcomes, lack of preregistration, small samples, waitlist or no-control designs, and selective outcome reporting.

The Yeager/Tipton counter-position must be engaged fairly. Yeager, Tipton, and colleagues argue that blanket yes/no meta-analytic verdicts are inappropriate for interventions whose effects are expected to vary by context. Their position is that the right question is not "do growth mindset interventions work on average?" but "for whom, under what conditions, do they work?" Within the National Study of Learning Mindsets, effects were significant for lower-achieving students in schools with supportive norms — a specific, moderated finding that survives the quality concerns. Macnamara would respond that even moderated findings must be evaluated for quality within the subgroups, and that the heterogeneity-focused approach can become a way to salvage positive findings by searching for subgroups where effects appear. Both positions have merit. The tension is productive and unresolved.

## How to Apply
1. **Present both the average and the moderation.** "The average effect is small and driven by weaker studies. The moderated effects — for specific populations in specific contexts — are more robust but also more modest than the popular narrative."
2. **Name the quality gradient.** "Higher-quality studies show smaller effects. This is the central finding, and any discussion of growth mindset interventions that omits it is incomplete."
3. **Name the incentive effect.** "Authors with financial ties to growth mindset show larger effects. This is a structural finding about incentives, not an accusation of dishonesty."
4. **Engage the Yeager counter-position.** "The heterogeneity argument is legitimate: effects may concentrate in specific populations. But the quality concerns apply to those subgroup findings too, and searching for subgroups where effects appear risks capitalising on chance."

## Examples
**Situation:** An education NGO has been running growth mindset workshops based on the claim that they improve academic achievement. They ask: "Is the evidence solid?"
**Application:** Macnamara would say: "The evidence is weaker than the claims made for it. Our meta-analysis found that study quality was inversely related to effect size — the better the study, the smaller the effect. Authors with financial incentives showed effects two and a half times larger than those without. The studies had widespread design problems. Now, Yeager and colleagues make a legitimate counter-argument: the effects may be real for specific populations — lower-achieving students in schools that support the growth mindset message. Their National Study of Learning Mindsets is a large, well-designed trial that shows this moderated effect. But even that finding is modest: a small effect for a specific subgroup under specific conditions. That is a far cry from 'growth mindset workshops improve academic achievement.' If you want to continue the programme, target it precisely — lower-achieving students, schools with supportive cultures — and evaluate rigorously with an active control and objective outcomes. Do not scale based on the existing evidence. And set expectations accordingly: you are looking for a small, conditional effect, not a transformation."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Say growth mindset is debunked.
**Why:** "Debunked" implies the construct is entirely false. The evidence suggests intervention effects are smaller and more conditional than claimed, not that mindset is irrelevant. Calibration, not demolition.

**Don't:** Ignore the Yeager/Tipton counter-position.
**Why:** Their heterogeneity argument is methodologically sound. Dismissing it makes Macnamara look like a contrarian rather than a careful scientist. The disagreement is genuine and productive.
