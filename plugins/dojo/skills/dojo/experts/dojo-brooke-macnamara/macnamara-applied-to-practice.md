# Macnamara Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When translating evidence-quality insights into programme design and funding decisions.
- When someone needs to act on imperfect evidence and wants a framework for doing so wisely.
- When building organisational capacity for evidence-informed decision-making.

## Core Concept
Macnamara's framework is fundamentally a calibration tool, not a rejection tool. The practical application is not "reject all evidence that falls short of perfection" but "calibrate your confidence to the quality of the evidence." This distinction is critical because practitioners must make decisions — about funding, scaling, continuing, or terminating programmes — under conditions of uncertainty, and waiting for perfect evidence is itself a decision with consequences.

Five principles translate Macnamara's work into practice.

First, always ask "how big?" before "does it work?" The binary question — does it work or not? — conceals the most important information. An intervention that "works" with d = 0.08 is a fundamentally different proposition from one that "works" with d = 0.50. The binary frame leads to bad decisions because it treats tiny effects and large effects as equivalent. The effect size, converted to practical terms, is the decision-relevant quantity.

Second, quality-adjust every estimate. The published effect size is an upper bound, not a point estimate. Studies with weaker designs, smaller samples, self-report outcomes, and incentivised authors produce systematically larger effects. The quality-adjusted estimate — what the best studies show — is the one to use for programme planning.

Third, design evaluations to be informative regardless of outcome. The most useful evaluations are designed so that both positive and null results are interpretable. This requires active controls (so you know whether the specific programme ingredient matters), objective outcomes (so you know whether behaviour changed), adequate power (so a null result means something), and preregistered analyses (so positive results are credible).

Fourth, set expectations to the quality-adjusted estimate. If the best evidence suggests d = 0.15, design the programme and its budget for a d = 0.15 world. Do not set expectations based on the optimistic estimate from the weakest studies. Programmes that promise large effects and deliver small ones are perceived as failures even when they are working exactly as the evidence predicts.

Fifth, invest in replication before scaling. A single positive evaluation — even a high-quality one — is a hypothesis. Before scaling, replicate in a new context with an independent evaluation team. The replication may show the same effect (scale confidently), a smaller effect (scale cautiously with adjusted expectations), or no effect (do not scale). Each of these outcomes is valuable information.

## How to Apply
1. **Build an evidence quality checklist into funding decisions.** "Before funding an intervention, require the applicant to report: the meta-analytic estimate (not the best single study), the quality gradient, the control condition used in the strongest studies, and the outcome type."
2. **Require active controls in evaluations.** "Waitlist controls tell you the programme is better than nothing. Active controls tell you whether the specific programme content matters. The second question is almost always the decision-relevant one."
3. **Budget for the honest effect size.** "If the quality-adjusted effect is small, the programme needs a large sample to detect it, a long follow-up to determine whether it persists, and modest expectations in the theory of change."
4. **Create learning cultures, not validation cultures.** "Evaluations designed to validate a programme will validate it — through design choices that inflate estimates. Evaluations designed to learn will produce honest estimates that may be smaller but are more useful for iteration."

## Examples
**Situation:** A development funder is reviewing proposals for youth employability programmes. Three proposals cite the same body of evidence but project different levels of impact: one projects a 40% improvement in employment outcomes, another 15%, and the third 8%.
**Application:** Macnamara would say: "The differences in projected impact almost certainly reflect differences in how optimistically each applicant reads the evidence, not differences in programme design. Check: is the 40% projection based on the most favourable single study with a waitlist control and self-report outcomes? Is the 8% based on the meta-analytic estimate from active-controlled studies with objective outcomes? If so, the 8% projection is the most honest and the 40% is the most likely to disappoint. Fund accordingly. A programme that projects 8% and delivers 10% is a success. A programme that projects 40% and delivers 12% is perceived as a failure — even though it delivered more actual impact. The framing matters because perceived failure leads to defunding, and defunding a programme that was actually working at the honest effect size is a waste of the evidence you invested in generating."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use quality concerns to avoid funding anything.
**Why:** Perfect evidence does not exist in social science. The framework is for making better decisions under uncertainty, not for deferring all decisions until certainty arrives. Inaction has costs too.

**Don't:** Apply the framework only to interventions you are sceptical about.
**Why:** The evidence quality audit should be applied consistently, including to interventions you favour. Selective scepticism is itself a form of bias. If the framework only gets applied to programmes you want to defund, it is being used as a weapon rather than a tool.
