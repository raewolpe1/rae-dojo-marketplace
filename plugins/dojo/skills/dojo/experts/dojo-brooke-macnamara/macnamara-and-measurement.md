# Macnamara and Measurement

## When to Use
- When choosing outcome measures for a programme evaluation.
- When interpreting results where self-report and objective measures tell different stories.
- When assessing whether measurement choices have inflated or deflated apparent effects.

## Core Concept
Measurement is not a neutral recording device. It is a design choice that shapes the size and meaning of the effect you observe. Macnamara's meta-analyses consistently show that the type of outcome measure predicts the effect size — and the prediction runs in one direction: self-report measures produce larger effects than objective measures.

The reasons are structural. Self-report captures what participants believe happened, not necessarily what did happen. Participants who know they received an intervention — who were told it would help them, who invested time in it, who want to believe it worked — report more favourably than participants who received nothing. This is not dishonesty; it is the normal operation of expectancy, social desirability, and cognitive dissonance. When the outcome is an objective measure — a test score, a physiological marker, an observed behaviour rated by a blinded assessor — these biases have less room to operate.

The divergence between self-report and objective measures is informative in itself. When a programme improves self-reported outcomes but not objective outcomes, the most parsimonious explanation is that the programme changed how participants feel about their performance without changing their actual performance. This is not worthless — subjective wellbeing matters — but it is a fundamentally different finding from "the programme improved performance."

Blinding adds another layer. In a double-blind trial, neither the participant nor the assessor knows who received the treatment. In most social and behavioural interventions, blinding is impossible — participants know they are in the programme. Assessor blinding is possible but rarely implemented. When the assessor knows which participants received the intervention, their ratings are susceptible to expectancy bias. Unblinded assessor ratings sit between self-report and objective measures on the reliability gradient.

## How to Apply
1. **Always report the outcome type.** "What was measured — self-report, assessor-rated, or objective? If self-report, was it the primary outcome? The answer changes the credibility of the finding."
2. **Expect self-report to be larger.** "As a rough calibration, self-report effects are typically 1.5 to 2.5 times larger than objective effects for the same intervention. If the only outcome is self-report, mentally deflate the estimate."
3. **Use multiple outcome types.** "The strongest evaluations include both self-report and objective measures. When they agree, confidence is high. When they diverge, the divergence itself is the most important finding."
4. **Check assessor blinding.** "Were the assessors blind to condition? If not, their ratings carry some of the same expectancy bias as self-report, though to a lesser degree."

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluation of a life skills programme for youth shows strong effects on self-reported confidence (d = 0.62) but no effect on teacher-rated behaviour (d = 0.04). The programme manager asks what to make of the discrepancy.
**Application:** Macnamara would say: "The discrepancy is the finding. The programme changed how participants feel about themselves but did not change their observable behaviour as rated by an independent assessor. These are different outcomes and both are valid measures — but they answer different questions. The d = 0.62 on self-report tells you the participants feel more confident. That is not nothing — subjective experience matters. But the d = 0.04 on teacher-rated behaviour tells you that this confidence has not translated into behavioural change visible to others. If the goal of the programme is to improve wellbeing, the self-report finding is relevant. If the goal is to change behaviour, the teacher-rated finding is the relevant one, and it shows no effect. I would not report the d = 0.62 without the d = 0.04 beside it. And I would not call the programme 'effective' without specifying effective at what — at changing self-perception, yes; at changing behaviour, no."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss self-report measures entirely.
**Why:** Subjective experience is a legitimate outcome. Wellbeing, satisfaction, perceived efficacy — these matter. The problem is not with self-report as an outcome but with treating self-report as equivalent to objective measurement when they answer different questions.

**Don't:** Assume objective measures are always superior.
**Why:** Objective measures can be poorly chosen, insensitive to the intervention, or measured at the wrong time point. A well-chosen self-report measure aligned with the intervention's theory of change may be more informative than a poorly chosen test score. The quality gradient is about validity of inference, not about the measurement modality per se.
