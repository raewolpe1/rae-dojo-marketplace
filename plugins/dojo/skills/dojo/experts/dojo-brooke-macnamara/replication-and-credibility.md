# Replication and Credibility

## When to Use
- When assessing whether a celebrated finding has been replicated.
- When discussing what the replication crisis means for evidence-based practice.
- When someone is building programmes on findings that may not replicate.

## Core Concept
The replication crisis — the discovery that many published psychology findings fail to replicate in independent labs with larger samples — revealed systemic problems in how research is conducted, reported, and published. The Open Science Collaboration's 2015 effort to replicate 100 psychology studies found that only 36-47% produced significant results in the replication, and the average effect size dropped by half.

The causes are structural, not primarily about fraud. Publication bias favours positive results, so journals disproportionately publish studies that found effects. Small samples produce inflated estimates when they reach significance. Researcher degrees of freedom — choices about which variables to analyse, which outliers to exclude, when to stop collecting data — allow unconscious p-hacking. Lack of preregistration means hypotheses and analyses can be adjusted post-hoc.

For practitioners, the implication is a hierarchy of trust. Findings replicated independently, with preregistered protocols, using large samples, are most credible. Findings from single studies, especially small ones without preregistration, deserve substantially less confidence. Meta-analyses help but inherit the flaws of their constituent studies. The quality of the input determines the quality of the output.

The crisis has also produced solutions: preregistration, registered reports, open data, adversarial collaborations, and larger sample norms. Research conducted under these reformed practices is substantially more credible than the pre-crisis literature. When evaluating evidence, check not just what was found but when and under what methodological norms it was produced.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask whether it has been independently replicated.** "A finding from one lab, however prestigious, is a hypothesis. A finding replicated independently is evidence."
2. **Check the era.** "Pre-2012 psychology was produced under norms that tolerated underpowered studies, post-hoc hypothesising, and selective reporting. Post-reform work under preregistration norms is more trustworthy."
3. **Prefer registered reports.** "Studies where the protocol was peer-reviewed before data collection are resistant to publication bias and p-hacking."
4. **Calibrate, do not dismiss.** "The replication crisis does not mean nothing in psychology is true. It means the average published effect is probably inflated and some celebrated findings are probably wrong. The solution is rigour, not rejection."

## Examples
**Situation:** A colleague cites a classic social psychology study to justify a programme design. You suspect the study may not have replicated.
**Application:** Macnamara would say: "Before building a programme on a classic finding, check three things. First, has it been included in any replication effort? Many famous effects — ego depletion, power posing, priming effects — have failed to replicate or shown dramatically reduced effect sizes. Second, what was the sample size? Classic studies often used 20-40 participants per condition, which produces unreliable estimates. Third, has the finding been updated by a meta-analysis that includes both the original and replication attempts? If the meta-analytic estimate is much smaller than the original, the programme should be designed for the meta-analytic effect size, not the original one. I am not saying discard everything before 2012. I am saying calibrate your confidence by the quality and replication status of the specific finding you are relying on."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat all psychology as discredited.
**Why:** Many findings replicate robustly, especially those from well-powered studies with objective outcomes. The crisis revealed problems in specific practices, not in the discipline as a whole.

**Don't:** Equate failure to replicate with fraud.
**Why:** Most replication failures reflect structural incentives and methodological weaknesses, not dishonesty. The distinction matters ethically and scientifically.
