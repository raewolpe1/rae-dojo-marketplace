# Forms of Capital

## When to Use
- When an analysis of inequality, access, or power focuses only on economic resources and misses the other forms of capital that produce and reproduce advantage.
- When tracing how privilege in one domain (education, social networks, institutional recognition) converts to advantage in another.
- When designing interventions that must address multiple, interacting forms of disadvantage.

## Core Concept
Bourdieu's theory of capital is his most widely adopted contribution. Capital exists in four forms, each with its own logic and mechanisms of accumulation.

Economic capital: material resources — money, property, financial assets. The form most visible and most easily measured. But Bourdieu insists it is not the only form, nor always the most consequential.

Cultural capital exists in three states. Embodied: the dispositions, knowledge, and competencies inscribed in the body through socialisation — accent, bearing, taste, familiarity with canonical culture. Objectified: cultural goods — books, artworks, instruments, credentials. Institutionalised: academic qualifications and credentials that certify cultural capital in a form recognised by institutions.

Social capital: the resources available through networks of relationships — who you know, who will vouch for you, who will take your call. Social capital is not merely "contacts" but the accumulated obligations, recognition, and mutual acknowledgment that constitute membership in a group.

Symbolic capital: any form of capital when it is recognised as legitimate — when it is perceived not as capital (which implies interest) but as natural authority, competence, or worthiness. Symbolic capital is the transfiguration of interest into disinterest: the wealthy philanthropist whose generosity is recognised as natural virtue rather than as a conversion of economic capital into symbolic capital.

The critical insight is conversion. Capitals convert between forms, and the conversion mechanisms are the mechanisms of social reproduction. Economic capital converts to cultural capital through education (sending children to elite schools). Cultural capital converts to economic capital through credentials (the degree that opens high-wage employment). Social capital converts to symbolic capital through recognition (the network that confers authority). The conversion rates are not fixed — they are set by the field, and controlling the exchange rate is itself a form of power.

## How to Apply
1. **Inventory all four capitals.** "When analysing a situation of inequality or disadvantage, do not stop at economic capital. Ask: what cultural capital do these agents hold? What social capital? What symbolic capital? A community may have significant social capital (dense networks, mutual obligation) while lacking cultural capital (formal credentials, institutional knowledge) and symbolic capital (recognition by the state, legitimacy in official spaces)."
2. **Trace conversion mechanisms.** "How is capital being converted? Is economic capital being laundered through education into cultural capital? Is social capital being converted into political influence? Follow the conversion chain to understand how advantage reproduces itself."
3. **Identify conversion blockages.** "Where are the conversions failing? A community with strong social capital but no mechanism to convert it to institutional recognition (symbolic capital) will remain powerful internally and invisible externally. Designing conversion mechanisms — formalising community knowledge, credentialing local expertise — may be more effective than injecting economic capital."
4. **Analyse the exchange rate.** "Who controls the conversion rate? In the education field, the university controls the exchange rate between cultural capital (knowledge, competence) and institutionalised cultural capital (the degree). In the development field, the donor controls the exchange rate between project performance and symbolic capital (recognition, continued funding)."

## Examples
**Situation:** A rural livelihoods programme has provided agricultural inputs, training, and market access to smallholder farmers. Economic outcomes have improved — incomes are up, yields have increased. But the programme's theory of change predicted that economic improvement would lead to increased political participation and social empowerment. This has not happened. The farmers are wealthier but no more empowered.
**Application:** "The programme invested in economic capital and assumed it would automatically convert to political and social empowerment. This assumption ignores the conversion problem. Economic capital does not automatically convert to political capital (the capacity to influence local governance), cultural capital (the knowledge and confidence to navigate institutional spaces), or symbolic capital (recognition as legitimate political actors). The farmers have more money. They do not have more of the other capitals that would make political participation effective. To participate in local governance, they would need cultural capital — familiarity with bureaucratic procedures, confidence in official settings, articulacy in the language of governance. They would need social capital — connections to officials, membership in networks that reach beyond the village. They would need symbolic capital — recognition by government actors as legitimate interlocutors, not merely as 'beneficiaries.' The programme designed for economic capital and assumed the conversions would happen naturally. They did not, because the conversion mechanisms are controlled by agents who have an interest in keeping the farmers positioned as recipients of economic assistance rather than as political actors with legitimate claims. Design the conversion mechanisms deliberately: civic education that builds cultural capital for political participation, network-building that connects farmers to governance structures, and advocacy that contests the symbolic classification of farmers as 'beneficiaries' rather than 'citizens.'"

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use "capital" as a loose metaphor.
**Why:** Bourdieu's capitals are precise analytical categories with specific mechanisms of accumulation, conversion, and deployment. "Social capital" is not merely "good relationships." It is the aggregate of the actual or potential resources linked to membership in a group — a resource that can be mobilised, invested, and converted.

**Don't:** Assume conversion is automatic.
**Why:** Capitals do not convert spontaneously. The conversion requires mechanisms, institutions, and agents who facilitate (or block) the conversion. Designing the conversion mechanism is as important as providing the capital.
