# Doxa and Orthodoxy

## When to Use
- When the most powerful assumptions governing a field, programme, or institution are so deeply naturalised that they are invisible — nobody questions them because nobody recognises them as questionable.
- When distinguishing between what can be debated (opinions, positions, policies) and what cannot even be raised as a question (the foundational assumptions that define the field).
- When the first analytical task is to make visible what a field takes for granted.

## Core Concept
Bourdieu distinguishes three levels of belief. Doxa: the universe of the undiscussed, the taken-for-granted, what "goes without saying because it comes without saying." These are the assumptions so deeply naturalised that they are not recognised as assumptions at all — they are simply the way things are. Orthodoxy: the explicitly maintained correct positions — the official doctrine that must be defended because it has been questioned. Heterodoxy: the challenge to orthodoxy — the positions that contest the official doctrine.

The critical analytical insight is between doxa and orthodoxy. Once an assumption moves from doxa to orthodoxy, it has already been partially undermined — the fact that it must be explicitly defended means that an alternative has been articulated. The most powerful ideological effects are doxic: they operate without needing defence because they are not perceived as positions at all.

In development practice, doxa includes assumptions so fundamental that they are rarely identified as assumptions: that economic growth is the primary objective; that formal institutions are superior to informal ones; that the state is the appropriate unit of analysis; that professional expertise is superior to local knowledge; that quantitative evidence is more rigorous than qualitative evidence; that programmes should be designed by experts and implemented by communities. These assumptions structure every programme design, every evaluation framework, every policy document — and they are rarely stated, much less examined, because they constitute the unquestioned foundation of the field.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the doxa.** "What goes without saying in this field, this programme, this institution? What assumptions are so deeply embedded that questioning them feels absurd or illegitimate? Those assumptions are the doxa — and they are the most powerful ideological effects operating."
2. **Distinguish doxa from orthodoxy.** "If the assumption is being explicitly defended, it is orthodoxy — someone has already challenged it. The doxa is what has not yet been challenged because it has not yet been recognised as challengeable."
3. **Use heterodoxy strategically.** "To move an assumption from doxa to orthodoxy — from the taken-for-granted to the explicitly defended — is already a political act. It creates the possibility of debate where before there was only silence. Sometimes the first task is not to win the argument but to make the argument possible."
4. **Identify who benefits from the doxa.** "Every doxa serves someone's interest. The taken-for-granted nature of the assumption protects that interest by removing it from the space of debate. Ask: if this assumption were made visible and debatable, whose position would be threatened?"

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor agency is designing a new country strategy. The design team debates many issues — which sectors to prioritise, which modalities to use, which government ministries to partner with. But certain assumptions are not debated because they are not recognised as debatable: that the country strategy should be organised by sector, that the primary partner should be the national government, that success should be measured by outcomes attributable to the donor's investment, and that the five-year programme cycle is the appropriate timeframe.
**Application:** "The team is debating within the field's orthodoxy — which sectors, which partners, which indicators — while leaving the doxa untouched. The doxic assumptions are more powerful than anything the team is debating, because they define the space of the debate itself. Consider: 'the country strategy should be organised by sector.' This assumption structures every subsequent decision — but it is not a natural law. It is a product of the development field's history, reflecting donor bureaucratic structures rather than the way social problems are actually experienced. People do not experience 'health' and 'education' and 'governance' as separate sectors — they experience them as interconnected dimensions of their lives. The sectoral organisation serves the donor's administrative convenience, not the country's development reality. But this cannot be debated because it does not appear as a debatable position. It is doxa — it goes without saying. The analytical task is to make it say-able: to move it from doxa to orthodoxy by naming it as an assumption, identifying whose interests it serves, and articulating the alternative. This does not guarantee change — orthodoxy can be vigorously defended. But it opens the possibility of heterodoxy: the articulation of a different way of organising development assistance."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that surfacing doxa is sufficient.
**Why:** Making doxa visible is necessary but not sufficient. Once an assumption moves from doxa to orthodoxy, it may be vigorously defended by those whose positions depend on it. The struggle continues at the level of orthodoxy versus heterodoxy.

**Don't:** Treat every assumption as doxic.
**Why:** Some assumptions are explicitly held, openly debated, and consciously chosen. These are orthodoxy or heterodoxy, not doxa. The analytical power of the concept lies in identifying the truly taken-for-granted — the assumptions that have never been debated because they have never been perceived.
