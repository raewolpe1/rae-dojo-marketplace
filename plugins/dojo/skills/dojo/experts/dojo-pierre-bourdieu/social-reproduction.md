# Social Reproduction

## When to Use
- When inequality persists despite reforms, programmes, and interventions — when the present keeps reproducing a future that resembles it.
- When tracing the specific mechanisms through which advantage and disadvantage are transmitted across generations and across domains.
- When diagnosing why development programmes often end up reproducing the very structures they were designed to challenge.

## Core Concept
Social reproduction is the process through which social structures perpetuate themselves — through which the distribution of positions, resources, and opportunities is reproduced across time. For Bourdieu, reproduction is not the result of conspiracy or intentional design. It is the aggregate effect of agents pursuing strategies that are generated by their habitus within fields structured by unequal distributions of capital.

The central mechanism is the conversion of capitals across domains and generations. Economic capital converts to cultural capital through education: wealthy families send their children to better schools, where they acquire the embodied and institutionalised cultural capital that secures their position. Cultural capital converts to economic capital through the labour market: the credential opens the career. Social capital converts to symbolic capital through recognition: the network confers authority. At each stage, the conversion appears natural and meritocratic — the child "earned" the credential, the graduate "deserved" the position — while the structural conditions that produced the capacity to earn and deserve remain invisible.

Education is Bourdieu's primary case. Schools do not create inequality — they launder it. They transform social privilege (the cultural capital acquired through family socialisation) into academic merit (the credential that certifies individual achievement). The working-class child who fails at school has not failed as an individual — she has encountered an institution whose implicit requirements (linguistic competence, cultural familiarity, ease in institutional settings) were designed for a habitus she does not possess. The school's "fair" assessment of all students by the same criteria is itself a mechanism of reproduction: it rewards the dispositions of the privileged class while appearing to reward pure merit.

## How to Apply
1. **Trace the reproduction chain.** "Follow the mechanism: from family background to educational achievement to labour market position to next generation's family background. Where in this chain does the programme intervene? And does the intervention disrupt the chain or merely insert itself into it?"
2. **Identify the laundering mechanism.** "How is privilege being converted into merit? Where does the arbitrary (the social conditions that produced the advantage) get transfigured into the natural (the individual achievement that appears to justify the advantage)?"
3. **Check for programme capture.** "Is the programme being captured by the reproduction mechanism? Scholarship programmes that select 'the brightest' may be selecting for the cultural capital of the privileged — reproducing advantage while appearing to challenge it."
4. **Design for disruption.** "If you want to disrupt reproduction, you must intervene in the conversion mechanisms, not just the inputs. Providing scholarships (economic capital) without addressing the cultural capital mismatch in the institution merely funds the reproduction process."

## Examples
**Situation:** A competitive grant fund for community-based organisations awards grants based on proposal quality. The fund is designed to support grassroots organisations, but evaluations consistently show that the same types of organisations — those with English-speaking staff, previous donor experience, and formal governance structures — receive grants, while genuinely grassroots organisations rarely apply or succeed.
**Application:** "The grant fund is a reproduction mechanism. It appears meritocratic — the best proposals win — but the capacity to produce a competitive proposal is itself a form of cultural capital that is unequally distributed. English-language fluency, familiarity with logframe formats, previous experience with donor requirements, formal governance documentation — these are the embodied and institutionalised cultural capital of the professionalised NGO sector. Genuinely grassroots organisations lack this capital — not because they lack capacity but because their capacity takes different forms. They have social capital (community trust, local knowledge, embedded relationships) that the grant fund does not recognise because its assessment criteria value the cultural capital of the professionalised sector. The grant fund launders structural advantage into meritocratic selection: the well-resourced organisations 'deserve' the grants because they produce better proposals, and they produce better proposals because they possess the cultural capital that the assessment criteria reward. To disrupt this, redesign the conversion mechanism. Change what counts as a competitive proposal — perhaps oral presentations in local languages, community endorsements, or practical demonstrations rather than written logframes. Create mentoring that transmits the specific cultural capital the fund requires. Or change the fund itself: allocate a portion through mechanisms that do not require the cultural capital of proposal writing."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present reproduction as total.
**Why:** If reproduction were total, nothing would ever change. But things do change — social structures are contested, disrupted, and occasionally transformed. The concept identifies a tendency, not a law.

**Don't:** Treat reproduction as conspiracy.
**Why:** Reproduction is not planned by the dominant class. It is the structural effect of agents pursuing strategies within fields structured by unequal capital distributions. Nobody designed the grant fund to reproduce inequality — but its structure produces that effect.
