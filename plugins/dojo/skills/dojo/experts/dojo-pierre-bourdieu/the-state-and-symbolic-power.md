# The State and Symbolic Power

## When to Use
- When the state's power to classify, categorise, and define the official version of social reality is shaping development outcomes.
- When official categories — citizen/non-citizen, formal/informal, urban/rural, employed/unemployed — are being treated as neutral descriptions rather than as exercises of symbolic power.
- When development programmes rely on state classifications that themselves produce the problems the programmes aim to address.

## Core Concept
Bourdieu defines the state as the holder of the monopoly of legitimate physical and symbolic violence. The emphasis on symbolic violence is his distinctive contribution. The state does not merely repress; it classifies. It defines who is a citizen and who is not, what is legal and what is illegal, what is formal and what is informal, who is employed and who is unemployed, what counts as a family and what does not. These classifications are not neutral descriptions of a pre-existing reality — they are acts of symbolic power that produce the reality they claim to describe.

The census, the identity document, the land title, the business licence, the poverty assessment — all are state acts of classification that sort populations into categories with profound material consequences. To be classified as "informal" is to be placed outside the state's framework of recognition, protection, and support. To be classified as "unemployed" is to be measured against a model of employment that may not correspond to the reality of how people actually sustain their livelihoods. To be classified as "poor" by a particular measure is to be subjected to a particular regime of assistance — or excluded from it.

The state's classificatory power is particularly consequential for development because development programmes typically work through state categories. They target "the poor" as defined by government poverty lines, serve "citizens" as defined by state documentation, and operate in the "formal" sector as defined by state regulation. Populations that fall outside these categories — the undocumented, the informally employed, those whose poverty takes forms the state measure does not capture — are rendered invisible not by accident but by the structure of the state's classificatory apparatus.

## How to Apply
1. **Analyse the classification, not just the classified.** "Before accepting 'the informal sector' as a natural category, ask: who created this classification? What definition of 'formal' does it presuppose? Whose interests does the formal/informal distinction serve? The category itself is an exercise of state power."
2. **Trace the material effects of classification.** "Being classified as 'informal' means: no access to formal credit, no legal protection of property rights, no inclusion in social protection schemes, no representation in official statistics. The classification produces the marginalisation it appears merely to describe."
3. **Identify classification struggles.** "Who is contesting the state's categories? When communities refuse to be classified as 'beneficiaries' or 'informal settlers,' they are contesting the state's symbolic power — the power to define who and what they are."
4. **Design beyond state categories.** "If the state's categories exclude the people the programme is meant to serve, do not accept the categories. Design targeting mechanisms that reach beyond the state's classificatory apparatus — community-based identification, self-targeting, universal approaches that do not require classification."

## Examples
**Situation:** A national housing programme targets "informal settlements" for upgrading. The state's definition of "informal" requires that settlements lack formal tenure documentation. Communities that have customary tenure arrangements — recognised locally but not by the state's land administration system — are classified as "informal" and subjected to a tenure formalisation process that replaces customary arrangements with individual title deeds.
**Application:** "The state's classification of these communities as 'informal' is an act of symbolic power, not a neutral description. The communities have tenure — customary, community-recognised, locally legitimate. The state's classificatory apparatus cannot see this tenure because it defines 'formal' as 'documented in the state's land registry.' The classification produces informality by defining formality in terms that exclude existing arrangements. The housing programme then 'solves' the informality it created by imposing individual title deeds — which replace a communal tenure system with the state's preferred model. This is not upgrading; it is the exercise of the state's monopoly of legitimate classification — the power to define what counts as real tenure. The programme should ask: what tenure systems already exist? How do they function? For whom? The answer may reveal that the community's 'informal' arrangements provide security, flexibility, and social protection that the state's 'formal' title deeds do not. The classification 'informal' is not a description of deficiency — it is the state's failure to recognise what exists."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce the state to repression.
**Why:** The state's most powerful effects operate through classification, not coercion. The census is more consequential than the police for most people's daily experience of state power.

**Don't:** Treat state categories as inevitable.
**Why:** State categories are historical products of specific political processes. They can be contested, reformed, and redesigned. The current classification is not the only possible one.
