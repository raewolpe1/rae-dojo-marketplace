# Habitus

## When to Use
- When a programme or reform has changed formal rules, structures, or incentives but practices have not changed — people continue doing what they did before.
- When behaviour appears "irrational" from a policy perspective but makes perfect sense when understood as the product of embodied dispositions.
- When diagnosing the gap between what people are supposed to do (according to the programme design) and what they actually do.

## Core Concept
Habitus is Bourdieu's central concept: the system of durable, transposable dispositions acquired through socialisation that generate and organise practices and representations. Habitus is not habit — it is the generative principle that produces practice. It is the embodied sense of what is possible, appropriate, and natural for someone in a particular social position.

Habitus operates below conscious deliberation. It is not a set of rules that agents follow but a practical sense — a feel for the game — that generates appropriate responses to situations without the agent consciously calculating. The middle-class student who "naturally" knows how to address a professor, structure an argument, and navigate institutional bureaucracy is not following rules — she is deploying a habitus acquired through years of socialisation in a family and social environment where these dispositions are the water one swims in.

Critically, habitus is durable — it outlasts the conditions that produced it. The dispositions acquired through poverty persist even when economic conditions change. The deference learned through generations of subordination persists even when formal equality is declared. This is the hysteresis effect: the lag between changed conditions and unchanged dispositions. It explains why development programmes that change the formal environment often fail to change behaviour — because the habitus that generates behaviour was formed under previous conditions and does not automatically update.

Habitus is also transposable — dispositions learned in one domain transfer to others. The deference learned in the family transfers to the school, the workplace, the political forum. This is why interventions in one domain often fail to produce change: the habitus that generates the targeted behaviour was formed across all domains of social life, and changing one domain does not change the habitus.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose the habitus-field mismatch.** "The programme changed the field (new rules, new structures, new incentives). But the habitus has not changed. The participants bring dispositions formed under previous conditions. The mismatch between old habitus and new field explains why the programme is not producing the expected behaviour."
2. **Identify the generating dispositions.** "What do people do without thinking? What feels natural, obvious, 'the way things are done'? These taken-for-granted practices are products of habitus — and they will persist despite formal reform unless the habitus itself is engaged."
3. **Trace the formation history.** "Where was this habitus formed? Through what socialisation processes — family, school, community, work — were these dispositions acquired? Understanding the formation history reveals why the dispositions are so durable."
4. **Design for habitus, not just rules.** "If you want to change practice, you must engage the habitus — not just the rules. This means long-term, immersive interventions that reshape embodied dispositions, not short-term training that transmits information without transforming practice."

## Examples
**Situation:** A public financial management reform in a national government has introduced new procurement procedures, new reporting requirements, and a new IT system. Training has been provided to all staff. Two years later, an audit finds that the new procedures are being followed formally — the paperwork is completed, the reports are filed — but actual procurement decisions continue to be made through the old informal networks. Officials use the new system to document decisions that were actually made through the old system.
**Application:** "The reform changed the field: new rules, new procedures, new technology. It did not change the habitus. The officials bring to the new system dispositions formed over decades: the practical sense of who to call, which relationships to maintain, how to navigate the informal networks that actually determine who gets contracts. The new IT system captures what the officials want it to capture — the formal record — while the old habitus continues to generate the actual practice. The training transmitted information about the new rules. It did not transform the embodied dispositions that generate procurement practice. The officials know the new rules — they can recite them, complete the forms, generate the reports. But their habitus — the practical sense of how procurement actually works — was formed under the old system and operates below the level of the new rules. The reform produced compliance without change. To engage the habitus, you would need something far more intensive: sustained mentoring, institutional restructuring that disrupts the old networks, new incentive structures that make the old practices genuinely costly, and time — because habitus changes slowly, through repeated practice in new conditions, not through training that transmits rules."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat habitus as deterministic.
**Why:** Habitus is generative, not mechanical. It produces improvised responses, not predetermined ones. The agent is not a puppet of their habitus — but their improvisations operate within the range of possibilities that the habitus makes conceivable.

**Don't:** Confuse habitus with culture or mindset.
**Why:** "Culture" and "mindset" are vague, voluntaristic categories that suggest dispositions can be changed by changing attitudes. Habitus is embodied, durable, and structural — it is inscribed in the body, not merely in the mind.
