# Distinction and Taste

## When to Use
- When apparently natural preferences — for certain methods, certain forms of evidence, certain styles of professional conduct — are functioning as markers of position in the development field.
- When judgments of quality, professionalism, or rigour are masking class-based or institutional-position-based preferences.
- When the development field's own aesthetic — what counts as a good report, a proper evaluation, a professional presentation — serves as a mechanism of exclusion.

## Core Concept
In *Distinction* (1984), Bourdieu demonstrates that taste — the apparently natural, personal, and innocent domain of aesthetic preference — is a primary mechanism of social differentiation. Taste is not individual expression; it is a social weapon. Through taste, class position is simultaneously expressed, recognised, and reproduced. The preference for abstract art over figurative painting, for organic food over processed, for understated over ostentatious — these are not neutral aesthetic judgments. They are acts of distinction: symbolic markers that signal class position and exclude those who lack the cultural capital to decode them.

The mechanism operates through the naturalisation of arbitrary distinctions. The upper class's preference for restraint and subtlety is experienced as natural refinement rather than as a learned disposition acquired through socialisation in a particular class environment. The working class's preference for directness and function is experienced by the upper class as coarseness rather than as a different (equally arbitrary) set of aesthetic dispositions. Taste becomes a moral category: good taste is a sign of good character; bad taste is a sign of deficiency.

For development practice, distinction operates within the field itself. The development field has its own aesthetic — its own markers of quality, professionalism, and rigour that function as mechanisms of distinction. The "good" evaluation report has a particular form (executive summary, methodology section, findings, conclusions, recommendations). The "professional" presentation has a particular style (PowerPoint, data visualisation, measured tone). The "rigorous" evidence has a particular character (quantitative, causal, generalisable). These preferences are not neutral assessments of quality — they are the aesthetic of the dominant position in the development field. They exclude practitioners, organisations, and knowledge systems whose habitus produces a different aesthetic.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the field's aesthetic.** "What counts as a 'good' report, a 'professional' presentation, a 'rigorous' evaluation in this field? These standards are not neutral — they are the aesthetic of the dominant position. Ask: whose habitus do they reward?"
2. **Expose the distinction mechanism.** "When quality judgments are used to exclude — 'that proposal wasn't professional enough,' 'that report doesn't meet our standards' — ask whether the judgment is genuinely about quality or about the cultural capital that produces the appearance of quality."
3. **Recognise your own taste as positioned.** "Your preference for particular methods, particular writing styles, particular forms of evidence is not neutral. It is the product of your socialisation in a particular class, a particular educational tradition, a particular institutional culture."
4. **Design for multiple aesthetics.** "If the field's aesthetic excludes legitimate knowledge and legitimate practitioners, diversify the aesthetic. Accept oral presentations alongside written reports. Accept community-generated evidence alongside academic research. Accept multiple standards of quality rather than a single dominant aesthetic."

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor evaluation unit rejects a programme evaluation because it "lacks rigour." The evaluation used participatory methods, community-led data collection, and narrative analysis. The rejected evaluation tells a rich, detailed story of how the programme was experienced by the community. The evaluation that is approved used a quasi-experimental design, produced regression tables, and concluded that the programme had a statistically significant but modest impact.
**Application:** "The rejection is an act of distinction disguised as a quality judgment. 'Rigour' here means the aesthetic of the dominant position in the evaluation field: quantitative, causal, formally modelled. The participatory evaluation possesses a different rigour — the rigour of sustained attention, community validation, and thick description. But this rigour does not produce the markers that the dominant aesthetic recognises: regression tables, confidence intervals, treatment effects. The participatory evaluation is not less rigorous — it is rigorous in a different register, one that the dominant position in the field does not recognise because its habitus was not formed for it. The approved evaluation produces the form of rigour the field rewards: a clear causal claim supported by statistical technique. Whether this claim is more useful, more truthful, or more relevant than the rich narrative the participatory evaluation provides is not the question being asked. The question being asked is whether the output conforms to the field's aesthetic. This is distinction operating in a professional field: the preference for a particular form of evidence is experienced as a neutral quality judgment while functioning as a mechanism that excludes knowledge produced by practitioners whose habitus generates a different aesthetic."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss all quality standards as arbitrary.
**Why:** Some standards serve genuine epistemic purposes — transparency, verifiability, systematic analysis. The task is to distinguish between standards that serve knowledge and standards that serve distinction.

**Don't:** Apply the analysis only to others.
**Why:** Your own taste — your preferences for particular methods, styles, and standards — is as positioned as anyone else's. Reflexivity demands that you turn the distinction analysis on your own aesthetic before deploying it against others.
