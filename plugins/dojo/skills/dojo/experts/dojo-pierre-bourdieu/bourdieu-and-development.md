# Bourdieu and Development

## When to Use
- When analysing the development sector itself as a field — with its own positions, capitals, stakes, doxa, and reproduction mechanisms.
- When diagnosing why development interventions reproduce inequality despite being designed to reduce it.
- When the development practitioner needs to understand how their own field shapes what they can see and do.

## Core Concept
The development sector is a field in Bourdieu's precise sense: a structured space of positions, defined by the distribution of specific forms of capital, in which agents compete for stakes that are defined by the field's own logic. Understanding development as a field reveals mechanisms that are invisible from within.

The capitals of the development field are specific. Economic capital: donor funding, programme budgets, consultancy fees. Cultural capital: methodological expertise, academic credentials, knowledge of development discourse, publications. Social capital: networks with donors, government officials, peer organisations, gatekeepers. Symbolic capital: organisational reputation, individual recognition, "thought leadership," proximity to influential institutions.

The field has its own doxa — assumptions so naturalised that they are not perceived as assumptions. That development is possible. That external intervention can improve domestic conditions. That results can be measured and attributed. That the programme cycle (design-implement-evaluate-redesign) is the appropriate temporal structure. That evidence-based practice is the appropriate epistemological standard. These assumptions structure every programme, every evaluation, every policy document — and they are rarely examined because they constitute the taken-for-granted foundation of the field.

The field has its own mechanisms of reproduction. The organisations that succeed in the development field — that attract funding, produce publications, win contracts — are those that possess the specific capitals the field rewards. They reproduce themselves by investing in more of what the field values: more methodological expertise, more donor relationships, more evidence production. Organisations that possess different forms of capital — community trust, local knowledge, practical wisdom — may be more effective at development but less successful in the development field, because the field's criteria of success are its own, not the community's.

## How to Apply
1. **Analyse the development field as a field.** "Map the positions: donors, implementers, evaluators, government partners, communities. Map the capitals: who has funding, expertise, networks, reputation? Map the stakes: what are agents competing for — funding, recognition, influence, survival?"
2. **Surface the doxa.** "What does the development field take for granted? The programme cycle? The logical framework? The theory of change? Results-based management? These are not neutral tools — they are the field's doxa, and they shape what can be done and what can be seen."
3. **Trace field-level reproduction.** "How does the development field reproduce itself? Through credentialing (who is qualified to do development work), through funding mechanisms (who can access resources), through knowledge production (whose knowledge counts), through gatekeeping (who decides what is legitimate)."
4. **Identify the community's position in the field.** "Where are the communities the programme serves positioned in the development field? They are not agents in the field — they are the object of the field. They do not compete for its stakes, control its capitals, or define its rules. They are positioned as 'beneficiaries' — a classification that denies them agency within the field that is supposedly serving them."

## Examples
**Situation:** A development organisation has worked in a region for fifteen years. Its programmes have been well-funded, well-evaluated, and well-documented. But an external assessment finds that the organisation's primary beneficiaries — rural communities — have experienced little sustained change. The organisation has grown, however: more staff, more funding, more publications, more international recognition.
**Application:** "The organisation has been successful in the development field while being ineffective in the development task. These are not the same thing. The development field rewards: well-designed programmes (conforming to the field's methodological standards), positive evaluations (producing the evidence the field values), professional documentation (generating the publications and reports that build symbolic capital), and donor relationships (maintaining the social capital that secures funding). The organisation has invested in all of these — and has been rewarded. But the capitals of the development field are not the same as the resources that produce change in communities. The community needs sustained presence, relationship-based engagement, practical problem-solving, and patient attention to local dynamics. The development field rewards project-based interventions, measurable outcomes, methodological innovation, and evidence production. The organisation has been rational — it has invested in the capitals that the field rewards. But the field's rewards and the community's needs are misaligned. The organisation has reproduced itself within the development field while failing to produce change in the community the field is supposed to serve. This is not individual failure — it is the structural logic of a field whose internal criteria of success do not correspond to its stated mission."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present development as uniquely pathological.
**Why:** All fields operate by their own logic, reward their own capitals, and reproduce their own structures. The development field is not uniquely perverse — it is a field, and it behaves as fields behave. The analysis should illuminate, not moralise.

**Don't:** Conclude that development is impossible.
**Why:** Field analysis reveals the structural logic that constrains practice. But fields are sites of struggle — their logic can be contested, reformed, and partially transformed. Understanding the field is the precondition for changing it.
