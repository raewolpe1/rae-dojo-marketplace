# Bourdieu and Language

## When to Use
- When language — which language is spoken, which register is valued, whose speech is recognised as legitimate — is functioning as a mechanism of exclusion in development practice.
- When participatory processes reproduce linguistic hierarchies by privileging those who speak the dominant language, the formal register, or the professional vocabulary.
- When analysing how linguistic capital shapes access to institutions, resources, and power.

## Core Concept
In *Language and Symbolic Power* (1991), Bourdieu argues that language is not merely a medium of communication but a form of capital deployed in linguistic markets. A linguistic market is any social situation in which speech is produced, evaluated, and rewarded according to implicit criteria. In the academic linguistic market, theoretical sophistication is valued. In the bureaucratic linguistic market, procedural precision is valued. In the development linguistic market, the language of results, evidence, and impact is valued.

Linguistic capital — the capacity to produce speech that is recognised as legitimate in a particular market — is distributed unequally, and its distribution tracks the distribution of other forms of capital. Those who speak the dominant language, in the dominant register, with the dominant vocabulary, possess linguistic capital that converts directly to symbolic capital (authority, credibility) and indirectly to economic capital (access to positions, contracts, funding).

The concept of the legitimate language is crucial. In any field, one form of linguistic practice is recognised as the standard against which all others are measured. In the development field, English is the legitimate language globally; within national contexts, the official language of the state is the legitimate language; within institutional contexts, the professional register of development discourse is the legitimate register. Those who command the legitimate language possess a form of capital that is invisible as capital because it appears as natural competence.

## How to Apply
1. **Map the linguistic market.** "In this space — this workshop, this consultation, this evaluation — whose language is valued? Who speaks with authority? Whose speech is recorded, translated, and acted upon? Whose is ignored, simplified, or treated as 'anecdotal'?"
2. **Identify the linguistic capital distribution.** "Who in this room has the linguistic capital to be heard? Those who speak the professional register, the official language, the formal vocabulary? The linguistic capital distribution mirrors — and reinforces — the overall distribution of power."
3. **Analyse 'participation' as linguistic market.** "Participatory processes create linguistic markets. The question is not whether people participate but in whose linguistic terms they participate. A community consultation conducted in English, using development vocabulary, in an institutional setting, is a linguistic market designed for professionals, not communities."
4. **Redesign the linguistic market.** "If the linguistic market excludes those the process is meant to serve, change the market. Conduct the consultation in local languages. Value oral testimony. Recognise that 'inarticulate' in the professional register may be highly articulate in a different register."

## Examples
**Situation:** A multi-stakeholder platform brings together government officials, NGO staff, community representatives, and donors to discuss a district development plan. The meeting is conducted in English, uses PowerPoint presentations, and follows a structured agenda with time slots for each speaker. Community representatives attend but speak rarely and briefly. The facilitator notes their "low participation" and recommends "capacity building for community engagement."
**Application:** "The community representatives' 'low participation' is not a reflection of their capacity. It is the predictable effect of a linguistic market designed for a different habitus. The meeting's linguistic market values: English proficiency, familiarity with development vocabulary, comfort with formal presentation formats, and the confident self-authorisation to claim speaking time in an institutional setting. These are the linguistic capital of the professional class. The community representatives may be highly articulate, persuasive, and analytically sophisticated — in their own language, in their own register, in their own communicative contexts. The meeting has not measured their capacity; it has measured their linguistic capital in a market designed for others. The recommendation for 'capacity building' is symbolic violence: it locates the problem in the community representatives' deficiency rather than in the institutional setting's exclusionary design. The remedy is to redesign the linguistic market: conduct sessions in local languages with professional translation; replace PowerPoint with formats familiar to community communication (storytelling, dialogue, demonstration); restructure the agenda to allow the communicative practices that community members command; and recognise that the goal is not to train community members to participate in the professional linguistic market but to create a market in which their existing linguistic capital is valued."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat language as merely a technical communication barrier.
**Why:** Translation solves the language problem at the technical level. But the linguistic market operates at a deeper level — the register, the vocabulary, the communicative format, the implicit criteria of legitimate speech. Translation does not address these.

**Don't:** Romanticise local languages without analysing the linguistic market.
**Why:** Local languages may themselves contain linguistic hierarchies — gendered speech norms, age-based authority, clan-based exclusions. The analysis must examine all linguistic markets, not only the one dominated by the development profession.
