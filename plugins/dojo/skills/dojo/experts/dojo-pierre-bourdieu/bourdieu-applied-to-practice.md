# Bourdieu Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When the practitioner understands Bourdieu's concepts in principle but needs a structured protocol for applying them to a specific situation.
- When conducting a Bourdieusian analysis of a programme, policy, institution, or intervention.
- When building analytical capacity among practitioners who are new to Bourdieu's toolkit.

## Core Concept
Bourdieu's sociology is inherently practical — it was developed through fieldwork, not armchair philosophy. But translating his concepts into structured professional practice requires a protocol that sequences the analytical moves in a productive order.

Five analytical steps constitute a Bourdieusian assessment:

First, field mapping. Identify the field: what is the structured space of positions in which the programme operates? Who are the agents? What are the stakes? What are the rules of the game? The programme does not enter a vacuum — it enters a field, and the field's existing logic will shape everything the programme can do.

Second, capital inventory. For each agent in the field, enumerate their capitals: economic, cultural, social, symbolic. Map the distribution: who has what? Identify the conversion mechanisms: how is capital being transformed between forms? Identify who controls the exchange rates.

Third, habitus diagnosis. What dispositions do the agents bring? What do they do without thinking? What feels natural? What is inconceivable? The habitus determines what the programme's rules and incentives will actually produce, regardless of what they are designed to produce.

Fourth, symbolic violence and doxa analysis. Where is domination operating through accepted categories? What goes without saying? What classifications appear natural but serve particular interests? Where have the dominated internalised the categories of their domination?

Fifth, reflexivity. Turn the analysis on yourself. What is your position in this field? What capitals do you hold? What does your habitus make visible and invisible? How does your institutional position shape the analysis you have produced? This step is not optional — without it, the analysis reproduces the structures it claims to analyse.

## How to Apply
1. **Sequence the analysis.** "Do not begin with agents — begin with the field. Do not begin with behaviour — begin with positions. Do not begin with outcomes — begin with capitals. And do not end without reflexivity."
2. **Map before intervening.** "The field analysis should precede programme design. If you design first and analyse later, the programme will already be captured by the field's existing logic."
3. **Use the protocol iteratively.** "The first pass will be incomplete. As you learn more about the field, return to the analysis. The capital inventory will reveal habitus dynamics. The habitus diagnosis will reveal doxa. The doxa analysis will reveal symbolic violence. Each step enriches the others."
4. **Document the reflexive step.** "Write the reflexivity analysis. Put it in the assessment report — not as a confessional preamble but as a methodological section. Name your position, your capitals, your habitus, and how these shaped what you saw and what you missed."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant is asked to assess why a local governance programme has not produced the expected improvements in service delivery despite five years of investment in capacity building, institutional reform, and citizen engagement.
**Application:** "Apply the protocol. Step one, field mapping: the local governance space is a field. The agents are: the district executive, the elected councillors, the traditional leaders, the civil servants, the NGOs operating in the district, and the citizens. Map their positions: who dominates? The district executive controls budget allocation (economic capital) and administrative authority (symbolic capital). The traditional leaders control community legitimacy (symbolic capital) and social networks (social capital). The elected councillors hold formal authority but may lack the other capitals needed to exercise it. Step two, capital inventory: the capacity building provided cultural capital (training, skills, knowledge of procedures) but did not change the distribution of economic, social, or symbolic capital. The councillors can recite the procedures but cannot exercise authority because the other forms of capital remain concentrated elsewhere. Step three, habitus diagnosis: the civil servants' habitus was formed under a centralised system. Five years of decentralisation reform have not changed the embodied disposition to defer to the centre, to manage information as a source of power, to treat citizens as supplicants. Step four, doxa and symbolic violence: the programme takes for granted that formal institutional reform produces governance improvement. This is the development field's doxa — and it is contradicted by five years of evidence. Step five, reflexivity: the consultant is positioned in the development field, whose doxa includes the assumption that institutional reform works. This position may make it difficult to see that the programme's theory of change is the problem, not merely its implementation. The assessment should acknowledge this."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the protocol as a checklist.
**Why:** Each step requires genuine analytical work — not the mechanical ticking of boxes but the careful, situated analysis of a particular field. The protocol structures inquiry; it does not replace it.

**Don't:** Skip the reflexivity step.
**Why:** Without reflexivity, the analysis reproduces the analyst's position. The most common error in applied Bourdieu is applying the analysis to others while exempting oneself.
