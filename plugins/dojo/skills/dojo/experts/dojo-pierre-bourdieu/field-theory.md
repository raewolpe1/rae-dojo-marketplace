# Field Theory

## When to Use
- When entering a new social domain — a government ministry, a development sector, a community — and needing to understand the logic that governs practice before intervening.
- When an agent's behaviour makes no sense in isolation but becomes intelligible when their position in the field is understood.
- When analysing why a particular domain operates the way it does — what is valued, what is at stake, who dominates, and who is dominated.

## Core Concept
A field, for Bourdieu, is a structured space of positions defined by the distribution of a specific form of capital. Each field has its own logic, its own stakes, and its own forms of capital that are valued within it. The academic field values cultural capital (publications, credentials, theoretical sophistication). The economic field values economic capital. The political field values political capital (alliances, constituencies, the capacity to mobilise). The development field values a complex mix of all four forms.

Fields are not institutions — they are relational structures. An agent's position in the field is defined not by their absolute properties but by their relation to other positions. The professor is defined in relation to the student, the junior colleague, the department chair, the disciplinary mainstream. Change one position and all others shift.

Every field is a site of struggle between those who dominate (the incumbents, who seek to preserve the structure that serves them) and those who are dominated (the newcomers, who seek to transform it). The stakes of the struggle are the definition of what counts as capital within the field — what is valued, what is legitimate, what is recognised. The dominant have an interest in orthodoxy (preserving the established definition of value); the dominated have an interest in heterodoxy (challenging it).

For development practice, field analysis is the necessary first step before any intervention. Every community, every ministry, every sector is a field with its own logic. The intervention that ignores the field's logic will be captured by it — its resources will be appropriated by those who already dominate the field, and its categories will be translated into the field's existing terms.

## How to Apply
1. **Map the positions.** "Who are the agents in this field? What positions do they occupy? Who dominates? Who is dominated? Who is the newcomer? Who is the incumbent?"
2. **Identify the capital at stake.** "What form of capital determines position in this field? Economic capital? Cultural capital? Social capital? Symbolic capital? A specific combination?"
3. **Analyse the rules of the game.** "What counts as legitimate practice in this field? What is valued? What is rewarded? What is sanctioned? These are the rules — often unwritten — that govern the field."
4. **Identify the struggle.** "Who is trying to preserve the current definition of value (orthodoxy)? Who is trying to change it (heterodoxy)? Your intervention will be drawn into this struggle whether you intend it or not."

## Examples
**Situation:** An international NGO is designing a community-based natural resource management programme. They plan to establish community resource management committees with elected representatives, transparent decision-making, and equitable benefit-sharing.
**Application:** "Before establishing new committees, map the existing field. The natural resources in this community are already governed — not by formal committees but by a field of positions, capitals, and stakes. Who currently controls access to the resources? What form of capital determines control — is it traditional authority (symbolic capital), economic wealth (economic capital), political connections (social capital), or knowledge of the resource (cultural capital)? Who benefits from the current arrangement, and who is excluded? Your committee will not enter a vacuum. It will enter this field. The elected representatives will be elected within this field — which means the existing distribution of capital will determine who is elected. The transparent decision-making will occur within this field — which means the existing power relations will determine who speaks and whose voice carries weight. The equitable benefit-sharing will be distributed within this field — which means the existing mechanisms of appropriation will capture whatever benefits the programme produces. Unless the programme analyses the field first and designs specifically to disrupt the existing distribution of capital, the new committee will reproduce the old power structure in new institutional clothing. This is not a design flaw — it is the predictable outcome of entering a field without mapping it."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the field as static.
**Why:** Fields are sites of ongoing struggle. The distribution of capital shifts. New agents enter. Old rules are contested. Any analysis is a snapshot of an ongoing process.

**Don't:** Analyse agents independently of their field position.
**Why:** An agent's behaviour is intelligible only in relation to their position. The village leader who "resists" the programme may be protecting a position in the field that the programme threatens. Understanding the position reveals the logic of the resistance.
