# The Twelve Leverage Points

## When to Use

- When the user needs to identify where to intervene in a system for maximum effect.
- When interventions are being prioritised and the user needs a framework for assessing their potential impact.
- When conventional interventions are failing and higher-leverage options are needed.

## Fails When

- **The user needs a specific policy recommendation.** Leverage points identify categories of intervention, not specific policies.
- **The system is simple enough that leverage points analysis is overkill.**

## Core Concept

Meadows's essay "Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System" (1999) is one of the most cited works in systems thinking. She identifies twelve places to intervene in a system, ranked from least to most effective:

12. Constants, parameters, numbers (subsidies, taxes, standards)
11. The sizes of buffers and stabilising stocks
10. The structure of material stocks and flows
9. The lengths of delays relative to the rate of system change
8. The strength of negative feedback loops
7. The gain around driving positive feedback loops
6. The structure of information flows
5. The rules of the system (incentives, punishments, constraints)
4. The power to add, change, evolve, or self-organise system structure
3. The goals of the system
2. The mindset or paradigm out of which the system arises
1. The power to transcend paradigms

The key insight is that most policy interventions operate at the lowest leverage levels — adjusting parameters (tax rates, subsidy levels, standards) — while the highest-leverage interventions involve changing goals, paradigms, and the power to self-organise. This explains why enormous policy effort often produces minimal change: the interventions are at the wrong level.

For development practitioners, the implication is profound. Most development programmes operate at leverage levels 12–10: adjusting parameters (funding levels), building buffers (savings, reserves), and changing physical structures (infrastructure). These are necessary but low-leverage. Higher-leverage interventions change information flows (making consequences visible to decision-makers), change the rules (altering incentive structures), change goals (redefining what the system optimises for), and change paradigms (shifting the mental models that determine what is considered possible).

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose the current leverage level.** What kind of intervention is being proposed? Parameter change? Rule change? Goal change? Paradigm change?
2. **Ask whether higher-leverage interventions are available.** If the proposed intervention is a parameter adjustment, is there a rule change, information flow change, or goal change that would be more effective?
3. **Expect resistance at higher levels.** The higher the leverage, the more the intervention threatens existing power structures. Paradigm changes are the most powerful and the most resisted.
4. **Use multiple leverage levels simultaneously.** Parameter changes may be necessary for immediate relief while structural changes produce lasting improvement.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African province is trying to reduce youth unemployment through a wage subsidy (a parameter change — leverage level 12).
**Application:** The leverage points framework suggests looking higher. The wage subsidy adjusts a parameter — the cost of hiring young people — but doesn't change the structures that produce unemployment. Higher-leverage interventions might include: changing information flows (level 6) — connecting young people to job information and employers to skills information; changing the rules (level 5) — reforming labour regulations that make hiring risky for small firms; enabling self-organisation (level 4) — supporting youth-led enterprise rather than only subsidising employment by existing firms; and changing the goal (level 3) — shifting the economic development strategy from "create jobs" to "build productive capacity" in young people. The wage subsidy may help at the margin, but structural unemployment requires structural interventions.
**Result:** Supplement the wage subsidy with higher-leverage interventions that address structural drivers of youth unemployment.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume that high-leverage interventions are always feasible.
**Why:** Paradigm changes and goal changes require political will and social consensus that may not exist. Low-leverage interventions may be the only ones politically feasible in the short term.

**Don't:** Dismiss low-leverage interventions.
**Why:** Parameter changes (funding, standards, prices) can provide immediate relief even if they don't produce lasting structural change. The ideal strategy often combines multiple leverage levels.
