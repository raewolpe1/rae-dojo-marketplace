# Paradigm as Deepest Leverage

## When to Use

- When lower-leverage interventions have failed and paradigm-level change is the remaining option.
- When the dominant assumptions about how a system works are themselves the problem.
- When the user needs to understand why systems resist change even when better alternatives are available.

## Fails When

- **The problem is genuinely technical, not paradigmatic.** Not every problem requires paradigm change.
- **The user needs actionable short-term intervention.** Paradigm change is long-term and uncertain.

## Core Concept

Meadows placed paradigm change as the second-highest leverage point (and transcending paradigms as the highest). A paradigm is the shared set of assumptions, beliefs, and mental models from which a system arises — the worldview that determines what is considered normal, possible, and desirable. Paradigm change is so powerful because everything else in the system flows from it: goals, rules, information structures, and physical structures all reflect the dominant paradigm.

The reason paradigm change is rare and difficult is that paradigms are largely invisible to those who hold them. The paradigm of economic growth as the primary measure of progress is so deeply embedded that most policy-makers cannot imagine an alternative — it is not an assumption they hold but the ground they stand on. Similarly, the paradigm that development means becoming more like wealthy industrialised countries shapes development policy in ways that are rarely questioned.

Meadows argued that paradigm change happens not through argument within the existing paradigm but through demonstrating that the existing paradigm cannot explain or solve current problems, while an alternative paradigm can. Thomas Kuhn's structure of scientific revolutions applies beyond science: paradigm shifts in policy happen when the accumulation of anomalies — problems the existing paradigm cannot solve — becomes overwhelming, and an alternative framework is available.

## How to Apply

1. **Name the paradigm.** What are the unstated assumptions driving the current approach? Make them explicit.
2. **Identify the anomalies.** What problems is the current paradigm failing to solve? What evidence contradicts its predictions?
3. **Articulate the alternative.** What different assumptions would produce different — and better — outcomes?
4. **Demonstrate, don't just argue.** Paradigm change happens through evidence and experience, not through persuasion alone. Pilot programmes, demonstration projects, and successful examples from elsewhere can shift paradigms.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's economic development paradigm equates development with GDP growth and formal employment, yet decades of GDP growth have not reduced unemployment or inequality.
**Application:** Meadows's framework identifies this as a paradigm problem. The assumption — that GDP growth automatically produces development outcomes (employment, poverty reduction, inclusion) — is contradicted by evidence. GDP has grown, but unemployment has risen, inequality has deepened, and poverty remains widespread. The anomalies are overwhelming. An alternative paradigm might define development as expanding human capabilities and productive livelihoods — measured by employment, inclusion, health, education, and environmental sustainability — rather than aggregate output. This paradigm shift would change what is measured (beyond GDP), what is valued (productive livelihoods, not just formal jobs), and what is invested in (human capability, not just physical capital).
**Result:** Name the failing paradigm, articulate the alternative, and use evidence and demonstration to shift the dominant mental model.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat paradigm change as something a consultant can deliver in a workshop.
**Why:** Paradigm shifts are social and political processes that unfold over years or decades. A workshop can plant seeds but cannot produce the shift.

**Don't:** Dismiss people who hold the existing paradigm as stupid or corrupt.
**Why:** Paradigms are invisible to those who hold them. The people operating within a failing paradigm are usually intelligent and well-intentioned — they are constrained by assumptions they cannot see.
