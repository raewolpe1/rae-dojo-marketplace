# Systems and Development Evaluation

## When to Use

- When conventional evaluation approaches (logframes, linear theories of change, counterfactual designs) are inadequate for programmes operating in complex systems.
- When the user needs to integrate systems thinking into monitoring and evaluation practice.
- When emergence, feedback loops, and nonlinearity need to be accounted for in evaluation design.

## Fails When

- **The programme is genuinely simple and linear.** Not every programme needs systems-informed evaluation.
- **The user needs specific evaluation methodologies.** This file covers the principles; specific methods (developmental evaluation, contribution analysis, systems mapping) are practice tools.

## Core Concept

Meadows did not write directly about programme evaluation, but her systems thinking has profound implications for evaluation practice. Conventional evaluation assumes linear causality (the programme causes the outcome), stable contexts (the system around the programme doesn't change significantly during evaluation), and attributable effects (the programme's contribution can be isolated from other factors). In complex systems, all three assumptions fail.

Linear causality fails because programmes interact with feedback loops — a programme may trigger reinforcing loops that amplify its effect, or balancing loops that dampen it, or both simultaneously. The programme's effect is not a fixed quantity but a dynamic interaction between the intervention and the system's response. Stable contexts fail because complex systems are constantly evolving — the context at the end of the programme may be fundamentally different from the context at the beginning, for reasons unrelated to the programme. And attribution fails because in a system with multiple interacting feedback loops, isolating the contribution of a single intervention is often impossible.

Meadows's framework suggests that evaluation in complex systems should: track system-level behaviour patterns (not just programme outputs); map and monitor the feedback loops the programme is trying to influence; assess whether the programme is changing system structure (feedback loops, information flows, rules, goals) or merely changing parameters; expect and document unintended consequences; and use evaluation as a real-time learning tool (feeding information back into programme design) rather than a retrospective judgment tool.

## How to Apply

1. **Map the system at baseline.** Before evaluating the programme, map the system it operates in: what feedback loops maintain the problem? What are the delays? Where are the leverage points?
2. **Track system behaviour, not just programme outputs.** Monitor the behaviour of the system over time, not just the outputs the programme produces. The programme may produce outputs without changing system behaviour.
3. **Assess structural change.** The most important evaluation question in complex systems is not "did the programme produce the intended output?" but "did the programme change the system's structure in ways that produce better behaviour over time?"
4. **Build feedback loops into evaluation.** Design M&E systems that provide real-time information to programme managers, enabling adaptive management. Annual evaluations are too slow for complex systems.
5. **Document emergence.** Record what happened that was not predicted — the unintended consequences, the unexpected interactions, the emergent behaviours. These are often more informative than the intended outcomes.

## Examples

**Situation:** An evaluation of a South African land reform programme measures hectares redistributed, beneficiaries settled, and agricultural output on redistributed farms. The evaluation finds that hectares and beneficiaries are on target, but agricultural output is declining, and the evaluation cannot explain why.

**Application:** A systems-informed evaluation would map the feedback loops that determine agricultural productivity on redistributed land. These include: access to credit (redistributed farms often lack collateral, restricting access to production finance); access to markets (small-scale farmers face barriers to established value chains); access to extension services (post-settlement support is often inadequate); water rights (legal frameworks may not transfer with land); and social dynamics (individual vs. communal production models). The programme changed one stock (land ownership) without changing the feedback loops that determine how productively that land is used. The systems evaluation would identify which feedback loops are constraining productivity, track whether the programme (or complementary programmes) is addressing them, and assess whether the overall system of land, credit, markets, and support is moving toward a sustainable configuration — not just whether hectares have been redistributed.

**Result:** Shift evaluation from output counting (hectares, beneficiaries) to system dynamics assessment (are the feedback loops that determine productive land use being strengthened?).

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Abandon rigour in the name of complexity.
**Why:** Systems-informed evaluation is more rigorous than conventional evaluation, not less. It requires deeper analysis of causal mechanisms, more comprehensive monitoring of system dynamics, and more honest assessment of what can and cannot be attributed.

**Don't:** Use systems thinking to excuse poor programme performance.
**Why:** "The system is complex" is not an explanation for why a programme failed. Systems thinking should identify the specific structural features that constrained the programme, enabling better design — not provide a general excuse.
