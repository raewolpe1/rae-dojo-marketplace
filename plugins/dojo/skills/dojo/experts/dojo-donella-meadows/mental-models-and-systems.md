# Mental Models and Systems

## When to Use

- When unstated assumptions are shaping and constraining analysis or intervention design.
- When stakeholders see the same situation differently because they hold different mental models.
- When surfacing and examining assumptions is a prerequisite for progress.

## Fails When

- **The disagreement is empirical, not conceptual.** If stakeholders agree on the model but disagree about data, this file is less relevant.
- **The user needs facilitation techniques.** This file covers the concept; facilitation is a practice skill.

## Core Concept

Mental models are the internal representations people use to understand how the world works — the simplified maps of complex reality that guide perception, interpretation, and action. Meadows emphasised that mental models are the invisible infrastructure of systems: every policy, every programme, every institution is built on assumptions about how the system works. If those assumptions are wrong, the intervention will fail no matter how well executed it is.

The challenge is that mental models are largely unconscious. People don't usually say "my mental model of the economy is..." — they simply see the economy through their model and act accordingly. This makes mental models powerful (they shape all subsequent analysis) and dangerous (they are rarely examined).

Common mental model errors in development include: the assumption that the system is linear (inputs produce proportional outputs) when it is actually nonlinear (thresholds, tipping points, exponential dynamics); the assumption that the system is in equilibrium (will return to normal after a shock) when it may be in transition (moving to a new state); the assumption that causes are local and immediate when they may be distant and delayed; and the assumption that the system responds predictably to intervention when it may respond counterintuitively.

Meadows's prescription: expose your mental models to the open air. Make assumptions explicit. Test them against evidence. Hold them lightly — be willing to revise when evidence contradicts the model. And when working with multiple stakeholders, make the difference in mental models explicit before trying to agree on interventions.

## How to Apply

1. **Surface assumptions.** Before designing an intervention, ask: what do we assume about how this system works? Write the assumptions down.
2. **Compare mental models.** When stakeholders disagree, map their different mental models of the same system. Often the disagreement is not about values but about models.
3. **Test models against evidence.** Does the model's prediction match observed behaviour? If not, the model needs revision.
4. **Create spaces for model revision.** Workshops, simulations, field visits, and data review can all challenge and update mental models.

## Examples

**Situation:** A national treasury and a health department disagree about health spending. The treasury's mental model treats health as consumption (spending that does not produce economic return). The health department's mental model treats health as investment (spending that produces economic return through productivity, human capital, and reduced future costs).
**Application:** The disagreement is not about data but about mental models. Both departments look at the same budget line but see different things because they hold different models of how health spending relates to economic performance. Surfacing the mental models explicitly — making each side articulate its assumptions — is the first step. Testing the models against evidence is the second: does the evidence support health spending as consumption or investment? The evidence strongly supports the investment model. But if the mental model mismatch is not made explicit, the debate will continue as a budget fight rather than a model comparison.
**Result:** Surface the competing mental models, test them against evidence, and build a shared model that can support evidence-based budgeting.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume your own mental model is correct.
**Why:** Everyone's mental model is a simplification of complex reality. It is always partially wrong. The question is how wrong, and in which ways.

**Don't:** Try to change someone's mental model by arguing within your own.
**Why:** Mental models resist argument. They change through experience, evidence, and encounter with anomalies — not through debate. Create experiences that challenge the model rather than arguments that defend your own.
