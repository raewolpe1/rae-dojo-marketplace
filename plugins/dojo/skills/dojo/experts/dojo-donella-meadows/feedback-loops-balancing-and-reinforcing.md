# Feedback Loops: Balancing and Reinforcing

## When to Use

- When the user needs to understand why systems grow, stabilise, oscillate, or collapse.
- When vicious cycles (poverty traps) or virtuous cycles (development momentum) are operating.
- When the dynamics of change resistance or change amplification need explanation.

## Fails When

- **The system genuinely has no significant feedback.** Some processes are open-loop.
- **The user needs the full system map.** This file covers feedback concepts; full mapping requires combining multiple files.

## Core Concept

Feedback loops are the engines of system behaviour. A feedback loop exists when the output of a process feeds back to influence the input. There are two fundamental types.

Reinforcing (positive) feedback loops amplify change — they produce exponential growth or exponential decline. Population growth is a reinforcing loop: more people produce more births, which produce more people. Compound interest is a reinforcing loop: more money earns more interest, which produces more money. Poverty is a reinforcing loop: poverty reduces health and education, which reduces productivity, which deepens poverty. Reinforcing loops are the source of both virtuous cycles and vicious cycles.

Balancing (negative) feedback loops resist change — they push the system toward a goal or equilibrium. A thermostat is a balancing loop: when temperature exceeds the set point, the heater turns off; when it drops below, the heater turns on. Market prices are (in theory) a balancing loop: high prices reduce demand and increase supply, pushing prices back down. Government regulation is a balancing loop: when behaviour deviates from the norm, the regulation intervenes to correct it.

System behaviour depends on which loops dominate. In a growing economy, reinforcing loops (investment → productivity → income → more investment) dominate. In a stagnating economy, balancing loops (bureaucracy, corruption, disincentives) may suppress the reinforcing loops. In a collapsing system, reinforcing loops drive decline (capital flight → economic contraction → more capital flight).

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the feedback loops.** For any system behaviour, ask: what reinforcing loops are driving change? What balancing loops are resisting it?
2. **Determine which loop dominates.** Is the system growing (reinforcing dominates), stable (balancing dominates), or oscillating (delays in balancing loops)?
3. **Strengthen virtuous reinforcing loops.** If a positive cycle exists — investment → productivity → income → more investment — what would strengthen it?
4. **Break vicious reinforcing loops.** If a negative cycle exists — poverty → poor health → low productivity → poverty — where can it be interrupted?
5. **Don't fight balancing loops.** If a balancing loop is resisting your intervention, understand why — the loop may be serving a purpose. Change the goal of the loop rather than trying to overwhelm it.

## Examples

**Situation:** A rural development programme in Limpopo invests in agricultural infrastructure, but farmers don't increase production because they can't access markets, and the lack of production means transport operators don't service the area.
**Application:** This is a reinforcing loop in the wrong direction — a vicious cycle. Low production → no transport services → no market access → low production. The same structure could run in the positive direction: investment in market access → more production → more transport services → better market access → more production. The leverage point is breaking the negative loop by intervening at the weakest link: establishing a transport service or aggregation point that creates the initial market access, which triggers the reinforcing loop in the positive direction. Once the virtuous cycle starts, it can become self-sustaining.
**Result:** Identify the vicious cycle and intervene to flip it into a virtuous cycle by addressing the weakest link.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume that reinforcing loops always run forever.
**Why:** Every reinforcing loop eventually encounters a limit — resource depletion, market saturation, institutional capacity. The question is what the limit is and when it bites.

**Don't:** Treat balancing loops as obstacles.
**Why:** Balancing loops often serve essential functions — they maintain stability, prevent overshoot, and protect system integrity. Overriding them can destabilise the system.
