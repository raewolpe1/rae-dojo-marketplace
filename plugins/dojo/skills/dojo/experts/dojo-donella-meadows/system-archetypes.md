# System Archetypes

## When to Use

- When a recurring systemic pattern needs diagnosis and the user may not realise a known archetype applies.
- When rapid pattern recognition can accelerate system analysis.
- When the user describes a problem that has a classic systemic structure.

## Fails When

- **The system is genuinely novel.** Not every problem fits a known archetype.
- **The user needs archetype-specific intervention design.** This file surveys the archetypes; intervention design requires additional analysis.

## Core Concept

System archetypes are recurring patterns of system behaviour that appear across domains. Recognising the archetype enables faster diagnosis and intervention design because each archetype has a known structure and known leverage points. The major archetypes, drawn from systems dynamics and Senge's adaptation:

**Fixes That Fail:** A quick fix addresses a symptom but creates side effects that worsen the underlying problem. Example: using pesticides kills pests but also kills predators, requiring more pesticide.

**Shifting the Burden:** A symptomatic solution reduces the motivation to address the fundamental cause, which atrophies. Example: emergency food aid reduces hunger but displaces local agriculture, increasing long-term food insecurity.

**Limits to Success (Limits to Growth):** A reinforcing loop drives growth until it encounters a balancing loop (a limit). Example: a successful programme grows until it overwhelms institutional capacity.

**Success to the Successful:** Resources flow to the successful, giving them more advantage, while the unsuccessful fall further behind. Example: productive regions attract investment, while poor regions lose it — inequality widens.

**Tragedy of the Commons:** Individuals acting in self-interest deplete a shared resource. Example: overfishing, overgrazing, aquifer depletion.

**Escalation:** Two parties respond to each other's actions with increasing intensity. Example: arms races, price wars, competitive poaching.

**Growth and Underinvestment:** Growth approaches a limit that could be raised by investment, but the investment is delayed because the limit hasn't been reached yet. When the limit bites, the system collapses. Example: infrastructure underinvestment until the infrastructure fails.

## How to Apply

1. **Listen for the pattern.** When a user describes a problem, ask: does this match a known archetype?
2. **Name the archetype.** Naming the pattern gives the user a framework for understanding why the problem persists and what the structural drivers are.
3. **Identify the leverage point specific to the archetype.** Each archetype has a known leverage point: for shifting the burden, strengthen the fundamental solution; for tragedy of the commons, establish governance; for success to the successful, create equalising mechanisms.
4. **Test the diagnosis.** Ask: does this archetype's predicted behaviour match what we observe? If yes, the archetype's leverage points apply.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African city's response to informal settlement fires is to rebuild shelters and provide emergency relief, but fires recur repeatedly in the same areas.
**Application:** This is "fixes that fail." The symptomatic solution (rebuild after fire) addresses the immediate need but doesn't address the structural causes: overcrowding, flammable building materials, lack of electricity (which leads to open-flame cooking and heating), and inadequate emergency access roads. Each rebuild restores the conditions that produce the next fire. The leverage point is the fundamental solution: electrification (reducing open flames), settlement upgrading (reducing overcrowding and improving materials), and fire prevention infrastructure (access roads, fire breaks). The symptomatic fix is necessary (people need shelter after a fire), but without the fundamental solution, the pattern recurs.
**Result:** Supplement emergency response with structural interventions that address the root causes of fire risk.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Force every problem into an archetype.
**Why:** Archetypes are pattern-recognition tools, not universal classifications. Some problems are genuinely novel or combine multiple archetypes.

**Don't:** Use archetypes as substitutes for rigorous analysis.
**Why:** Recognising the archetype is the beginning of analysis, not the end. The specific dynamics, delays, and leverage points require detailed investigation.
