# Moral Realism

## When to Use
- When the question of whether moral standards are real — objective, discoverable, binding — arises in professional practice.
- When moral relativism ("it depends on the culture/context/stakeholder") threatens to dissolve genuine moral accountability.
- When grounding professional ethics in something more substantial than institutional policy or personal preference.

## Core Concept
Murdoch is a moral realist in the Platonic tradition: she holds that the Good is real, that moral truths are discovered rather than invented, and that moral perception — seeing clearly — is a form of knowledge, not merely a form of feeling. This puts her in direct opposition to both the existentialist view (morality is a matter of arbitrary choice) and the emotivist view (moral statements are merely expressions of preference).

Murdoch's moral realism is grounded not in metaphysical argument but in moral experience. When we struggle to see another person justly — overcoming our prejudices, our self-interest, our habitual categories — we experience the pull of something real. The effort to see clearly is not arbitrary; it has a direction. That direction is toward truth, justice, accuracy — toward the Good. This experience of being drawn toward clearer seeing is the phenomenological evidence for moral realism.

For development practice, moral realism matters because it provides a foundation for accountability that transcends institutional convenience. If justice is real, then a programme that treats people unjustly is wrong — not merely "inappropriate" or "not aligned with our values" but genuinely wrong. If dignity is real, then practices that degrade dignity violate something that exists independently of whether the programme design acknowledged it.

## How to Apply
1. **Ground accountability in moral reality.** "This programme is not merely ineffective — it is unjust. That is not a preference statement; it is a moral fact that the evaluation must acknowledge."
2. **Resist the relativist slide.** "Different stakeholders may have different preferences, but not all preferences are equally valid. The preference to exclude vulnerable populations from services is not equivalent to the preference to include them. One is closer to the Good."
3. **Use moral language with confidence.** "Just, unjust, dignified, degrading, truthful, dishonest — these are not subjective labels. They describe real features of situations that attention can perceive."
4. **Distinguish moral realism from moral certainty.** "The Good is real, but our perception of it is always partial and fallible. Moral realism does not mean moral arrogance — it means moral seriousness."

## Examples
**Situation:** During a programme design workshop, a consultant raises concerns that the proposed targeting criteria will systematically exclude elderly persons living alone. The programme team responds that targeting is a technical decision based on cost-effectiveness analysis, not a moral question.
**Application:** "Targeting is presented as a technical decision — and technically, cost-effectiveness analysis may support excluding elderly persons if the cost per outcome is higher. But the decision to exclude vulnerable human beings from assistance is not merely technical. It is moral. The elderly persons living alone are real people with real needs and real dignity. The cost-effectiveness analysis is a useful tool, but it cannot be sovereign — because it measures only what it measures, and what it does not measure (dignity, justice, the moral weight of vulnerability) is also real. Murdoch would insist: attend to the particular people you are proposing to exclude. See them — not as data points in a cost-effectiveness calculation but as persons. Does the decision still feel technical? The Good is sovereign over cost-effectiveness, not the reverse."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Claim moral certainty on the basis of moral realism.
**Why:** Moral realism says the Good is real; it does not say we perceive it perfectly. Our moral vision is always partial, always potentially distorted by ego. Moral realism demands humility about one's own perception while maintaining confidence that there is something real to perceive.

**Don't:** Dismiss cultural difference as irrelevant.
**Why:** Moral realism does not deny that moral understanding varies across cultures. It holds that beneath the variation, there are moral realities — justice, dignity, truth — that all cultures engage with, though differently. Cultural sensitivity and moral realism are compatible.
