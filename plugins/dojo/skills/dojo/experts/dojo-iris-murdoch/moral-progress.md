# Moral Progress

## When to Use
- When the practitioner wants to become better at their work but conceives of improvement only in technical terms — more skills, better methods, additional credentials.
- When moral progress — becoming better at seeing, more honest in reporting, more just in engagement — needs to be named as a legitimate and central form of professional development.
- When the slow, undramatic nature of genuine moral improvement needs to be acknowledged and honoured.

## Core Concept
Murdoch's account of moral progress is distinctive because it locates change in perception rather than action. Moral progress is not primarily about making different choices — it is about seeing more clearly, so that when the moment of choice arrives, the right action appears as natural and obvious. The work of moral progress happens before the moment of decision, in the slow, patient discipline of learning to attend to reality without ego-distortion.

Murdoch's famous example is the mother-in-law (M) who dislikes her daughter-in-law (D). M finds D common, undignified, tiresomely juvenile. But M is a morally serious person. She recognises that her judgments may reflect her own prejudice rather than D's reality. So she undertakes the discipline of attention: she looks again, more carefully, more justly. Over time, her perception shifts. She comes to see D as not vulgar but refreshingly simple, not undignified but spontaneous, not juvenile but youthfully joyful. No external action has changed. D has not changed. M's behaviour toward D may not even change visibly. But a profound moral event has occurred: M has overcome a piece of ego-distortion and now sees D more justly.

For professional practice, moral progress means becoming better at seeing communities, programmes, and situations as they actually are — stripping away the professional categories, methodological habits, institutional expectations, and personal ambitions that distort perception. This is slow work. It does not happen in a training workshop. It happens over years of disciplined attention — and it can always be undone by a fresh assertion of the ego.

## How to Apply
1. **Name perception as the site of progress.** "Professional development is not only about acquiring new methods. It is about refining your capacity to see. Are you seeing this community more clearly than you saw the last one? That is moral progress."
2. **Honour the slow work.** "Moral progress is undramatic. It does not announce itself. It is the gradual refinement of perception — seeing a little more justly, attending a little more carefully, reporting a little more honestly. This is not inferior to dramatic transformation; it is the only kind of genuine moral change."
3. **Use past blindness as evidence.** "Think of something you now see that you could not see five years ago — a form of injustice, a pattern of exclusion, a community dynamic. That shift in perception is moral progress. It tells you that further shifts are possible."
4. **Resist the myth of arrival.** "You will never finish becoming a better practitioner. The ego reasserts itself; new situations reveal new blind spots; the discipline of attention must be renewed daily. This is not discouraging — it is the nature of moral life."

## Examples
**Situation:** A mid-career evaluator reflects on her professional growth. She has acquired new methods (contribution analysis, process tracing, realist evaluation), attended numerous trainings, and published several papers. But she has a nagging sense that something more fundamental has changed — or should change — that her professional development metrics do not capture.
**Application:** "Your professional development record is impressive and real. But the change you sense — the one your metrics cannot capture — is moral progress. Think of how you conducted your first evaluation: you arrived with a methodology, applied it, and reported the findings. Now think of your most recent evaluation: you arrived with a methodology but also with a discipline of attention — you noticed things you would not have noticed ten years ago. The quiet tension in the room when the programme manager described the targeting criteria. The way certain women did not speak in the focus groups. The gap between the log frame's description of 'empowerment' and the reality of the women's lives. This is not methodological improvement — it is perceptual refinement. You see more because you have learned to attend more carefully. This is the moral progress Murdoch describes: the gradual overcoming of the ego's distortions — the assumptions, the categories, the professional habits that once stood between you and the reality. You have not arrived. You will never arrive. But you are moving in the right direction — and that direction is toward clearer seeing."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Promise transformation.
**Why:** Moral progress is gradual, partial, and reversible. The practitioner who expects dramatic transformation will be disappointed. The practitioner who recognises that she sees a little more clearly than she did last year is on the right track.

**Don't:** Reduce moral progress to professional competency development.
**Why:** Competency is about what you can do; moral progress is about what you can see. A practitioner can acquire every competency in the professional framework and still be morally blind.
