# Attention and Moral Vision

## When to Use
- When the quality of perception is the fundamental issue — the practitioner is not seeing the situation, the community, or the programme clearly.
- When assumptions, methodological habits, or institutional categories are distorting what is perceived.
- When establishing that seeing clearly is prior to acting well.

## Core Concept
Murdoch's central moral insight is that morality is primarily a matter of attention. Against the dominant view that morality consists in dramatic moments of choice — the existentialist leap, the utilitarian calculation — Murdoch argued that moral quality resides in the continuous activity of attending to reality. The morally good person is the person who sees clearly — who directs what Murdoch calls "a just and loving gaze" toward what is actually there, rather than projecting their own desires, anxieties, and categories onto the world.

Murdoch illustrates this with the famous example of a mother-in-law (M) and daughter-in-law (D). M initially sees D as "unpolished, lacking in dignity and refinement." Over time, through a deliberate effort of attention, M comes to see D as "not vulgar but refreshingly simple, not undignified but spontaneous." M's outward behaviour may not change at all — the moral transformation is entirely internal, in the quality of M's vision. But this internal transformation is genuine moral progress: M has moved from ego-distorted perception to clearer seeing.

For development practice, this insight is foundational. The practitioner who sees a community through categories — 'poor,' 'vulnerable,' 'food-insecure' — is not seeing the community. They are seeing their categories, projected onto reality. The moral task is to attend: to see the particular people, the specific relationships, the distinctive culture and history that constitute this community's reality. This seeing is not a preliminary to the real work — it is the real work. Everything that follows — design, implementation, evaluation — depends on the quality of this initial perception.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask what you are actually seeing.** "Describe the community, the programme, the situation in particular terms — not categories. What surprised you? What did you not expect? These are the moments where reality contradicts your projections."
2. **Identify the ego's projections.** "What assumptions are you bringing? What does your methodology predispose you to see? What would you prefer to see? These are the sources of distortion."
3. **Practise the loving gaze.** "Attend to the situation with patience and generosity — not sentimentality but the genuine desire to see it as it really is, in its own terms rather than yours."
4. **Check your vocabulary.** "Are the words you use to describe the situation adequate to its reality? Or do they reduce it to something manageable? Expand the vocabulary until it matches the complexity of what you see."

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluation team visits a women's savings group that the programme reports as highly successful — regular meetings, growing savings, loan repayment rates above 95%. The evaluation terms of reference ask them to verify the quantitative indicators. They arrive, observe the meeting, and confirm the numbers.
**Application:** "You have measured without seeing. The numbers are accurate — the savings group meets regularly, savings grow, loans are repaid. But what did you actually see when you sat in that meeting? Did you see the power dynamics — who speaks and who is silent? Did you see whether the women are making genuine decisions or performing a methodology that was prescribed by the programme? Did you see the woman who looked anxious — what was her situation? Did you see the relationships — friendship, rivalry, obligation — that the financial data cannot capture? The evaluation confirmed the categories: savings, loans, repayment. But the categories are not the reality. The reality is particular women in a specific social context making particular decisions about their money and their relationships. To see that reality requires attention — the patient, ego-overcoming effort to perceive what is actually there rather than what the terms of reference asked you to verify. Your evaluation will be technically correct and morally blind. The moral task is to attend."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present attention as merely looking harder at data.
**Why:** Murdochian attention is not cognitive effort but moral discipline. It requires overcoming the ego's desire to see what confirms its expectations. It is fundamentally about the quality of the perceiver, not the quantity of perception.

**Don't:** Imply that clear seeing automatically produces right action.
**Why:** Murdoch believed that clear seeing makes right action more likely, but she did not claim it was sufficient. Structural constraints, power dynamics, and material conditions also shape what can be done.
