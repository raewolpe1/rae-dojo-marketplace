## When to Use

Use this file when the user asks about:
- Using AI to help think through complex problems, not just execute tasks
- Stress-testing ideas or arguments with AI
- AI as a Socratic partner or devil's advocate
- Getting past surface-level AI output into genuine ideation help
- Pre-writing conversations before committing to a draft

## Fails When

- The user wants AI to think for them rather than with them — the value is in the friction, not the output
- The user has no starting point of their own — you need a position to stress-test
- The stakes require expert human review (medical, legal, financial decisions)

## Core Concept

Most people use AI as an execution tool: give it a task, get output. The far more interesting use is as a thinking partner—something that pushes back on your reasoning, surfaces what you're not seeing, and helps you get clearer about what you actually think before you commit to a direction.

The difference is significant. When AI executes, it produces output you evaluate. When AI thinks with you, it asks questions that change the output you'd have produced alone. The first is faster. The second is better.

I started treating AI as a thinking partner almost by accident. I was stuck on a newsletter essay—I knew what I wanted to say but couldn't figure out why it felt flat. Instead of asking AI to improve the draft, I described my situation: "I'm trying to write about how AI changes expertise, but the draft feels obvious. Here's my current argument. What am I missing? What would a smart critic say?" What came back wasn't a better draft—it was three questions that exposed the hole in my argument. The essay I eventually wrote was substantially different and substantially better.

There's a pre-writing conversation that most writers and thinkers skip. They jump from vague intention to draft, skipping the phase where you stress-test the idea before you commit to expressing it. AI is extraordinarily useful for this pre-commitment phase: it can steelman opposing views, identify assumptions you're treating as settled, surface related questions you haven't addressed, and ask "what would happen if you're wrong about this?"

The key is moving from task-giving to dialogue. Task-giving sounds like: "Write an intro for my essay about X." Dialogue sounds like: "I'm trying to make the case that X, but I feel like I'm missing something important. Here's my current reasoning. What assumptions am I making? What would a skeptic say?" One produces output; the other produces insight.

There are three modes I've found particularly productive:

**Stress-testing.** Share your argument or plan. Ask AI to identify the three strongest objections, the most important assumption you haven't addressed, and what evidence would change your mind. This is especially valuable before important decisions or public commitments.

**Perspective multiplication.** Ask AI to respond to your idea from the perspective of three different stakeholders—someone who benefits, someone who loses, someone in a different context. This surfaces implications you'd otherwise miss.

**Clarification pressure.** When you're confused about what you actually think, describe the confusion and ask AI to ask you questions—not to answer them but to help you figure out what you'd say. The conversation that results often clarifies faster than solo reflection.

The quality of thinking-partner conversations scales dramatically with the quality of what you bring. If you share a vague sense that something is off, you get generic encouragement. If you share a specific argument with your current best reasoning, you get specific, useful pushback. Bring real content; get real improvement.

## How to Apply

1. **Before a major piece of work, have a pre-conversation.** Describe what you're trying to accomplish, what you currently believe, and what you're uncertain about. Ask AI what you might be missing. Let this change your plan before you execute.

2. **Use the devil's advocate prompt explicitly.** "Here's my position. Please argue the strongest possible case against it. Don't hedge. Tell me where I'm most likely to be wrong."

3. **Describe confusion, not just tasks.** "I'm trying to decide between X and Y. Here's my current thinking. Help me think through what I'm not considering" produces better results than "Compare X and Y."

4. **Iterate within the conversation.** Don't accept the first response. Push back: "You said Z, but what about W?" or "That's an interesting objection, but I think I can answer it by saying... Does that work?" The quality of thinking-partner AI use is in the iteration.

5. **Close with synthesis.** After a substantial thinking-partner conversation, ask AI to summarize: "What have we established? What's still uncertain? What are the key questions I still need to answer?" This consolidates the conversation into something actionable.

## Examples

**Example 1: Pre-decision stress-test**
A product manager is considering deprecating a feature that 20% of users rely on but that adds complexity to the codebase. She asks: "I'm thinking about removing Feature X. Here's my reasoning: [specifics]. What am I not thinking about? What would users who rely on this feature say? What would my CEO say when she sees the backlash?" The thinking-partner response reveals two user segments she hadn't differentiated, a potential PR angle she hadn't anticipated, and a migration path she hadn't considered. The decision that results is more defensible and better designed.

**Example 2: Clarity before commitment**
A consultant is preparing a recommendation for a client. He has a recommendation but feels vague about whether it's really the right one. Instead of drafting the recommendation, he describes the situation to AI: "I'm going to recommend X to this client. Here's the context. Before I commit to this, push back on the reasoning. Where's the weakest point in my argument? What would a smart client say in response?" The response identifies two assumptions he was treating as facts. He adjusts the recommendation and builds supporting evidence for the assumptions before presenting. The client meeting goes significantly better.

## Anti-Patterns

**Seeking validation, not challenge.** Framing the conversation so AI agrees with you rather than pushes back. "Here's my great idea, isn't it great?" produces agreement. "Here's my idea. What's wrong with it?" produces insight. Most people, without realizing it, structure their AI conversations to confirm rather than challenge.

**Accepting the first response.** The first response in a thinking-partner conversation is rarely the most useful. It's the second and third round—after you've responded to the pushback, explained your counter-reasoning, and pushed for specificity—where the real value emerges. One-shot thinking-partner prompts are almost always inferior.

**Confusing fluency for insight.** AI produces confident-sounding, well-structured responses. This can make generic observations feel like genuine insight. The check is: "Did I learn something I wouldn't have thought of myself?" If yes, it's insight. If it's mostly articulating what you already knew, it's articulate confirmation bias.
