## Core Concept

The productivity genre has a structural problem: it sells universal systems to people who have individual contexts. The system that transformed the author's life may be poorly suited to your specific work type, personality, constraints, and goals. The solution is not to find a better universal system — it's to develop the practice of learning what works for you through deliberate self-experimentation.

Self-experimentation is systematic personal observation with an intent to learn and update. It differs from trying things randomly (which teaches you nothing) and from following systems (which teaches you what works for the author). The key elements are: a clear hypothesis, an observation period with some structure, and a genuine willingness to update based on what you find.

The hypothesis is the most important and most neglected part. Most people "try things" without any clear articulation of what they're testing. "I'm going to try time blocking this week" is not a hypothesis. "I think my writing quality suffers when I work in 30-minute blocks rather than 2-hour blocks because I can't reach the thinking depth I need for the first 20 minutes" is a hypothesis. The difference is testability.

For AI tools specifically, self-experimentation is especially valuable because the field is changing fast, your needs are specific, and the advice is contradictory. Rather than asking "should I use AI for X," run a four-week experiment where you use AI for X in weeks one and three and don't in weeks two and four, and track your output quality and how you feel about your work. The answer you find will be more reliable than any general recommendation.

What to observe in a self-experiment: output quantity and quality (where you can estimate it), your experience of the work (energized, bored, anxious, frustrated), downstream effects (did the habit persist? did it create other problems?), and threshold conditions (when did the approach work well vs. poorly). The last two are often more valuable than the first two.

The self-experimentation stance is also a way of maintaining intellectual honesty about what's working. Most people adopt a system, invest in it, and then become defensive of it because abandoning it would mean they made a mistake. The self-experimenter expects to update — the experiment is not a test of whether they chose correctly, it's a tool for finding out what to choose next.

## How to Apply

1. **Turn questions into hypotheses.** When you're curious whether something will work for you, write a specific hypothesis: "I think [X] will improve [Y] because [Z]." The specificity forces you to clarify what you're actually testing and what would count as success or failure.

2. **Define your metrics before starting.** What will you look at to evaluate the experiment? Commit to these before you start. If you decide after the experiment what to measure, you'll unconsciously select metrics that confirm what you already believe.

3. **Run experiments in weeks, not days.** Most changes take a week or two to stabilize into real behavioral patterns. One-day trials tell you how something feels when it's new; two- to four-week trials tell you how it works when it's routine.

4. **Write a debrief.** At the end of the experiment, write a short summary: hypothesis, what you observed, what you concluded, what you'll do next. The act of writing forces clarity and creates a record you can return to.

5. **Update based on evidence.** The whole point is willingness to revise. If you find that deep work blocks don't work for you because your best thinking happens in shorter bursts, that's a real finding — not a character flaw. Act on it.

## Examples

**Example 1: Testing AI for weekly newsletter writing**
A writer wants to know if AI speeds up her newsletter production. Hypothesis: "Using AI for research synthesis before writing will reduce my total newsletter production time from 4 hours to 2.5 hours without reducing reader response rates." Experiment: 4 weeks alternating, measuring total time and reader reply rate (her subjective quality proxy). Result: production time dropped from 4 to 3.2 hours with AI (not 2.5 — the hypothesis was off), but reader replies increased slightly, suggesting the research phase she'd previously rushed was now more thorough. Conclusion: AI for research synthesis is worth continuing; adjust time expectations.

**Example 2: Testing focus strategies for deep work**
A software engineer thinks his best code quality comes from 4-hour focus sessions, but he's not sure if that's actually true or if it's just a story he's telling himself. Hypothesis: "My code review acceptance rate is higher when a single session produces the code vs. when it's produced across multiple 1-hour blocks." Experiment: 3 weeks of 4-hour blocks, 3 weeks of 1-hour blocks (matched for total time), tracking code review feedback. Result: surprising — quality was similar, but he felt worse working in 4-hour blocks because of the pressure. Conclusion: multi-session shorter work is preferable given equal quality. He updated a belief he'd held for years.

## Anti-Patterns

**Running experiments without hypotheses.** "I'm going to try this for a week" without a clear expectation produces no learning. You finish the week, you have a vague impression, and you draw a conclusion based on that impression rather than evidence. This is how people confirm whatever they were already inclined to believe.

**Changing too many variables at once.** If you switch your task management system, your morning routine, and your AI tool usage simultaneously, you can't determine which change produced which effect. Change one thing at a time. The impatience to improve everything at once is the enemy of learning anything.

**Interpreting early discomfort as failure.** New approaches feel awkward for days or weeks. The discomfort of newness is not the same as evidence that something doesn't work. Distinguish between "this feels uncomfortable because it's new" and "this is producing worse outcomes than what I was doing before." The first is expected; only the second is evidence against continuing.
