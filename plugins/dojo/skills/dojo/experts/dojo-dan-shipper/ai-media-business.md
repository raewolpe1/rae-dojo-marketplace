## Core Concept

The conventional media business bet in the early internet era was: build audience, then monetize via advertising. This model worked when attention was scarce and distribution was hard. Both of those conditions are reversing. Attention is still scarce, but distribution is now free. And AI is making content creation nearly free too, which destroys the scarcity economics that drove advertising CPMs.

The media business model that survives this environment is: build audience by demonstrating specific expertise and distinctive perspective, then sell that expertise and perspective directly to the people who value it most. This is the subscription model, and it works because what subscribers are paying for is not the information itself (which AI can increasingly reproduce) but the curation, synthesis, and point of view that the writer brings.

Every.to, which Dan Shipper co-founded, is a deliberate experiment in this model. Rather than a single newsletter with a single author, it's a bundle of writers who each have deep expertise in adjacent domains — AI, startups, psychology, business strategy. The bundle economics work because some readers come for one writer and discover others; shared infrastructure reduces each writer's overhead; and the brand association of the bundle raises each writer's perceived credibility.

The AI angle is central to the Every.to model in a specific way: they write about AI tools and workflows as their core subject matter, which means their readers are the people most interested in understanding and adopting AI in knowledge work. This creates a natural product-media flywheel: readers value the coverage because it helps them work better, and that trust makes them receptive to any software tools the company builds. This is a version of the media-as-distribution-channel for software products, which is a durable model when done with genuine editorial integrity.

The risk to AI-era media businesses is the same as the risk to all AI-adjacent businesses: if your core value proposition is information delivery and your readers can get equivalent information from AI directly, your subscription business collapses. The defense is specificity of perspective — the writer's distinctive view on their subject, accumulated through years of direct experience, is not reproducible by AI trained on generic web content.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify your specific epistemic angle.** What do you know that most writers in your space don't, and what is your distinctive interpretation of that knowledge? This angle must be specific enough that readers can't find it elsewhere — "writing about AI" is not specific enough; "writing about how AI changes internal knowledge management in enterprise software companies from the perspective of a former CTO" is getting there.

2. **Build toward a subscriber model early.** Even if you start free, build email capture and subscriber relationships from the beginning. Advertising-dependent media businesses are increasingly fragile; the direct subscriber relationship is more durable.

3. **Write about your actual work and experiments.** The most defensible content in an AI-abundant world is first-person experience. AI can synthesize others' experiences; it cannot replicate yours. The more your content is grounded in what you personally did, tried, learned, and concluded, the harder it is to commoditize.

4. **Consider the product-media flywheel explicitly.** If you're building software, your media presence should be in the exact space your software serves. If you're building media, be aware of what software products your audience might naturally want, because you have distribution for them.

5. **Invest in the bundle logic.** Writing alone is increasingly a high-effort, lower-monetization path. Combining with other writers with adjacent expertise — sharing infrastructure, cross-promoting audiences, raising collective credibility — improves the economics for everyone involved.

## Examples

**Example 1: The solo newsletter writer considering expansion**
A newsletter writer with 8,000 subscribers covers personal finance for freelancers. She's considering how to grow. The AI media business analysis: her specific angle (personal finance *for freelancers*, not general personal finance) is her moat. The threat is if she broadens to cover general personal finance — that's where commoditization lives. Her options are: go deeper into the specific niche (higher value, smaller total market) or find 2-3 writers covering adjacent freelancer topics (taxes, contracts, client management) to form a bundle. The bundle expands her addressable market without diluting the niche specificity that protects her.

**Example 2: The software founder considering media**
A founder building a project management tool for design teams is considering a content strategy. The AI media business framing: don't write generic "how to manage design projects" content — AI already produces that at scale and it won't rank or convert well. Instead, write extensively about the specific, lived experience of design team management at a granular level: what breaks down at 5 vs. 15 vs. 50 person teams, specific organizational patterns that emerge, the cultural dynamics of design-engineering collaboration. This content attracts exactly the people who would pay for her product, and it builds credibility through specificity that AI-generated content cannot match.

## Anti-Patterns

**Competing on volume.** Publishing more frequently to stay relevant is a losing strategy when AI can produce unlimited content. Frequency is not the value; perspective and specificity are. A newsletter that publishes once every two weeks with a distinctive point of view will outperform one that publishes daily with generic takes.

**Building on platforms you don't own.** Social media followers are rented audiences. The algorithm changes, the platform changes, and the relationship is mediated by a third party that can deplatform you or degrade your reach. The email list and the direct subscriber relationship are the durable assets. Build those first, everything else second.

**Confusing traffic with business.** High traffic without a subscription or product revenue model is a distribution asset without a monetization model. In an AI-content-abundant world, traffic itself is worth less than it used to be. The question is not how many people read your content but how many people value it enough to pay for it directly.
