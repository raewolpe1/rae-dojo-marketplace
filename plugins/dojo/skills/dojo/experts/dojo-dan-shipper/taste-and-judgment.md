## When to Use

Use this file when the user asks about:
- How to develop better judgment in creative or intellectual work
- The role of taste in the AI era
- How to evaluate AI outputs and know when they're good enough
- Why taste is hard to systematize
- The difference between knowing what good looks like and being able to produce it

## Fails When

- The user wants a framework for systematically improving taste — taste doesn't work that way
- The user is asking about aesthetic taste in art rather than functional taste in knowledge work

## Core Concept

There is a famous gap in creative development: for years, your taste exceeds your ability to produce. You know what's good before you can make something good. This is what Ira Glass described when he talked about young creative professionals — they have enough taste to recognize that their own work isn't very good, which is often what makes them quit before they develop the skill to close the gap.

AI inverts part of this problem but doesn't solve it. With AI, production is now cheap. You can generate many outputs quickly. But the evaluation problem remains: you still need to know what good looks like to select and refine the best outputs from the AI. Taste — knowing what good looks like — becomes more important, not less, as production fluency increases.

In the AI era, taste has two expressions: selection taste (knowing which outputs to keep) and direction taste (knowing how to prompt, guide, and constrain AI to produce better outputs in the first place). Both require a developed sensibility that AI itself cannot provide. The person with better taste uses AI to produce better work; the person with worse taste uses AI to produce worse work faster.

Taste in knowledge work — as distinct from aesthetic taste — is the ability to recognize which questions are worth asking, which arguments are actually compelling vs. superficially compelling, which analyses are genuinely illuminating vs. just technically correct, and which ideas are worth developing vs. abandoning. This kind of taste is built through exposure, practice, and reflection, and it cannot be shortcut.

The practical consequence for AI-era knowledge workers is: develop your taste actively, not incidentally. Read excellent examples of the kind of work you want to do. Analyze them: what makes them good? Where does the quality come from? Is it the structure, the evidence, the voice, the argument, the examples? Make your analysis of quality explicit rather than leaving it as a vague impression. This is the active cultivation of taste rather than the passive accumulation of it.

## How to Apply

1. **Collect examples of work you admire and analyze them explicitly.** What is it, specifically, that makes each piece good? Try to move beyond "it just works" to a specific claim: "this essay is effective because it opens with a concrete scene that embeds the abstract question, so the reader is already engaged before the argument starts." Explicit analysis is how vague taste becomes transferable judgment.

2. **Practice comparative evaluation.** Generate multiple versions of something — a paragraph, an argument, a structure — and rank them. Then articulate why the better one is better. This builds the habit of explicit quality assessment rather than intuitive selection.

3. **Use AI to generate contrast cases.** When you're not sure why one version is better than another, ask AI to generate a deliberately weaker version ("now make this argument in a more generic, less precise way"). Comparison against a known-worse version often reveals what makes the better version work.

4. **Develop your production to match your taste.** The taste-production gap is painful but generative. Rather than suppressing the gap (accepting lower quality) or abandoning (giving up because quality feels unachievable), use the gap as a guide: what specifically is missing from your current production that your taste identifies? That specific gap is what to practice.

5. **Protect your taste from contamination.** Consuming large amounts of mediocre work calibrates your taste downward. Be selective about what you read and watch, not in a gatekeeping sense but in a quality-calibration sense. Your baseline for "good" is set by what you regularly expose yourself to.

## Examples

**Example 1: The newsletter writer with inconsistent quality**
A newsletter writer produces some pieces she's proud of and some she knows are weak, but she can't reliably predict which will be which in advance. The taste-and-judgment diagnosis: she has selection taste (she can tell the good ones afterward) but her direction taste is underdeveloped (she can't guide the creative process reliably toward good outputs). Intervention: she takes her three best pieces and three worst pieces, and writes a detailed analysis of each — what specifically made the good ones work, what specifically made the weak ones fail. She now has an explicit quality model she can apply during production rather than only after.

**Example 2: The AI-assisted analyst producing mediocre work**
An analyst uses AI to draft his reports and is producing more output but isn't seeing quality improvements. He asks his AI dojo: "Why isn't my work getting better even though I'm using AI?" The taste diagnosis: he's using AI as a speed tool for a task where speed wasn't the bottleneck. His taste and judgment — what questions to ask, what arguments to make, what evidence to weight — haven't improved. Intervention: for his next report, before using AI, he spends 30 minutes writing out in his own words what a genuinely excellent version of this analysis would contain. He then uses AI to help execute that vision rather than to generate the vision for him.

## Anti-Patterns

**Delegating taste decisions to AI.** Asking AI "is this good?" or "which of these is better?" gets you AI's surface pattern-matching on quality, not a cultivated aesthetic judgment. Use AI for execution; exercise your own judgment on evaluation.

**Accepting good-enough because production is cheap.** The abundance of AI-generated output creates a psychological trap: since you can produce many versions quickly, stopping at the first acceptable version feels efficient. But in domains where quality matters, accepting acceptable is a form of taste atrophy. The standard should be "genuinely good" not "good enough to ship."

**Confusing familiarity with quality.** Work that sounds like good work — uses the right vocabulary, follows recognizable patterns, cites appropriate sources — is not the same as work that actually is good. This is the central failure mode of AI-generated content and of people whose taste has been calibrated by familiar forms rather than genuine excellence. The question is not "does this sound right" but "does this actually work."
