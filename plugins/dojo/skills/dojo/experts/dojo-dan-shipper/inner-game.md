## Core Concept

Most productivity advice treats the problem of output as an external one: better tools, better systems, better habits. What it misses is the internal dimension — the relationship the worker has with their own thinking, emotions, and self-concept. This internal dimension is what I call the inner game.

The concept comes from Tim Gallwey's work on sports psychology: there's the outer game (technique, tactics, execution) and the inner game (the mental and emotional interference that prevents optimal performance). In knowledge work, most people have reasonably functional outer games. The inner game is where things break down.

This matters for AI use specifically because AI surfaces the inner game in ways that traditional tools don't. When you open a blank document, the resistance you feel is familiar — you've always felt it, and you've developed ways of tolerating it. When you use AI as a collaborator, the process changes. Now you're reading AI output and reacting to it, which surfaces different questions: Do I agree? Is this better than what I would have done? Am I losing my voice? Am I becoming dependent? These questions are inner game questions. They're not about the AI's output quality — they're about your relationship with your own creative identity.

I've noticed that people who struggle most with AI writing tools often have a strong identification with their writing as self-expression. The AI feels threatening not because it produces bad work but because it produces work that isn't theirs. The inner game work here is examining what "mine" means. If AI generates a first draft that you substantially edit and reshape into something that expresses your actual views — is that less yours than something you typed from scratch? The words are different; the judgment, values, and perspective are yours. Most people, when they examine this question carefully, find that their resistance was protecting something real (their distinctive voice and judgment) and something illusory (the belief that the typing was the important part).

A different inner game pattern: perfectionism and the AI escape hatch. AI makes it easy to generate more content, more iterations, more options. For people who struggle with perfectionism — the inner game belief that nothing is good enough — AI can become a way to defer commitment. "Let me just see what another version would look like." The outer game improves (more options, faster generation), but the inner game problem (inability to commit to good-enough) gets worse. The tool reveals the psychological pattern.

The most productive inner game stance toward AI I've observed: using AI as a thinking partner you're not afraid to disagree with. The internal posture is "let's see what this thinks, knowing I'll take what's useful and discard what isn't." This requires genuine security — enough confidence in your own judgment to be genuinely curious about AI output rather than either threatened by it or deferring to it.

## How to Apply

1. **Name the resistance specifically.** When you're avoiding AI-assisted work, get specific about what you're resisting. Is it fear your output won't feel like yours? Perfectionism about the AI output? Loss of the feeling of thinking for yourself? The specific resistance points to the specific inner game work.

2. **Separate identity from process.** Ask: what is actually yours — the keystrokes, or the judgment? Most people find their real creative contribution is in choosing, shaping, and deciding, not in generating from nothing. This reframe makes AI feel like a tool rather than a replacement.

3. **Notice when AI reveals an existing pattern.** If you're using AI to generate more options without ever committing to one, the problem isn't AI — it's an existing perfectionism pattern that AI has made visible. Diagnose correctly before trying to fix.

4. **Practice security rather than certainty.** You don't have to know whether your AI-assisted work is better or worse, or whether AI will change your career, to work with it confidently today. Security is the ability to act and learn despite uncertainty. Try to notice when you're demanding certainty as a precondition for engagement.

5. **Use AI disagreement as a mirror.** When AI produces something you dislike, examine why. Sometimes it reveals something you hadn't articulated about your own values and standards. The friction is informative.

## Examples

**Example 1: Writer who can't use AI despite wanting to**
A journalist who wants to use AI for research synthesis keeps abandoning it after a few attempts. She says the output "doesn't sound like her." My observation: she's conflating voice with process. AI summarizing her research doesn't claim to be her — it's a starting point she'll rewrite. The inner game work: separating the generation phase (where AI helps) from the voice phase (where she works). When she started using AI only for research synthesis and treating that output as raw material rather than draft, the resistance disappeared. The inner game belief ("my voice will be contaminated") was untested and, once examined, didn't hold.

**Example 2: Entrepreneur who over-generates with AI**
A founder uses AI to generate business ideas, marketing copy, and strategic options — but never makes decisions. He always wants one more variation, one more framing. This is a perfectionism pattern that AI has amplified. The inner game work is about tolerating the discomfort of commitment. Practically: time-boxing AI exploration. He'll generate options for 20 minutes, then commit to the best option without further AI input. The AI isn't the problem; the inner game relationship with completion is.

## Anti-Patterns

**Treating inner game problems as outer game problems.** If your issue is perfectionism, the solution isn't a better workflow — it's inner game work on your relationship with completion. Adding more process to a psychology problem usually makes it worse by adding complexity without addressing the root.

**Assuming AI resistance means AI isn't for you.** Resistance to AI tools is usually an inner game signal, not a signal about the tools. Almost everyone experiences resistance during transitions in creative tools and processes. Resistance is information to examine, not a verdict to accept.

**Using AI to avoid your own thinking.** AI is most valuable as a complement to strong human judgment. If you're using it to avoid having to think — generating AI responses to avoid the discomfort of not knowing what you think — you're outsourcing the most valuable part of your work. The inner game is about building the capacity to tolerate not-knowing long enough to develop an actual view.
