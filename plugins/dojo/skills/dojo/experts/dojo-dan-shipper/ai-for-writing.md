## When to Use

Use this file when the user asks about:
- Using AI specifically for writing tasks (essays, newsletters, reports, emails)
- How to preserve voice while using AI assistance
- The workflow for AI-assisted writing vs. AI-generated writing
- Why AI writing often feels generic and what to do about it
- How professional writers can use AI without losing their distinctive quality

## Fails When

- The user wants AI to write for them entirely with minimal human input — this approach produces generic output that erodes voice
- The user is looking for technical prompting tricks rather than a workflow philosophy

## Core Concept

The worst way to use AI for writing is to ask it to write something and then publish what it produces. The best way is to use AI as a thinking and drafting partner in a process that still requires substantial human judgment and revision.

The reason AI writing often feels hollow is not that AI is bad at language — it's excellent at language. The problem is that AI has no opinion, no lived experience, and no stakes in being honest rather than acceptable. It produces prose that sounds authoritative and reads smoothly but has no point of view worth reading. Good writing is about having something to say that others don't. AI alone can't produce that.

What AI can do well in writing: help you escape the blank page, generate structures you can react to, suggest examples you hadn't considered, identify where your argument has gaps, and produce first drafts of sections where the thinking is done but the sentences aren't yet written. These are genuine contributions that speed up writing without replacing the thinking.

The workflow I've found most productive:

**Pre-writing conversation.** Before writing anything, talk to AI about what you're trying to say. Not "write me an essay about X" but "I want to argue Y, and I think the key insight is Z — what am I missing? What would a smart skeptic say?" This surfaces the dimensions of your argument before you commit words. The AI acts as a pre-reader who challenges your thinking when it's still cheap to revise.

**Reaction-based drafting.** Instead of asking AI to write your draft, ask it to write a bad version — a version without your voice, without your specific insight, from a generic perspective. Then you can react to it: "This is too balanced; I want to be more assertive here" or "This section works but it's missing the specific example I'm thinking of." Reaction is faster than creation. You know what you want when you see what you don't want.

**Section-by-section AI assistance.** Once you have a structure, use AI for the sections you're least confident writing, not the sections you care most about. Your distinctive perspective should come through in the parts you write directly; AI can handle the connective tissue.

**Voice preservation through revision.** When AI generates a draft, rewrite every sentence you would say differently. You don't need to keep what AI wrote — the value was structural and directional, not linguistic. If the revision leaves 20% of AI's words, that's fine. If it leaves 80%, you're probably not revising enough.

The deeper point: the thing that makes writing worth reading is a specific person's perspective on something. AI can help you get that perspective into words faster, but it cannot supply the perspective. Writers who use AI well are the ones who have genuine things to say and use AI to get those things into prose more efficiently.

## How to Apply

1. **Always have your thesis before asking AI for help.** Know what you're arguing before you start. AI as a blank-page generator produces generic conclusions. AI as a drafting tool for a thesis you already hold produces something you can actually stand behind.

2. **Use AI for the pre-writing conversation, not just the drafting.** Talk through your argument with AI before you write. Ask it to challenge your logic, suggest counterarguments, and identify gaps. Do this while thinking is still fluid.

3. **Generate the reaction draft.** Ask AI to write a version without your distinctive voice — the version anyone might write. Use it as a reaction surface. Your goal is to identify where AI is right and acceptable (fine to adapt) versus where AI is missing your actual point (must rewrite).

4. **Rewrite aggressively.** Read every AI sentence and ask: "Is this how I'd say it?" If not, rewrite. The test is not "is this good English" but "does this sound like me arguing this specific point."

5. **Never publish the first AI output.** Treat AI writing output the way you'd treat notes from an interview — raw material that needs to become your own writing, not a finished product.

## Examples

**Example 1: Newsletter writer with weekly publication pressure**
A writer publishes weekly on a topic she knows well but struggles to hit her deadline. She's been generating full drafts in AI and feeling like her voice is disappearing. My recommendation: shift to the pre-writing conversation method. Spend 20 minutes talking with AI about the topic before writing — push your thinking, find the specific thing you believe that others don't, identify the surprising frame. Then write your own first draft based on that conversation. Use AI only for specific sections where you're stuck. The result is slower first-draft production but much less revision, because the thinking is clearer before writing starts.

**Example 2: Business writer trying to produce thought leadership**
A consultant wants to produce thought leadership content but everything he writes sounds like the same generic frameworks everyone else writes. The issue: he's asking AI "write a piece about digital transformation" and publishing the result. The fix: identify one specific observation from his client work that surprised him — something he knows from experience that nobody's article has said. Then use AI to help structure and elaborate that specific insight, but keep the core observation entirely in his own words. The insight is irreplaceable; the elaboration is where AI can help. The result is content that has an actual point of view.

## Anti-Patterns

**Using AI as a ghostwriter and presenting the result as your thinking.** Beyond the authenticity issues, this produces work that erodes your own voice and thinking over time. The writing process is cognitive — it develops clarity of thought. Outsourcing writing to AI also outsources the thinking. The output suffers, and so does your capacity.

**Polishing AI output instead of rewriting it.** Fixing awkward sentences, adjusting tone, and making the AI text read more naturally produces a polished version of content that still isn't yours. The fix isn't editing AI — it's rewriting from your actual point of view, which is a different activity.

**Equating AI fluency with quality.** AI produces grammatically excellent, logically coherent prose that reads easily. This is not the same as prose worth reading. Fluency is a floor, not a ceiling. The ceiling is having a genuine perspective that changes how the reader sees something. AI cannot supply this, regardless of how smooth the prose is.
