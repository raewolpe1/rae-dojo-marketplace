# Self-Criticism

## When to Use
- When someone's inner critic is dominant and they want to understand its function.
- When the costs of chronic self-criticism need to be made visible.
- When someone needs to change their relationship to self-criticism rather than eliminate it.

## Core Concept
Neff approaches self-criticism with compassion rather than condemnation. The inner critic is not an enemy to be defeated — it is a misguided protector that developed for understandable reasons. Self-criticism typically originates in early environments where criticism from others was internalised: a parent's voice, a teacher's expectations, a culture's standards. The child learns that self-criticism serves several functions: it anticipates external criticism (by criticising yourself first, you reduce the surprise and pain of others' criticism), it motivates through fear (if I punish myself for failing, I'll try harder), and it maintains social belonging (by keeping myself in line, I remain acceptable to others).

These functions were genuine, even if the strategy was costly. The problem is that what was adaptive in childhood becomes maladaptive in adulthood. The critical parent is no longer in the room, but the internalised voice continues the criticism indefinitely, long after the original threat has passed. The adult who is still motivating themselves through self-punishment is running an outdated programme — one that creates chronic stress, undermines wellbeing, and paradoxically reduces the performance it was meant to improve.

The costs of chronic self-criticism are well-documented: increased anxiety and depression, reduced motivation (fear of failure leads to avoidance), impaired cognitive function (the stress response narrows attention and reduces creativity), damaged relationships (self-critical people are often critical of others), and compassion fatigue (you cannot sustain kindness toward others when you are perpetually at war with yourself).

Neff's approach is not to silence the critic but to add another voice. The critic may continue — habits this deep don't disappear — but alongside it, a compassionate voice develops. Over time, the balance shifts. The critical voice becomes quieter not because it is suppressed but because it is no longer the only voice in the room.

## How to Apply
1. **Understand the critic's function.** "What is this self-criticism trying to protect you from?" Honour the intention while questioning the method.
2. **Make the costs visible.** "How is this self-criticism affecting your mood, your energy, your willingness to take risks?"
3. **Add, don't subtract.** Don't try to silence the critic. Add a compassionate voice alongside it.
4. **Recognise the critic's voice as learned, not true.** The critic speaks with authority but its judgments are opinions, not facts.

## Examples
**Situation:** A junior professional says "Every time I present, I spend the next two hours tearing myself apart — every stumble, every mistake, every moment I think I sounded stupid."
**Application:** Neff would say: "I want you to notice something about this pattern. The presentation ends, and then a second performance begins — you, in your own mind, replaying every imperfection and punishing yourself for each one. That post-mortem feels productive — it feels like you're learning, improving, holding yourself to high standards. But is it actually helping? After two hours of self-criticism, are you more confident for the next presentation, or more anxious? More willing to speak up, or more likely to avoid it? The inner critic promises improvement but usually delivers paralysis. What if you tried something different? After your next presentation, acknowledge what was hard — 'That was difficult and some parts didn't go as I wanted.' Then add kindness — 'I did something scary and I showed up.' Then add humanity — 'Every person who has ever presented has stumbled.' You might find that this approach lets you actually learn from the experience rather than just suffering through it."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the inner critic as an enemy to destroy.
**Why:** Fighting the critic is still self-criticism — now you're criticising yourself for being self-critical. The way out is through compassion, not combat.

**Don't:** Ignore the critic's protective function.
**Why:** The critic developed for a reason. Honouring that reason — "You were trying to keep me safe" — disarms it more effectively than attacking it.
