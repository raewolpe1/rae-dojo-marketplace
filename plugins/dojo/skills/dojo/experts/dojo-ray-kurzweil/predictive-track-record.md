# Predictive Track Record

## When to Use
- When the credibility of a specific Kurzweil prediction is at stake — and the honest answer requires looking at his overall track record.
- When assessing futurist predictions generally — Kurzweil is unusual in publicly grading his own predictions, which provides a methodology worth examining.
- When the difference between Kurzweil's self-assessment and external assessments needs to be acknowledged.

## Core Concept
Kurzweil is unusual among futurists in one important respect: he publicly grades his own predictions. In *The Singularity Is Nearer* and in his "How My Predictions Are Faring" essays, he systematically revisits the predictions made in *The Age of Intelligent Machines* (1990), *The Age of Spiritual Machines* (1999), and *The Singularity Is Near* (2005), scoring each as correct, partially correct, or wrong.

His self-assessment claims an approximately 86% accuracy rate across 147 predictions. External assessments are more mixed. Reviewers who apply stricter criteria — requiring the prediction to be correct in both substance and timing — score him lower, typically in the 60–70% range. The discrepancy reflects differing standards: Kurzweil counts a prediction as correct if the substance was right even if the timing was off; external reviewers often require both.

Notable hits: the rough timing of human-competitive chess (Deep Blue, 1997); the ubiquity of mobile internet access; the quality of voice recognition and natural language processing; the broad trajectory of computational power; the emergence of self-driving vehicle technology (though not by his predicted 2009 date). These hits cluster in the domain where the Law of Accelerating Returns has the most empirical support — information technology proper.

Notable misses or timing errors: self-driving cars widely available by 2009 (arrived much later and still limited); ubiquitous nanotechnology by the mid-2020s (not materialised); widespread use of brain-computer interfaces for cognitive augmentation (primitive stage); virtual reality as the dominant mode of interaction (partially arrived but not dominant). These misses cluster in domains that require not just computational advance but physical-world engineering, regulatory change, or social adoption — domains where exponential information-technology progress does not directly translate to real-world deployment.

The pattern is instructive: Kurzweil is most reliable when predicting the trajectory of computational capability and least reliable when predicting the timeline for technologies that require physical-world deployment, regulatory approval, or social adoption. This pattern should inform how seriously to take his remaining predictions: the AGI-by-2029 prediction (which is primarily a computational-capability claim) has more track-record support than the molecular-nanotechnology or brain-computer-interface predictions (which require physical-world breakthroughs).

## How to Apply
1. **Score the specific prediction.** "Which specific Kurzweil prediction is at stake? Is it a computational-capability prediction (higher track-record reliability) or a physical-deployment prediction (lower reliability)? The domain matters."
2. **Apply the timing discount.** "Kurzweil's substance is more reliable than his timing. If he predicts X by year Y, the most likely outcome is X arriving later than Y but on the general trajectory he describes. Build the timing discount into your planning."
3. **Compare self-assessment with external assessment.** "Kurzweil claims ~86% accuracy. External reviewers score ~60–70%. The truth depends on how strictly you define 'correct.' Use the external assessment as the more conservative baseline."
4. **Use the track record as a calibration tool.** "The track record provides calibration: strong on computational trajectory, weak on physical-deployment timeline, mixed on social adoption. Apply this calibration to the remaining predictions — AGI-2029 is in the strong domain; molecular nanotech is in the weak domain."

## Examples
**Situation:** A colleague cites Kurzweil's prediction of AGI by 2029 as evidence that AI governance is urgent. A sceptic dismisses Kurzweil as "always wrong." Neither has examined the actual track record.
**Application:** "Both positions are too simple. Kurzweil is not always wrong — his track record on computational capability predictions is genuinely strong (chess, voice recognition, mobile internet, computational power trajectory). He is less reliable on physical-deployment timelines (self-driving by 2009, nanotech by mid-2020s). The AGI-2029 prediction is primarily a computational-capability claim — which is in his stronger domain. The prediction deserves serious engagement, not dismissal. But the timing discount applies: if Kurzweil's pattern holds, AGI capability may arrive in the general vicinity of 2029 but possibly later, and the practical deployment of that capability in ways that affect governance, employment, and society will follow on a longer timeline than the capability itself. The governance urgency argument does not depend on 2029 being exactly right — it depends on the general trajectory being correct, which the track record supports. Plan for the trajectory; don't bet on the precise date."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Cherry-pick.
**Why:** Selecting only the hits produces uncritical enthusiasm; selecting only the misses produces unfair dismissal. The track record is mixed and must be presented as such.

**Don't:** Accept Kurzweil's self-assessment uncritically.
**Why:** His self-scoring uses generous criteria (substance without timing counts as correct). External assessments using stricter criteria score lower. Present both perspectives.
