# Teaching Entrepreneurial Expertise

## When to Use
- Designing or reviewing any entrepreneurship curriculum, BDS offering, or accelerator.
- When the programme's culminating deliverable is a business plan.
- When asked how entrepreneurial capability is actually built.

## Fails When
- **The question is measured impact.** Route to McKenzie. He has the evidence on what training does; Sarasvathy has the argument about what it should contain.

## Core Concept

**The artefact you require determines the logic you teach.** This is the single most useful diagnostic for a curriculum.

If the culminating deliverable is a **business plan with a forecast**, the programme has required prediction as an input. It has therefore taught causal logic — and where the founders face uncertain conditions, it has taught them the logic of the *non*-expert, assessed them on the persuasiveness of numbers they could not know, and selected for the participants best at inventing them.

**The expert sequence is inverted relative to the standard curriculum.**

| Standard (causal) | Expert (effectual) |
|---|---|
| Identify opportunity | Inventory means |
| Research market | Act within affordable loss |
| Write plan | Attract self-selecting stakeholders |
| Raise resources | Let commitments reshape the goal |
| Execute | Repeat |

Note what the inversion does: the standard sequence **starts with the goal and produces action**; the expert sequence **starts with action and produces the goal**. Under uncertainty the first cannot work, because the goal was set with information nobody had.

**The replacement artefacts.** Three, all verifiable, all achievable at zero capital:
1. **Means inventory** — who you are, what you know, whom you know.
2. **Affordable loss statement** — what can be committed and lost without ruin. This is the budget, and it is not derived from a forecast.
3. **Evidence of one real commitment** — a named person or organisation who has committed something, and an account of how the venture changed as a result.

**Expertise is built by doing under supervision, not by content transfer.** A curriculum that teaches content and assesses recall is not building expertise in any domain, entrepreneurship included. The protocol studies were of people who had *built things*; the expertise lives in the decisions, and it is acquired by making them with feedback.

**The bridge to the evidence.** McKenzie's Togo trial found that psychology-based **personal initiative** training — teaching a proactive, self-starting mindset and behaviours — raised profits by 30% while a leading traditional business training programme produced a statistically insignificant 11%, in the same trial, in the same country, at the same time. That is not a test of effectuation, and it should not be presented as one. But it is the closest experimental evidence available that **teaching a way of acting outperforms teaching accounting content** — which is the direction this file argues for, and it is worth naming honestly as adjacent support rather than proof.

## How to Apply

1. **Look at the deliverable first.** It tells you which logic the programme teaches, regardless of what the syllabus says.
2. **Match artefact to conditions.** Predictable → plan. Uncertain → means, affordable loss, commitment.
3. **Assess decisions, not documents.** What did they commit? Who joined? How did the goal move?
4. **Build in doing with feedback.** Content plus recall does not produce expertise in any domain.
5. **Check the assessment panel.** If they can only score forecasts, the curriculum cannot change until they can score commitments.
6. **Cite McKenzie honestly** — adjacent evidence, not proof.

## Examples

**Situation:** A BDS curriculum runs five days, teaches record-keeping, marketing and financial planning, and culminates in a business plan.

**Application:** The deliverable is causal, so the curriculum is causal, whatever the marketing says. For founders in uncertain conditions it teaches the novice's logic. Redesign: replace the plan with the three effectual artefacts; restructure the five days around making real commitments in the field with feedback rather than around content delivery; and retrain the assessors, because a panel that can only score a forecast will fail every effectual founder in front of it. Note separately — and this is McKenzie's point, not mine — that a five-day course changes roughly one business practice in twenty regardless of its content, so the redesign is necessary but the dose may still be too small to matter.

**Result:** A curriculum that teaches the logic experts actually use — with an honest caveat about whether any curriculum of that length changes anything.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Require a business plan by default.
**Why:** It requires prediction as an input and therefore teaches predictive logic. It is a choice, not a neutral requirement.

**Don't:** Teach content and assess recall.
**Why:** No domain builds expertise that way. The protocols were of people who had built things.

**Don't:** Present effectuation as experimentally validated by McKenzie's Togo result.
**Why:** It is adjacent evidence for teaching a way of acting over teaching content. It is not a test of effectuation and claiming it is would be exactly the overclaiming this dojo is built to avoid.
