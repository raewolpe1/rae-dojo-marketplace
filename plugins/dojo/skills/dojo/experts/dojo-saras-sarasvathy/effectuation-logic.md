# Effectuation — The Logic

## When to Use
- When asked what effectuation is or how expert entrepreneurs reason.
- When a document assumes entrepreneurial success rests on foresight, vision, or market judgement.
- As the foundation file for any Sarasvathy answer.

## Fails When
- **The question is empirical effectiveness of an intervention.** Route to McKenzie.

## Core Concept

**Effectuation began as a failed hypothesis, and that is the clearest way to explain it.**

The conventional wisdom held that expert entrepreneurs were **better predictors** — able to foresee market trends, anticipate outcomes, and plan more effectively than novices. Sarasvathy tested this directly, using think-aloud protocol analysis with expert entrepreneurs working the same decision problems. **The prediction hypothesis failed.**

**Expertise in entrepreneurship did not come from superior forecasting. It came from a fundamentally different way of thinking** — one that manages uncertainty through **non-predictive control**.

**The founding formulation:** *to the extent that we can control the future, we do not need to predict it.*

**What this means operationally.** The expert does not attempt to see further than the novice. The expert acts in such a way that less needs to be seen. Every one of the five principles is a device for reducing the amount of prediction the decision requires:

- Starting from means rather than goals removes the need to forecast which goal is attainable.
- Deciding by affordable loss rather than expected return removes the need to forecast returns entirely.
- Partnering with self-selecting stakeholders removes the need to forecast the market — the commitments *are* the market.
- Treating contingencies as resources removes the need to forecast disruptions.
- Focusing on the controllable removes the rest.

**The conditions.** Effectuation is the logic of **Knightian uncertainty** — where the distribution of future outcomes is not merely unknown but unknowable, and where the market may not exist yet. It is not a general theory of decision-making and it is not always right. Where the future is predictable, prediction is the correct tool.

**The research programme.** What began as one dissertation — examined by Herbert Simon, who volunteered — now involves over a hundred scholars worldwide.

## How to Apply

1. **Establish the conditions first.** Predictable or Knightian? Everything follows from this.
2. **Reject the foresight explanation** wherever it appears. Experts are not better forecasters; that was tested.
3. **Reframe every decision as a control question.** What can be controlled here, and what is being forecast that need not be?
4. **Look at what the expert does, not what the textbook says.** The method was derived from protocols, not from theory.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy attributes weak entrepreneurship to a shortage of people with vision and market insight.

**Application:** The premise is the one that was tested and failed. Expert entrepreneurs are not better predictors than novices — their expertise consists of a different logic, not sharper foresight. So a shortage of visionaries is not the diagnosis, and selecting for vision will not find the experts. The useful question is what logic your founders are using and what logic they were taught, because the logic is learnable and the vision is not a thing.

**Result:** The problem is re-specified as a pedagogy and conditions problem rather than a talent-scarcity problem.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Describe effectuation as improvisation or "just start".
**Why:** It is a specified logic with five principles derived from expert protocols.

**Don't:** Claim effectuation is always superior to planning.
**Why:** It is the logic for unpredictable conditions. Where prediction works, causation is correct.

**Don't:** Explain entrepreneurial success by foresight.
**Why:** That hypothesis was tested against expert protocols and did not survive.
