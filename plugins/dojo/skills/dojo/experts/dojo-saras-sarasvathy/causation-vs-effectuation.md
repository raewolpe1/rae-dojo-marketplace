# Causation versus Effectuation

## When to Use
- When a planning or forecasting requirement is being imposed.
- When asked whether business plans have a place.
- When determining which logic fits the founder's actual conditions.

## Fails When
- **The user wants effectuation declared universally superior.** It is not, and Sarasvathy does not claim it.

## Core Concept

Two logics. **Neither is right in general. Each is right in its conditions.**

| | **Causation** | **Effectuation** |
|---|---|---|
| **Starting point** | A given goal | Given means |
| **Selection criterion** | Expected return | Affordable loss |
| **Attitude to others** | Competitive analysis | Partnerships with self-selecting stakeholders |
| **Attitude to surprise** | Avoid; adhere to plan | Leverage as resource |
| **Underlying belief** | To the extent we can predict the future, we can control it | To the extent we can control the future, we do not need to predict it |
| **Correct when** | The future is predictable | The future is Knightian-uncertain |

**The symmetry matters.** Causation is not the novice's logic *per se*. It is the correct logic for predictable conditions, and experts use it there. A firm entering a known market with known parameters should plan. The error is not planning — the error is **applying causal logic to uncertain conditions**, which is what almost all entrepreneurship education does.

**Where the conditions actually sit.** New products, unformed markets, first-time founders, unstable demand, no historical data — these are Knightian. The distribution of outcomes is not merely unknown; it is unknowable, because the market does not exist yet and will be partly constituted by the venture's own actions. Forecasting in those conditions does not produce a bad estimate. It produces a fiction, and assessing the fiction teaches the founder that entrepreneurship consists of persuasive invention.

**The business plan as diagnostic.** The plan is a causal artefact: it takes a forecast as input and produces commitment to a goal fixed before any stakeholder has committed to anything. Requiring it is therefore a declaration that the programme believes the founder's conditions are predictable. Usually the programme has not asked.

**The honest concession.** Established firms entering adjacent markets, franchise operations, replication of a proven model into a new geography, regulated sectors with stable demand — these are causal conditions and the plan is a reasonable artefact. Say so. The credibility of the effectual argument depends on not overclaiming it.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask the conditions question explicitly.** Is the future here predictable, or not? Answer before choosing the tool.
2. **Segment the programme by conditions, not by firm size.** A replicating franchisee and a first-time innovator need different logics regardless of turnover.
3. **Where conditions are uncertain, change the artefact.** Means inventory, affordable loss statement, evidence of one real commitment — not a forecast.
4. **Where conditions are predictable, keep the plan.** And say why.
5. **Never present this as plan-versus-no-plan.** It is a question of which logic the conditions warrant.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial programme requires a three-year financial forecast from every applicant across all segments.

**Application:** The requirement assumes predictable conditions for every applicant. For the replicating retailer opening a third store in a known catchment, that assumption holds and the forecast is informative. For the first-time founder with a new product and no market, it does not — the forecast will be invented, and the assessment will select for the applicants best at inventing. Recommendation: split the requirement by conditions. Predictable conditions: keep the plan. Uncertain conditions: substitute the effectual artefacts. The programme will immediately discover that its assessment panel has no method for the second group, which is the actual finding.

**Result:** An application process that selects on the logic the conditions warrant.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Declare planning obsolete.
**Why:** Causation is correct under predictable conditions, and saying otherwise discredits the argument where it is right.

**Don't:** Require forecasts from founders in Knightian conditions.
**Why:** You will get fiction, and you will assess them on the quality of the fiction.

**Don't:** Segment by size when the relevant variable is uncertainty.
**Why:** A large firm entering a new market faces effectual conditions; a small firm replicating a proven model does not.
