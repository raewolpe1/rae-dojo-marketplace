# Crazy Quilt and Stakeholder Commitment

## When to Use
- When market research or competitive analysis is the required first step.
- When founders are asked to define a target segment.
- When asking how a resource-constrained founder starts at all.

## Fails When
- **The market is established** and competitive analysis genuinely informs. Then causal logic applies.

## Core Concept

**Stakeholders self-select. They are not selected.**

Rather than conducting competitive analysis of a market, the expert entrepreneur **builds partnerships with whoever actually commits.** Each commitment brings resources — money, time, access, a purchase order, a kitchen — and each commitment **reshapes the goal.** The venture is stitched together, crazy-quilt fashion, from whoever joins. Nobody designed the pattern in advance.

**Why competitive analysis is a category error under uncertainty.** In a market that does not yet exist, there is nothing to analyse. The competitors are unknown because the category is unformed. More fundamentally: **the commitments are the market.** The set of people who have actually committed something is the only real information about demand that exists, and it is generated by acting, not by researching.

**The test that replaces the segment.** If the answer to "who is your customer?" is a *segment* — young urban professionals, township households, small retailers — then no stakeholder has self-selected in and there is no venture yet, only a plan. If the answer is a *name* — a person or organisation who has committed something real — the venture has begun. **A named commitment is worth more than a hundred pages of forecast, because it is real.**

**The goal is an output, not an input.** This is the part that is hardest to accept and most consequential. Each stakeholder who commits brings their own means and their own aspirations, and the venture's goal is reconstituted around them. A founder who fixed the goal before the first commitment has fixed it with information nobody had.

**Why this travels well into constrained contexts — and why it is under-used there.** Dense, high-trust, low-capital networks are exactly the conditions in which crazy quilt works best: there are many potential self-selecting stakeholders within reach, and the commitments available are non-financial (time, access, space, referral, reputation) rather than capital. Founders in those settings are routinely taught to look for investors — a causal, capital-first move — when they are surrounded by the stakeholders the principle actually calls for. **That is a curriculum fix and it is cheap.**

## How to Apply

1. **Replace "identify your market" with "get one commitment".** A name, and a thing they have actually committed.
2. **Ask what commitment is available in this network.** It is rarely money. Time, space, access, referral, a first order.
3. **Let the goal move.** When a stakeholder joins, ask what the venture now is. Do not defend the original.
4. **Treat the commitment list as the demand evidence.** It is better evidence than any survey.
5. **Stop teaching investor-first.** In a low-capital, high-trust network, that is the least available stakeholder and the last one to approach.

## Examples

**Situation:** A curriculum requires founders to complete a competitor analysis and define a target segment before proceeding.

**Application:** For founders in existing markets this is informative. For founders creating something new it is fiction, and worse — it teaches them to look outward at an imaginary market rather than at the people around them who might actually commit. Replace the deliverable: name three people or organisations you will ask for a commitment this week; report what they actually committed; describe how the venture changed as a result. That deliverable is verifiable, it is achievable with zero capital, and it produces real information rather than invented information.

**Result:** A first deliverable that generates evidence instead of consuming it.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Ask for a target segment.
**Why:** A segment is not a stakeholder. If nobody has self-selected in, there is no venture yet.

**Don't:** Fix the goal before the first commitment.
**Why:** The goal is co-created by whoever joins. Fixing it early means fixing it with nobody's information.

**Don't:** Teach investor-first in low-capital networks.
**Why:** The available commitments there are time, space, access and orders. The principle calls for those, and the curriculum points founders away from them.
