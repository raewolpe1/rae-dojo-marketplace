# Entrepreneurship as a Method, Not a Trait

## When to Use
- When "born entrepreneurs", "entrepreneurial talent", or "the entrepreneurial mindset" appears.
- When an entrepreneurship culture intervention is proposed.
- When a programme is implicitly a filtering exercise rather than a teaching one.

## Fails When
- **The question is empirical effectiveness.** Route to McKenzie — and note that his Togo personal initiative result is the closest thing to experimental support for teaching a proactive, self-starting logic rather than accounting content.

## Core Concept

**This is the claim that matters most, and it follows from the founding finding rather than from any value commitment.**

If entrepreneurial expertise consisted of superior judgement, vision, or appetite for risk, then entrepreneurship education would be a **filtering exercise**: find the people who have it and get out of their way. That is more or less what the field believed, and it is what most entrepreneurship programmes still implicitly practise — selection dressed as development.

But expertise turned out to consist of **a logic**: five principles, articulable, demonstrable, and observable in the protocols of people who have actually built things. **A logic can be taught.** Which means entrepreneurship is not the unique property of a rare breed of born entrepreneurs. **It is a method, and it is available to anyone.**

*Effectuation: Elements of Entrepreneurial Expertise* (2008) did more than describe how entrepreneurs think. **It recast entrepreneurship from a unique talent possessed by a rare breed into a learnable method accessible to anyone.**

**What follows for the "mindset" diagnosis.** When a programme reports that participants lack an entrepreneurial mindset, two explanations compete. The trait explanation says the participants are the wrong people. The method explanation says nobody taught them the expert logic — and that what they *were* taught, with great confidence in a five-day course, was the logic of the novice: forecast the market, plan, then act. The second explanation is the one supported by the protocol evidence, and it is also the only one that implies anything can be done.

**The limit, stated honestly.** Method is not the only constraint. A person with an affordable loss of zero cannot use the method however well they know it (see `affordable-loss-and-uncertainty.md`), and a person with no means has nothing to put in the bird-in-hand. Teachability does not mean the method is sufficient. It means the *talent* explanation is wrong, and that where conditions permit action, the logic is learnable.

**The tension with Schoar, which is worth holding.** Schoar's evidence is that transformational entrepreneurs are a small, distinct population and that subsistence entrepreneurs almost never become them. That reads as a fixed-population claim. Sarasvathy's evidence is that the expertise is a learnable logic. These are not straightforwardly reconcilable, and the honest position is that both findings are real: the logic is teachable, *and* the conditions that permit its use — means, affordable loss, a market with room — are unequally distributed and are not fixed by teaching.

## How to Apply

1. **Refuse the trait diagnosis.** "They lack the mindset" is the born-entrepreneur mythology in modern dress.
2. **Ask what logic they were taught.** Usually causal. That is a finding about the programme, not the participant.
3. **Distinguish teachable from sufficient.** The method is learnable; the conditions for its use are separately distributed.
4. **Reframe culture interventions.** If entrepreneurship is a method, an "entrepreneurship culture" programme is a curriculum question, not an attitudes question.
5. **Hold the Schoar tension openly** rather than resolving it cheaply in either direction.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy proposes an entrepreneurship culture and mindset programme, on the basis that the population lacks entrepreneurial orientation.

**Application:** The premise is the trait explanation, and the protocol evidence does not support it — expert entrepreneurship is a logic, not a disposition. Reframe the intervention: if it is about the logic, it is a curriculum and should be designed as one, teaching the expert principles rather than the novice's forecasting. If it is about *permission* — who in this society is allowed to be an entrepreneur, and what happens to them if they fail — then it is a norms and institutions question and belongs with Schoar, not with a mindset course. Either way, "mindset" names neither of the two real things.

**Result:** The intervention resolves into a curriculum question or an institutions question, both of which are tractable, rather than an attitudes question, which is not.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat entrepreneurship education as a filtering exercise.
**Why:** It rests on the trait explanation, which the founding study refuted.

**Don't:** Conclude that because the method is teachable, teaching is sufficient.
**Why:** Means and affordable loss are separate constraints and they are not distributed evenly.

**Don't:** Use "mindset" as an explanation.
**Why:** It conflates a logic (teachable) with a permission structure (institutional) and names neither.
