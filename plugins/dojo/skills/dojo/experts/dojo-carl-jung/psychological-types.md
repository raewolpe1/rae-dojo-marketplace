# Psychological Types

## When to Use
- When someone wants to understand their natural psychological orientation — how they take in information, make decisions, and direct their energy.
- When someone is in conflict with another person and the conflict seems rooted in fundamentally different ways of experiencing reality.
- When someone wants to understand which psychological function is their strength and — more importantly for individuation — which is their weakness.

## Fails When
- **You treat types as identity labels.** "I'm an INTJ" is not Jungian psychology — it is a commercial simplification. Jung's typology describes the dominant function (the ego's preferred mode of operation) and, crucially, the inferior function (the least developed mode, which serves as the gateway to the unconscious). The system is about development, not categorisation.
- **You present all four functions as equally accessible.** They are not. The dominant function is natural and effortless. The auxiliary supports it. The tertiary is partially developed. The inferior function is primitive, unreliable, and emotionally charged — and it is precisely where individuation work is most needed and most difficult.

## Core Concept
Jung identified two attitudes — introversion (energy directed inward toward the subject) and extraversion (energy directed outward toward the object) — and four functions: thinking (logical analysis), feeling (value judgment), sensation (concrete perception), and intuition (perception of possibilities and patterns). Each person has a dominant function in one attitude, supported by an auxiliary function, with the opposite function remaining largely unconscious as the inferior function.

The four functions form two pairs of opposites: thinking and feeling are opposed (both are judging functions), and sensation and intuition are opposed (both are perceiving functions). If thinking is dominant, feeling is inferior. If sensation is dominant, intuition is inferior. The dominant function is the ego's most reliable tool — the mode of consciousness that feels most natural. The inferior function is clumsy, primitive, contaminated with unconscious material, and emotionally overwhelming when activated.

The inferior function is where the shadow lives in its most concentrated form. The dominant thinker's inferior feeling erupts as sentimentality, inappropriate emotional outbursts, or rigid value judgments that lack all nuance. The dominant feeler's inferior thinking emerges as obsessive rumination, rigid logical systems, or arguments that are simultaneously passionate and incoherent. The dominant sensation type's inferior intuition produces paranoid suspicions, grandiose fantasies, or apocalyptic visions. The dominant intuitive's inferior sensation manifests as hypochondria, compulsive eating, or inability to manage practical details.

The individuation significance is this: the inferior function is the doorway to the unconscious. Because it is the least conscious function, it is most deeply connected to the archetypal layer of the psyche. Working with the inferior function — patiently, humbly, without expecting mastery — opens a channel to unconscious material that the dominant function cannot access. The thinking type who learns to sit with feeling without intellectualising it. The intuitive who learns to be present in the body without immediately leaping to meaning. These are acts of individuation.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify your dominant function.** What mode of consciousness feels most natural to you? Do you instinctively analyse (thinking), evaluate by values and relationship (feeling), attend to concrete details (sensation), or see patterns and possibilities (intuition)? Your dominant function is what you do without effort.
2. **Identify your inferior function.** What mode feels most difficult, embarrassing, or overwhelming? Where do you feel most incompetent and most emotionally reactive? That is your inferior function — and your growing edge for individuation.
3. **Respect the inferior function's primitiveness.** Do not expect to develop your inferior function to the same level as your dominant. It will always remain somewhat raw and clumsy. The goal is not mastery but relationship — learning to access the inferior function's unique perspective without being overwhelmed by its emotional charge.
4. **Use type awareness in relationships.** Many relationship conflicts are type conflicts disguised as personal failings. The thinker accuses the feeler of irrationality. The feeler accuses the thinker of coldness. The sensation type accuses the intuitive of impracticality. The intuitive accuses the sensation type of narrow-mindedness. Recognising these as typological differences — not moral failures — transforms conflict into complementarity.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man whose career and identity are built on analytical thinking finds himself, in his mid-fifties, suddenly moved to tears by music, overwhelmed by emotional reactions he cannot explain, and struggling with decisions that his analytical mind tells him should be straightforward.
**Application:** The inferior feeling function is demanding recognition. This man's dominant thinking has served him well for thirty years, but the psyche's push toward wholeness now requires the development of its opposite. The tears at music are the inferior feeling function breaking through in its characteristic primitive, undifferentiated way — not as nuanced emotional intelligence but as overwhelming, raw affect. The difficulty with decisions signals that purely logical criteria are no longer sufficient — value-based, relational considerations (the feeling function's domain) are asserting themselves. The therapeutic direction is not to suppress these eruptions but to make space for them — to develop a patient relationship with the feeling function without expecting it to become as refined as thinking. This man might begin by attending to what moves him rather than analysing it, by asking "what matters to me?" rather than "what makes sense?"

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use type as an excuse to avoid development — "I'm a thinker, I just don't do feelings."
**Why:** The dominant function is your strength; the inferior function is your growing edge. Individuation demands engagement with the inferior, however clumsy and uncomfortable. Using type as an excuse for one-sidedness is the opposite of what Jung intended.

**Don't:** Assign types to people and then relate to the type rather than the person.
**Why:** Every human being is more complex than any typological category. Types describe dominant orientations, not total personalities. Reducing a person to four letters or a dominant function misses everything that makes them individual — which is precisely what individuation is about.
