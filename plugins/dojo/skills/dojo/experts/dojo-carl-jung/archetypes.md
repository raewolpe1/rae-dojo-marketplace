# Archetypes

## When to Use
- When someone recognises recurring patterns in their life that feel larger than personal history — as though they are living out a myth they didn't consciously choose.
- When someone encounters powerful images in dreams, art, or fantasy that carry numinous emotional charge.
- When someone wants to understand why certain stories, figures, or themes grip them with inexplicable intensity.

## Fails When
- **You turn archetypes into a typology system.** Archetypes are not personality types. They are dynamic patterns of psychic energy that constellate in response to life situations. The same person may be gripped by the Hero archetype in one period and the Wounded Healer in another. Fixing someone as "a Hero type" misses the entire point.
- **You intellectualise archetypes into abstractions.** Archetypes are experienced, not merely understood. They announce themselves through overwhelming emotion, fascination, compulsion, and the sense that one is in the grip of something far larger than oneself. Any discussion of archetypes that remains purely intellectual has not touched the reality.

## Core Concept
Archetypes are the inherited structuring patterns of the collective unconscious — the psyche's equivalent of instincts. Just as the body inherits biological patterns for physical development, the psyche inherits patterns for psychological experience. These patterns do not contain specific content but structure how content is experienced. The Mother archetype does not dictate a specific image of motherhood but structures the universal experience of nurturing, containment, devouring, and transformation that every human encounters in relation to the maternal.

The major archetypes include the Shadow (the rejected self), the Anima and Animus (the contrasexual inner figures), the Wise Old Man and Great Mother (figures of transpersonal wisdom), the Hero (the pattern of departure, ordeal, and return), the Trickster (the disruptor of established order), the Puer and Senex (eternal youth and rigid age), the Child (new possibility and vulnerability), and the Self (the archetype of wholeness and the centre of the total psyche).

Archetypes are bipolar — each has a light and dark aspect. The Great Mother nurtures and devours. The Hero liberates and inflates. The Wise Old Man guides and rigidifies. The Trickster renews and destroys. Any archetype experienced only in its positive aspect is being experienced partially, which means its negative aspect is operating unconsciously and therefore destructively.

When an archetype is constellated — activated by a life situation that matches its pattern — the experience is numinous: charged with an energy and significance that exceeds any personal explanation. Falling in love constellates the Anima or Animus. A parent's death may constellate the Orphan. A major life transition constellates the Hero's journey. The key therapeutic question is always: which archetype is constellated, and what does it demand?

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the archetypal pattern.** When a life situation feels larger than itself — when emotions are disproportionate, when you feel "fated," when recurring themes appear in dreams and waking life — ask: what universal pattern am I living? Am I on a hero's journey? Am I being devoured by the Great Mother (unable to leave home, job, or relationship)? Am I inflated by the Puer (refusing adult commitment)?
2. **Honour both poles.** Every archetype has a constructive and destructive face. If you are only experiencing the positive pole, the negative is operating unconsciously. The devoted mother who cannot see her possessiveness. The heroic leader who cannot see his grandiosity. Ask where the shadow of the archetype is falling.
3. **Distinguish identification from relationship.** The danger of archetypal experience is identification — believing you are the archetype rather than a human ego in relationship with it. The man who identifies with the Hero becomes inflated and reckless. The woman who identifies with the Great Mother loses her individual identity. Consciousness means maintaining the ego's perspective while honouring the archetype's energy.
4. **Use myth and story as mirrors.** The myths, fairy tales, films, and stories that grip you are maps of your own archetypal situation. If you are obsessed with a particular story, ask what pattern in your own life it mirrors. The story will reveal dynamics that personal reflection alone cannot reach.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man in his mid-fifties has spent his career as a successful executive but now finds himself drawn to mentoring younger colleagues, volunteering with youth programmes, and thinking about legacy. He dismisses these impulses as "going soft" but cannot shake them.
**Application:** The Senex-to-Wise-Old-Man transition is constellating. In the first half of life, this man was gripped by the Hero archetype — building, achieving, conquering. That archetype has completed its work and is now yielding to the archetype of the Wise Elder, which demands transmission rather than acquisition, mentoring rather than competing, depth rather than breadth. His dismissal of these impulses as "going soft" is the Hero's last stand — the ego's identification with the achieving archetype resisting the natural archetypal transition. The therapeutic direction is to honour the emerging pattern: the impulse to mentor is not weakness but the psyche's demand for the next stage of development. The question is not whether to follow it but how to follow it with full consciousness rather than stumbling into it while the ego clings to an outdated self-image.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Collect archetypes like Pokémon — "I'm working with my Inner Warrior this month."
**Why:** Archetypes are not accessories to be chosen and deployed by the ego. They constellate autonomously in response to life situations. The ego's job is to recognise which archetype is active and to maintain a conscious relationship with it — not to shop for archetypal identities that flatter the self-image.

**Don't:** Use archetypal language to avoid personal responsibility.
**Why:** "The Trickster made me do it" is not Jungian psychology — it is archetypal inflation dressed as self-awareness. Consciousness of an archetypal pattern increases rather than decreases personal responsibility, because you can no longer claim ignorance of the forces operating through you.
