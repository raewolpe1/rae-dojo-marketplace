# Wiredu Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When the practitioner understands conceptual decolonisation in principle but needs a structured method for applying it.
- When designing professional development exercises that build capacity for critical conceptual examination.
- When translating Wiredu's philosophical method into the daily practice of development work.

## Core Concept
Wiredu's method can be structured as a five-step practice for development professionals:

First, concept identification. Identify the key concepts structuring the current programme, policy, or analysis. Democracy, participation, empowerment, accountability, sustainability, resilience, capacity — list them.

Second, genealogical examination. For each concept, trace its origin. Where did this concept come from? What philosophical tradition produced it? What historical conditions shaped it? Most development concepts originate in Western philosophical, economic, or political traditions. Name this.

Third, translation test. Attempt to translate the concept into the community's language. If the concept translates easily, it may be genuinely universal — or the translation may conceal a philosophical difference. If the concept does not translate, the gap is potentially significant.

Fourth, indigenous concept recovery. Ask: does the community's own tradition have a concept for the same domain? What is it? How does it differ from the imported concept? The differences are philosophically significant and practically consequential.

Fifth, redesign. If the indigenous concept differs from the imported one, consider redesigning the programme around the indigenous concept. Not as a cosmetic adjustment but as a genuine reconceptualisation that changes what the programme aims for, measures, and values.

## How to Apply
1. **Conduct a concept audit.** "Before programme design begins, list the key concepts the programme will use. For each, trace its genealogy. If every concept originates in Western traditions, the programme is conceptually colonised."
2. **Run the translation test.** "For each key concept, ask a linguistically competent community member: how would you say this in your language? If the translation requires elaborate paraphrasing, the concept may not exist in the community's framework — which is a philosophical finding, not a linguistic problem."
3. **Convene a concept dialogue.** "Bring together programme designers and community elders or intellectuals for a dialogue about key concepts. Present the programme's concepts; ask the community to present their own concepts for the same domains. The divergences are the material for conceptual decolonisation."
4. **Redesign from indigenous concepts.** "Where the community's concept differs from the programme's, pilot a redesign based on the indigenous concept. Measure different things. Value different outcomes. Pursue different goals. Compare the results."

## Examples
**Situation:** A governance programme is being designed for a district in a West African country. The programme uses standard governance concepts: accountability, transparency, participation, rule of law. The programme designer wants to examine these concepts through Wiredu's lens.
**Application:** "Apply the five steps. Step one, concept identification: accountability, transparency, participation, rule of law. Step two, genealogical examination: all four concepts originate in the Western liberal democratic tradition — specifically the Anglophone governance reform tradition developed by DFID and the World Bank in the 1990s. Step three, translation test: in the local language, 'accountability' requires a compound phrase that means something like 'answering for what one has done to those who gave authority.' 'Transparency' translates as something like 'acting where others can see.' These are not exact equivalents — the local expressions embed different philosophical assumptions (accountability as relational obligation rather than procedural compliance; transparency as communal oversight rather than information publication). Step four, indigenous concept recovery: the community's own governance tradition has concepts for what the programme addresses — the chief's obligation to the stool (accountability), the council's public deliberation (transparency), the community's right to be heard (participation), and the precedents established by ancestors (rule of law as customary jurisprudence). These are not identical to the programme's concepts — they are different philosophical positions. Step five, redesign: consider designing the governance programme around the community's own concepts. 'Accountability' becomes the chief's obligation to the stool — strengthen this mechanism rather than importing a new accountability framework. 'Participation' becomes the community's right to be heard in council — strengthen this tradition rather than creating new participation forums."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the five steps as a checklist.
**Why:** Each step requires genuine philosophical work — not the mechanical ticking of boxes but the careful, dialogical examination of concepts in conversation with the community.

**Don't:** Stop at concept examination.
**Why:** Identifying colonial concepts is necessary but insufficient. The practice must issue in redesign — changing what the programme does, not only what it says.
