# Tradition and Modernity

## When to Use
- When the tradition-modernity binary is structuring a development debate — positioning traditional practices as obstacles to modernisation.
- When advocating for the critical recovery of traditional resources: selecting from tradition what serves contemporary well-being and discarding what does not.
- When modernisation is being presented as culturally neutral when it is actually the adoption of specific Western institutional and cultural forms.

## Core Concept
Wiredu rejects the tradition-modernity opposition that structures much development thinking. This opposition assumes that tradition is backward (static, irrational, obstructive) and modernity is progressive (dynamic, rational, enabling). The development implication is clear: traditional practices must be abandoned and replaced by modern (Western) practices for development to occur.

Wiredu argues that this is both historically wrong and philosophically lazy. It is historically wrong because modernity itself emerged from tradition — European modernity is a critical engagement with European tradition, not its abandonment. It is philosophically lazy because it treats tradition as a monolith (all bad) and modernity as a monolith (all good), when both contain valuable and harmful elements.

The alternative is critical recovery: the philosophical examination of tradition to identify what serves contemporary well-being and what does not. The Akan governance tradition contains consensus democracy — a valuable resource for contemporary political design. It also contains patriarchal exclusions — which must be criticised and reformed. Critical recovery does both: it recovers consensus governance and reforms the patriarchal exclusions. It does not accept tradition wholesale, nor does it reject it wholesale.

For development practice, critical recovery replaces the tradition-modernity binary with a more productive question: what traditional resources are available for addressing contemporary challenges? This question treats tradition as a resource rather than an obstacle — while maintaining the critical stance that prevents uncritical traditionalism.

## How to Apply
1. **Refuse the binary.** "The choice between tradition and modernity is false. Tradition contains resources for modernity; modernity is itself a tradition. The question is not 'tradition or modernity?' but 'what from each tradition serves contemporary well-being?'"
2. **Practise critical recovery.** "Examine the traditional practice: what is its philosophical basis? What human need does it serve? Does it promote well-being? If yes, recover it for contemporary use. If no, critique it and propose alternatives — drawing on both traditional and modern resources."
3. **Question 'modernisation.'** "When modernisation is proposed, ask: modernisation toward what? The adoption of Western institutional forms is not neutral — it is the adoption of one tradition's solutions. Are there indigenous solutions to the same problem?"
4. **Integrate, don't replace.** "The best outcome is often integration: traditional governance structures strengthened by modern accountability mechanisms, traditional health practices complemented by biomedical evidence, traditional education enriched by contemporary knowledge."

## Examples
**Situation:** A public sector reform programme proposes replacing traditional community-level dispute resolution with formal court systems. The programme argues that formal courts are more transparent, accountable, and consistent — and that traditional dispute resolution is "backward."
**Application:** "The programme has adopted the tradition-modernity binary: traditional dispute resolution is backward; formal courts are modern. But examine the traditional system through the lens of critical recovery. The traditional system — typically elder-mediated, consensus-based, restorative rather than punitive — addresses several genuine needs: it is locally accessible (the formal court may be distant), it is culturally legitimate (the community trusts it), it is restorative (it aims to repair relationships rather than to punish), and it is responsive to social context (the mediator knows the parties and their history). The formal court system, by contrast, may be geographically distant, procedurally alien, punitive rather than restorative, and socially decontextualised. The programme should practise critical recovery. What does the traditional system do well? Local accessibility, cultural legitimacy, restorative justice, social context. What does it do badly? It may discriminate against women, it may be captured by powerful elders, it may lack transparency. The modern system addresses some of these weaknesses but creates new ones. The best outcome is integration: the traditional system's strengths (accessibility, legitimacy, restoration) with the modern system's strengths (transparency, non-discrimination, accountability). This is not a compromise — it is critical recovery in action."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Romanticise tradition uncritically.
**Why:** Tradition contains oppressive elements — patriarchy, exclusion, arbitrary authority — that must be identified and reformed. Critical recovery is critical: it examines tradition rather than celebrating it.

**Don't:** Present modernisation as culturally neutral.
**Why:** "Modernisation" usually means the adoption of Western institutional and cultural forms. This is not a neutral process — it is the substitution of one tradition's solutions for another's. Both traditions should be examined, and the best elements of both should be recovered.
