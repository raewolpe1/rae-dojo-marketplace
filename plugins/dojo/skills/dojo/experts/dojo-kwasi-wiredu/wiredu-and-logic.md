# Wiredu and Logic

## When to Use
- When claims about "different logics" or "different ways of knowing" arise and need to be examined philosophically.
- When arguing for the universality of basic logical principles while acknowledging that their expression and application vary across cultures and languages.
- When the relationship between language, thought, and reasoning needs to be clarified.

## Core Concept
Wiredu holds that the basic principles of logic — non-contradiction, modus ponens, the distinction between valid and invalid inference — are universal. All human communities reason, and all human reasoning must conform to basic logical constraints to be reasoning at all. The claim that "Africans have a different logic" is both empirically false (Africans reason as rigorously as anyone) and patronising (it implies that African thought is pre-logical).

However, Wiredu also recognises that the expression and application of logic vary across languages and cultures. The Akan language structures certain logical relationships differently from English — and this difference reveals not different logics but different ways of expressing the same logical relationships, with different philosophical implications. For example, the Akan expression that translates roughly as "I know" embeds a stronger evidential commitment than the English "I know" — it implies direct personal evidence in a way that the English does not.

For development practice, the logical universalism matters: when a programme's theory of change is logically incoherent, it is incoherent in any language. The cultural expression matters: when the programme asks a community to reason through a decision, it must do so in the community's own logical vocabulary, attending to the specific ways the community's language structures inference, evidence, and justification.

## How to Apply
1. **Maintain logical universalism.** "Basic logical principles apply everywhere. A programme design that is logically incoherent — that assumes A causes B without evidence, or that contradicts itself — is incoherent regardless of cultural context."
2. **Attend to logical expression.** "How does this community express logical relationships? How does its language structure evidence, inference, and justification? The logic is the same; the expression may differ."
3. **Resist the 'different logics' claim.** "When someone claims that the community 'thinks differently,' ask: differently how? If the claim is about different reasoning processes, examine it carefully — it may be patronising. If the claim is about different values or priorities, it may be legitimate but is not about logic."
4. **Use logical analysis across cultures.** "Logical analysis — identifying assumptions, tracing implications, testing consistency — is a universal tool. Apply it to development theories, programme designs, and policy arguments regardless of cultural context."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme evaluation is critiqued by a community leader who argues that the evaluation's logical framework does not capture the community's understanding of causation. The evaluator dismisses this as "cultural difference in reasoning."
**Application:** "The evaluator is wrong to dismiss the critique as 'different reasoning.' But the community leader's objection may be about something other than logic. Examine the critique carefully. If the community understands causation as multi-causal, non-linear, and embedded in social relationships — while the evaluation's logframe assumes single-cause, linear, measurable relationships — the disagreement is not about logic. It is about the model of causation. The community's model may be more sophisticated than the logframe's — recognising feedback loops, social dynamics, and contextual factors that the logframe's linear model cannot capture. This is not 'different logic.' It is a different (and possibly better) model of how change happens. The logical principles are the same: if A contributes to B under conditions C, and conditions C are present, then A should contribute to B. The disagreement is about what A, B, and C are — which is an empirical and conceptual question, not a logical one."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Deny cultural variation in reasoning styles.
**Why:** While basic logical principles are universal, reasoning styles, evidential standards, and argumentative conventions vary across cultures. These variations are not "different logics" but different applications of the same logic.

**Don't:** Accept "different logics" uncritically.
**Why:** The claim that a community has "a different logic" can be patronising — it implies that the community's reasoning does not conform to basic logical standards. Usually what is actually different is the model, the values, or the priorities — not the logic itself.
