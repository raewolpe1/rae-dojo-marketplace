# Wiredu and Development

## When to Use
- When the conceptual foundations of development practice need to be examined for colonial origins and indigenous alternatives.
- When designing programmes that use genuinely indigenous concepts rather than translations of Western ones.
- When the very concept of "development" needs to be philosophically interrogated.

## Core Concept
Wiredu's conceptual decolonisation applies directly to the concept of "development" itself. "Development" — as used in the international development industry — is a Western concept with specific philosophical assumptions: that societies progress linearly from traditional to modern, that this progress is measurable through economic indicators, that the Western institutional model represents the destination, and that external intervention can accelerate the journey.

Conceptual decolonisation asks: does the indigenous tradition have its own concept for what "development" claims to address? The Akan concept of nkɔsoɔ (progress, advancement) addresses the same domain but with different philosophical assumptions. Nkɔsoɔ is communal (the community advances together, not individuals in isolation), it is moral (advancement includes moral improvement, not just material gain), and it is embedded in the community's own understanding of its trajectory (where it has been and where it wants to go, rather than a linear path toward a Western model).

Designing development around nkɔsoɔ rather than around "development" would produce a different programme: one that measures communal advancement rather than individual outcomes, that includes moral and relational dimensions alongside economic ones, and that is directed by the community's own assessment of its trajectory rather than by external indicators of progress toward a predetermined model.

## How to Apply
1. **Interrogate 'development.'** "What philosophical assumptions are embedded in the concept of 'development' as used in this programme? Linear progress? Economic primacy? Western institutional models as destination? Name them."
2. **Seek the indigenous concept.** "What concept does the community use for what the programme calls 'development'? What assumptions does the indigenous concept carry? How does it differ from the programme's concept?"
3. **Design from the indigenous concept.** "If the community's own concept of progress, advancement, or flourishing differs from the programme's concept of 'development,' which should guide programme design? The cosmopolitan answer: begin with the indigenous concept and supplement with useful elements of the international concept."
4. **Decolonise the indicators.** "If the programme measures 'development' using indicators derived from Western concepts (GDP growth, institutional capacity, governance indices), ask: what would the indigenous concept measure instead? Design indicators that capture what the community's own concept of progress values."

## Examples
**Situation:** A rural development programme in Ghana measures success through standard indicators: agricultural productivity, household income, children's school enrolment, and access to health services. The community, when asked, describes its own understanding of progress differently: the quality of communal relationships, the capacity for collective action, the moral character of the youth, and the maintenance of cultural institutions.
**Application:** "The programme measures development. The community measures nkɔsoɔ. These are not the same thing. The programme's indicators capture the economic and service-delivery dimensions — which are important — but miss the communal, moral, and cultural dimensions that the community values most. The community does not reject the programme's goals (agricultural productivity and children's education are valued). But the community embeds these goals within a broader concept of progress that includes dimensions the programme's indicators cannot see. Conceptual decolonisation here means: redesign the results framework to capture both. Add indicators for communal well-being (the quality and density of mutual aid networks), collective capacity (the community's ability to act together on shared goals), moral development (the community's assessment of its youth's character), and cultural vitality (the maintenance and adaptation of cultural institutions). These are not 'soft' supplementary indicators — they are the primary indicators of nkɔsoɔ as the community defines it. The programme's economic indicators become important but partial — one dimension of a richer concept of progress."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reject all development concepts.
**Why:** Some development concepts — evidence-based decision-making, systematic planning, accountability for results — are genuinely useful regardless of their Western origin. Conceptual decolonisation examines concepts; it does not reject them categorically.

**Don't:** Treat decolonisation as relabelling.
**Why:** Translating "empowerment" into the local language does not decolonise the concept. Decolonisation means examining the philosophical content — and potentially replacing the concept with a genuinely indigenous one that addresses the same domain differently.
