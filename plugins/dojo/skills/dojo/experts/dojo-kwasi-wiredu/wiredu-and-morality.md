# Wiredu and Morality

## When to Use
- When the moral basis of development practice needs to be articulated — what makes an intervention right or wrong, good or bad.
- When grounding moral claims in something more substantive than institutional policy or individual preference.
- When the Akan moral tradition provides resources for a cross-cultural ethics grounded in human well-being.

## Core Concept
Wiredu grounds Akan morality in human well-being rather than in divine command, abstract principle, or cultural convention. What makes an action morally good is that it promotes human flourishing — the well-being of persons in community. What makes it morally bad is that it diminishes well-being. This is a form of moral naturalism: morality is grounded in observable facts about what promotes or diminishes human welfare.

The Akan moral tradition is communitarian: the individual's well-being is inseparable from the community's well-being. The good person (onipa pa) is the person who contributes to communal welfare — who fulfils kinship obligations, participates in communal life, and acts in ways that strengthen rather than weaken social bonds. Morality is not primarily about individual virtue or individual rights but about the quality of social relationships.

This does not make morality relative. Wiredu argues that human well-being has a universal core — all human beings need food, shelter, health, social connection, respect, and the capacity to participate in communal life. These needs are expressed through culturally specific thick practices, but the needs themselves are universal. A moral claim grounded in well-being is cross-culturally valid because human well-being is cross-culturally real.

## How to Apply
1. **Ground moral claims in well-being.** "Does this programme promote human well-being? Not as defined by the donor or the government but as experienced by the people the programme serves. Well-being is the moral standard."
2. **Include the communal dimension.** "Well-being is not only individual. It includes the quality of social relationships, the strength of communal bonds, and the capacity for mutual support. A programme that improves individual outcomes while weakening community is not promoting well-being."
3. **Test against universal needs.** "Do the programme's outcomes address genuine human needs — food, health, shelter, social connection, respect, participation? These needs are the universal core of well-being, even if their expression varies."
4. **Use well-being as a conversation anchor.** "When values clash across cultures, well-being provides a conversation anchor. Both parties can agree that human well-being matters — even if they disagree about its specific expression."

## Examples
**Situation:** A debate emerges within a programme team about whether to prioritise economic outcomes (income, employment) or social outcomes (community cohesion, mutual support networks). The team is divided along disciplinary lines: economists favour economic outcomes; sociologists favour social outcomes.
**Application:** "The debate is structured by a false dichotomy. In the Akan moral framework, well-being is both economic and social — it encompasses material sufficiency (the person has enough to live) and social integration (the person is embedded in relationships of mutual support). A person with income but no communal relationships is not flourishing. A person with strong social bonds but no means of subsistence is not flourishing. Well-being is the integration of both. The programme should measure both dimensions and recognise that the relationship between them is synergistic: economic improvement strengthens social bonds (people have more to share) and social bonds support economic activity (mutual aid, risk-sharing, collective action). The disciplinary division — economics versus sociology — reflects a Western academic partition that the Akan tradition does not recognise. Well-being is one thing, not two."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat well-being as entirely subjective.
**Why:** Wiredu argues that well-being has a universal core — genuine human needs that apply across cultures. While the expression of well-being varies, the needs themselves are objective.

**Don't:** Ignore the communal dimension.
**Why:** The Akan moral tradition insists that individual well-being is inseparable from communal well-being. A moral analysis that considers only individual outcomes has adopted a Western individualist framework without examining it.
