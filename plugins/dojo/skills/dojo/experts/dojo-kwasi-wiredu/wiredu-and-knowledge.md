# Wiredu and Knowledge

## When to Use
- When the concept of knowledge itself — what counts as knowing, what counts as evidence, what justifies belief — needs to be examined across traditions.
- When development's evidence hierarchy (RCTs > quasi-experiments > qualitative > local knowledge) reflects epistemological assumptions that need to be surfaced.
- When the community's own knowledge system operates with different assumptions about what constitutes valid knowledge.

## Core Concept
Wiredu's epistemological contribution lies in his analysis of how different traditions conceptualise knowledge itself. In Western epistemology, knowledge is typically defined as justified true belief — a proposition that is believed, that is true, and for which the believer has adequate justification. This definition has been productive but is not the only possible conceptualisation.

The Akan concept of knowledge (nimdeɛ) embeds different assumptions. Knowing in Akan involves direct personal engagement — it is closer to acquaintance than to propositional belief. The Akan expression translated as "I know" carries a stronger evidential commitment than the English "I know" — it implies personal experience and direct encounter rather than mere propositional assent. This is not a weakness but a philosophical position: knowledge requires engagement, not merely information.

For development practice, the epistemological differences matter because they shape what counts as valid evidence. The development evidence hierarchy — which places randomised controlled trials at the top and local knowledge at the bottom — reflects a specific epistemological tradition: empiricist, propositional, method-centred. Other epistemological traditions — experiential, relational, practice-centred — may value different forms of evidence. The farmer who "knows" her soil because she has worked it for decades possesses knowledge that no RCT can produce and that the evidence hierarchy does not recognise.

## How to Apply
1. **Examine the evidence hierarchy.** "What does this programme count as valid knowledge? RCTs? Systematic reviews? Expert opinion? Local knowledge? The hierarchy reveals epistemological assumptions that should be surfaced and examined."
2. **Recognise experiential knowledge.** "The community's knowledge — acquired through direct engagement, tested through practice, validated through collective experience — is a form of knowledge, not merely a form of belief. The evidence hierarchy should accommodate it."
3. **Apply conceptual decolonisation to epistemology.** "The concept of 'evidence-based practice' embeds a specific epistemology: knowledge is produced through controlled investigation and applied to practice through systematic review. The community may operate with a different epistemology: knowledge is produced through direct engagement and applied through practice wisdom."
4. **Design for multiple epistemologies.** "A programme that takes epistemological diversity seriously would use multiple forms of evidence: statistical evidence from studies, experiential evidence from practitioners, communal evidence from communities, and practical evidence from implementation."

## Examples
**Situation:** A climate adaptation programme commissions a scientific study of changing rainfall patterns. The study produces valuable data. Meanwhile, local farmers report that their own observations of rainfall patterns — based on decades of direct experience, correlated with ecological indicators the study did not measure — tell a different story.
**Application:** "Two epistemologies are producing different knowledge. The scientific study produces propositional knowledge: statistically analysed data on rainfall trends measured by meteorological instruments. The farmers produce experiential knowledge: observed patterns based on decades of direct engagement with the environment, interpreted through ecological indicators (flowering times, insect behaviour, soil moisture) that the instruments did not measure. Neither epistemology is simply 'right.' The scientific study captures what its instruments can measure; the farmers capture what their experience has taught them to observe. The programme should integrate both: use the scientific data for broad trend analysis and the farmers' experiential knowledge for local, contextualised understanding. The farmers' knowledge is not 'anecdotal evidence' to be placed below the scientific study in the evidence hierarchy. It is a different form of knowledge — experiential, relational, ecologically embedded — that captures dimensions of reality the scientific study cannot see. Wiredu's epistemological analysis suggests: examine the concept of 'evidence' itself. If evidence means only what controlled investigation produces, local knowledge is excluded by definition. If evidence means what provides justified confidence in a belief, local knowledge qualifies — and may be more reliable in specific contexts than the scientific study."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat all epistemologies as equally valid for all purposes.
**Why:** Different epistemologies are suited to different questions. Scientific epistemology is suited to questions about general trends; experiential epistemology is suited to questions about specific local conditions. The claim is not that all are equal but that all are genuine.

**Don't:** Reduce epistemological questions to political questions.
**Why:** The colonial structure of the evidence hierarchy is real and must be named. But the epistemological questions — what constitutes knowledge, what justifies belief, what counts as evidence — are genuinely philosophical, not merely political.
