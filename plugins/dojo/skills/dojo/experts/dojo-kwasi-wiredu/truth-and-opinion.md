# Truth and Opinion

## When to Use
- When a dispute appears intractable and may be so because the parties are treating an opinion dispute as a truth dispute, or vice versa.
- When multi-stakeholder processes need a framework for productive disagreement.
- When the distinction between what can be established empirically and what requires negotiation and accommodation is unclear.

## Core Concept
Wiredu draws a fundamental distinction between truth and opinion that has both logical and practical implications. Truth concerns matters that are in principle decidable by investigation — they have determinate answers that can be discovered. Opinion concerns matters where legitimate disagreement persists even after all the facts are known — matters of taste, value, priority, and interpretation.

Many disputes that appear to be about truth are actually about opinion — and recognising this dissolves the conflict. Two parties who disagree about the best method for community engagement are not disagreeing about truth (which method produces the best outcome is an empirical question) but about opinion (what counts as "best" depends on values and priorities). If both parties recognise that the dispute is about opinion rather than truth, they can negotiate and accommodate rather than insisting that one party must be wrong.

Conversely, disputes that are treated as matters of opinion may actually be about truth. Whether a programme reaches its intended beneficiaries is a truth question — it can be investigated and answered. Treating it as a matter of opinion ("well, it depends on your perspective") evades accountability.

The practical value of the distinction is enormous. Multi-stakeholder processes routinely founder because all disagreements are treated identically — as disputes to be resolved by persuasion, negotiation, or voting. But truth disputes require investigation (what actually happened?), while opinion disputes require accommodation (how do we live with our different values?). Using the wrong process for the wrong type of dispute produces frustration and failure.

## How to Apply
1. **Classify the dispute.** "Is this a dispute about truth (what actually happened, what the evidence shows, what the numbers reveal) or about opinion (what matters most, what we should prioritise, what counts as success)? The classification determines the process."
2. **Apply the right process.** "Truth disputes require investigation — data collection, analysis, evidence review. Opinion disputes require negotiation — deliberation, compromise, mutual accommodation. Do not investigate opinions or negotiate truths."
3. **Dissolve false disputes.** "Some disputes that appear intractable are actually misclassified. If both parties believe they are arguing about truth when they are actually arguing about opinion, reclassifying the dispute may dissolve the deadlock."
4. **Acknowledge the grey zone.** "Some disputes involve both truth and opinion elements. Whether child labour reduces educational outcomes is a truth question. Whether reducing child labour is worth the short-term economic cost to families is an opinion question. Separating the elements clarifies the discussion."

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluation steering committee is deadlocked over the evaluation's findings. The programme team argues that the programme succeeded because it achieved its output targets. The donor argues that the programme failed because impact indicators did not improve. Both sides claim the evidence supports their position.
**Application:** "The deadlock is produced by a misclassified dispute. The parties are presenting an opinion dispute as a truth dispute. The truth questions are empirical: did the programme achieve its output targets? (Probably yes — this can be verified.) Did impact indicators improve? (Probably not — this can also be verified.) Both parties can agree on the empirical facts. The dispute is actually about opinion: what counts as 'success'? The programme team defines success as achieving outputs (which is within their control). The donor defines success as achieving impact (which depends on many factors beyond the programme's control). This is a legitimate disagreement about the appropriate standard of evaluation — a disagreement about values and priorities, not about facts. Recognising it as an opinion dispute transforms the discussion: instead of arguing about what the evidence 'really' shows (a false truth dispute), the parties can negotiate about the standard of evaluation (a genuine opinion dispute). The evidence does not resolve this dispute — it informs a conversation that must be resolved through deliberation about what standard of success is appropriate for this type of programme."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat all moral claims as mere opinion.
**Why:** Some moral claims are closer to truth than to opinion — the claim that torture is wrong, for example, is not merely an expression of preference. The truth-opinion distinction is a tool for analysis, not a mechanism for dissolving all moral claims into relativism.

**Don't:** Use the distinction to avoid engagement.
**Why:** Classifying a dispute as "opinion" does not mean it is unimportant or that engagement is unnecessary. Opinion disputes require genuine deliberation and accommodation — not dismissal.
