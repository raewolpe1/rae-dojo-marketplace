# Conceptual Decolonisation

## When to Use
- When the concepts through which a programme, policy, or analysis is structured need to be examined for colonial origins.
- When Western concepts — democracy, development, governance, rights, capacity — are being used as universal standards without examining whether the indigenous tradition has its own concepts for the same domains.
- When genuine decolonisation requires going beyond political and institutional reform to the reform of thought itself.

## Core Concept
Conceptual decolonisation is Wiredu's most distinctive contribution. It names the most fundamental form of decolonisation: the examination of the concepts through which we think. Political decolonisation transfers power. Institutional decolonisation reforms structures. Conceptual decolonisation examines the categories of thought themselves — the concepts of democracy, personhood, justice, truth, knowledge, development — to determine which are genuinely indigenous and which are colonial imports that have been adopted without examination.

The method is philosophical, not political. Wiredu does not argue that Western concepts are necessarily wrong or that indigenous concepts are necessarily right. He argues that the concepts must be examined — that the uncritical adoption of Western concepts in African contexts represents a failure of philosophical rigour, not a deficiency of the indigenous tradition.

The procedure: take a concept that is being used in the African context — say, "democracy." Ask: what is the indigenous concept that addresses the same domain? In the Akan tradition, governance is deliberative: decisions are made through council deliberation aimed at consensus, not through majoritarian voting. The indigenous concept is not equivalent to the Western concept — it is a different philosophical position with different implications. The uncritical adoption of "democracy" as equivalent to "multi-party elections with majority rule" has imported a specific Western institutional form while suppressing the indigenous alternative. Conceptual decolonisation makes this substitution visible and recovers the indigenous concept as a genuine philosophical alternative.

For development practice, conceptual decolonisation is transformative. Every programme concept — participation, empowerment, accountability, sustainability, resilience — has a history. That history is usually Western. The question is: does the indigenous tradition have its own concept for the same domain? If so, what does the indigenous concept reveal that the Western concept conceals? And what would the programme look like if it were designed around the indigenous concept rather than the Western one?

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the concept.** "Which concepts are structuring this programme? Democracy? Participation? Empowerment? Accountability? List them."
2. **Trace the genealogy.** "Where did this concept originate? What philosophical tradition produced it? What historical conditions shaped it? The genealogy reveals the concept's cultural specificity."
3. **Seek the indigenous equivalent.** "Does the community's own tradition have a concept for the same domain? What is the Akan, Zulu, Igbo, or Swahili concept that addresses the same questions? If the indigenous concept differs from the Western one, the difference is philosophically significant."
4. **Design from the indigenous concept.** "What would the programme look like if designed around the indigenous concept rather than the Western one? This is not merely renaming — it is redesigning from different philosophical foundations."

## Examples
**Situation:** A community-driven development programme uses the concept of "empowerment" — defined as increasing individuals' control over decisions that affect their lives. The programme measures empowerment through individual decision-making indicators: women making household financial decisions independently, community members participating in formal governance structures, individuals accessing services autonomously.
**Application:** "The concept of 'empowerment' as individual control over decisions is a Western liberal concept — rooted in the philosophical tradition of individual autonomy. The Akan concept that addresses the same domain is different. In Akan thought, a person is not fully a person in isolation — personhood is achieved through social relationships, communal contribution, and the fulfilment of obligations to others. The Akan concept of well-being is not individual control but harmonious social integration — being embedded in a web of relationships in which one both gives and receives. An Akan 'empowerment' programme would not measure individual decision-making in isolation. It would measure the quality of social relationships — the extent to which persons are integrated into supportive, reciprocal networks that enable them to fulfil their communal obligations and receive communal support. The Western programme measures whether the woman makes household financial decisions independently. The Akan programme would measure whether the woman is embedded in relationships that support her well-being and enable her contribution to communal life. These are different concepts of flourishing — and the difference is philosophically significant. The programme should at least examine the indigenous concept before defaulting to the Western one. It may find that the indigenous concept captures dimensions of well-being that the Western concept misses."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reject all Western concepts as colonial.
**Why:** Some Western concepts may be genuinely useful — analytical rigour, systematic methodology, institutional accountability. Conceptual decolonisation examines concepts; it does not reject them categorically.

**Don't:** Treat conceptual decolonisation as merely renaming.
**Why:** Translating a Western concept into an indigenous language and calling it "decolonised" changes nothing. Conceptual decolonisation means examining the philosophical content of the concept — and potentially replacing it with a genuinely different indigenous concept.
