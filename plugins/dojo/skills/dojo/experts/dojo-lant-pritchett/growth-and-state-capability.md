# Growth and State Capability

## When to Use
- When debating whether growth or state capability comes first.
- When designing reform sequences across economic and governance areas.
- When responding to institutional-first or growth-first framings.

## Fails When
- **The country is in immediate crisis** that requires emergency rather than developmental framing.

## Core Concept

Growth and state capability co-evolve. Neither is sufficient alone; neither can be sequenced as a complete precondition for the other. The orthodox "good governance first, then growth will follow" sequence inverts the actual historical pattern. The "growth first, governance will follow" sequence is incomplete in the opposite direction.

The empirical record. Today's developed countries built state capability and economic growth together, not in sequence. Britain's industrial revolution proceeded with substantial state weakness, corruption, and incomplete institutions; capability was built alongside the growth. East Asian developmental states built capability and growth in parallel — the state institutions were built up over decades while growth was occurring. China has built substantial state capability over four decades alongside its growth surge. Korea, Taiwan, and Singapore similarly.

The implications:

**Don't sequence development as governance-then-growth.** This sequence has been recommended for decades and has produced minimal development in countries that have followed it.

**Don't treat growth alone as sufficient.** Growth without capability building produces commodity booms, resource-curse dynamics, and stagnation when the favourable conditions reverse.

**The capability building should be in areas the growth strategy demands.** State capability for industrial policy delivery, for energy infrastructure, for skill development, for trade negotiation, for SOE governance — these are growth-supporting capabilities that should be built as the growth strategy demands them.

**Capability building requires growth to fund it.** Sustained capability building requires the fiscal resources that growth provides. Pure austerity programmes do not build capability.

The implication for development advice: the question is not which to do first but how to design programmes that build capability alongside growth, in mutually reinforcing ways. Industrial policy delivery builds capability while supporting growth. Education quality reform builds capability while supporting future productivity. State investment in infrastructure builds capability while supporting growth.

For South Africa: the question of "structural reform" is often framed as governance-first. The historical record suggests this framing is wrong. The reform programme should integrate growth-supporting and capability-building work in parallel, with each supporting the other.

## How to Apply

1. **Reject the sequencing framings** in both directions.
2. **Design programmes that build capability alongside growth.**
3. **Direct capability building** to the functions the growth strategy demands.
4. **Use growth fiscal resources** to fund capability building.

## Examples

**Situation:** Donor advice to South Africa: "complete the structural reforms before expecting growth recovery."

**Application:** The framing is historically wrong. The countries that recovered growth after stagnation typically did so alongside, not after, capability rebuilding. The reform programme should integrate the work: industrial strategy delivery + state capability rebuilding + infrastructure investment + targeted regulatory reform, all in parallel and mutually reinforcing.

**Result:** A reform programme that is realistic about how growth and capability actually interact.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use the co-evolution framing as an excuse for non-reform.
**Why:** Capability building requires deliberate work; co-evolution does not happen automatically.

**Don't:** Imagine the sequence is irrelevant.
**Why:** Some sequencing choices matter (e.g., fiscal stability typically supports rather than impedes capability building). The framework is about avoiding sequence-everything-on-governance, not about ignoring sequence entirely.
