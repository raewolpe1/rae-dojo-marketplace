# Schooling Ain't Learning

## When to Use
- When education policy is being debated in terms of enrolment, completion, or schooling years.
- When the orthodox response to education problems is "more schooling."
- When evaluating education system performance.

## Fails When
- **The country genuinely has both schooling and learning problems** at the access level. The framework applies most sharply where schooling has expanded.

## Core Concept

Schooling and learning are different things. The book *The Rebirth of Education: Schooling Ain't Learning* (2013) makes the case empirically and policy-relevantly.

**Schooling** is measurable inputs: time spent in school, enrolment rates, completion rates, school facilities. It is what national accounts of education track. It is what conventional education indicators measure.

**Learning** is the actual acquisition of knowledge, skills, and competencies. It is what schooling is supposed to produce. It is what we should care about if we care about education's developmental contribution.

The empirical record across the developing world over the past three decades:

**Schooling has expanded dramatically.** Primary completion rates have risen substantially across most developing countries. Secondary enrolment has expanded. Tertiary access has grown. The schooling expansion is one of the largest social changes of the period.

**Learning has not kept pace.** Across many countries, children complete primary schooling without basic literacy or numeracy. They complete secondary schooling without competencies that secondary education is supposed to produce. The learning outcomes have been stagnant or have declined while schooling expanded.

The combination produces a population that is schooled-but-not-learning at a vast scale. The "more education" approach has produced more credentials without more capability.

The orthodox response — more schooling, more spending, more access — does not address the binding constraint, which is what happens in the classroom. The binding constraints on learning include: teacher quality and time-on-task; pedagogical approach (teaching at the right level); accountability for learning rather than for schooling; classroom-level practice; school management; parental engagement with learning rather than with enrolment.

For South Africa: classic case of schooling-without-learning at scale. Primary completion is high; international assessments (TIMSS, PIRLS, SACMEQ) show severe learning deficits. The corrective requires shifting the system's focus from schooling provision to learning production — which requires substantial implementation work at classroom level.

## How to Apply

1. **Measure learning**, not just schooling.
2. **Identify the binding constraint on learning** (usually teacher quality, classroom practice, or accountability for learning).
3. **Design interventions on the binding constraint** rather than on expanding access.
4. **Resist "more schooling" responses** when the actual problem is learning quality.

## Examples

**Situation:** South African education debates focused on free higher education, expanded access, and school building programmes.

**Application:** These address schooling access at the margins; they do not address the binding learning constraint at primary and secondary levels. Reframing: the binding constraint is what happens in classrooms during the years children are already in school. Interventions on teacher quality, classroom practice, and learning accountability would produce more learning per rand than additional schooling access.

**Result:** Policy attention reoriented from schooling expansion to learning improvement.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use the framework to oppose all schooling expansion.
**Why:** Access expansion remains valuable where access genuinely binds.

**Don't:** Treat learning as solvable by curriculum reform alone.
**Why:** Classroom-level implementation is the binding constraint, not curriculum design.
