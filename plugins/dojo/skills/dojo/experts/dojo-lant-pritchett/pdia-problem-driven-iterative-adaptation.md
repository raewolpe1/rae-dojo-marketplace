# PDIA — Problem-Driven Iterative Adaptation

## When to Use
- When designing capability-building interventions.
- When traditional capacity-building approaches (workshops, manuals, technical assistance) have not produced functional improvement.
- When tackling complex implementation challenges that require local adaptation.

## Fails When
- **The authorising environment will not sustain iteration.** PDIA requires political and donor commitment to learning over the multi-year horizon.
- **The problem is trivial** — straightforward technical problems may not need PDIA.

## Core Concept

PDIA (Andrews, Pritchett, Woolcock, *Building State Capability*, 2017) is a methodology for building state capability through iterative problem-solving. Each word in the name matters.

**Problem-Driven.** Start with a specific local problem that authorisers care about solving, not with a best-practice template imported from elsewhere. The local problem provides the motivation for learning; the imported template provides only the form.

**Iterative.** Solutions are not designed in advance and rolled out. They are developed through small experiments — try something, observe what happens, modify, try again. The iterations produce both the solution and the capability to develop solutions.

**Adaptive.** The organisation learns from each iteration. The next iteration uses what was learned. Over time, the organisation builds the capability to do this work, not just to execute imported templates.

The methodology contrasts with orthodox capability-building approaches:

**Workshops, training, manuals.** Produce trained individuals; do not build organisational capability.

**Best-practice transfers.** Produce isomorphic mimicry; the form arrives but the function does not.

**Big-bang reforms.** Produce reform fatigue; the demanded changes exceed implementation capability.

**Technical assistance.** Produces dependency; the external expertise replaces the internal capability rather than building it.

PDIA design elements:

**Authorising environment.** Build political and donor support for the iterative work. The authorisation must tolerate small failures, learning over delivery in early phases, and a multi-year horizon.

**Action research teams.** Cross-functional teams of people who actually do the work, focused on solving specific problems.

**Rapid iteration cycles.** Short cycles (weeks, not years) that produce learning quickly.

**Reflection and adaptation.** Structured reflection on what worked, what didn't, and why.

**Capability emerging through the work.** As problems get solved iteratively, the routines and skills for solving them become embedded in the organisation.

For South Africa: PDIA approaches have been applied in specific contexts (some municipalities, some education interventions). Broader uptake faces challenges from donor and political environments that demand pre-designed outcomes and dispersed accountability.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the specific local problem** with authoriser support.
2. **Form a cross-functional team** focused on the problem.
3. **Iterate rapidly** — try, observe, modify, try again.
4. **Reflect structurally** on what is being learned.
5. **Build the capability** through the work over time.

## Examples

**Situation:** A municipality is struggling with refuse collection delivery.

**Application:** PDIA approach: convene a cross-functional team (operations, finance, councillor liaison, community engagement); identify the specific binding constraint (e.g. vehicle availability, route design, household payment, contractor management); try a small modification; observe; modify; iterate. Over 6–12 months, the team builds the operational capability and produces a better-functioning system.

**Result:** Capability built through the work, not through training.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Convert PDIA into another best-practice template to import.
**Why:** The point is local problem-driven iteration, not imported methodology.

**Don't:** Skip the authorising environment work.
**Why:** Without political and donor support, iteration becomes labelled as failure when small experiments don't immediately succeed.
