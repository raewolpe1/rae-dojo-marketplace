# Case for Experiments and Iteration

## When to Use
- When designing a reform programme that should learn rather than just be deployed.
- When responding to "we already know what works" complacency.
- When evaluating whether to pilot or roll out.

## Fails When
- **Speed is essential** and iteration would delay catastrophically.
- **The intervention is well-validated** in the specific context.

## Core Concept

Reform should be structured for learning, not for one-shot deployment. Most reforms have substantial uncertainty about which design will work in the specific context; iterative experimentation produces information that pre-design cannot.

The elements:

**Small-scale experiments.** Try the reform in selected contexts first, with structured comparison to non-treated contexts. The pilot generates information.

**Multiple variations.** Where the design has multiple plausible versions, test variations in parallel rather than picking one upfront. The information from variation is itself valuable.

**Rapid cycles.** Pilots that take 5 years generate information too slowly to inform reform. Faster cycles (months, not years) produce learning that can inform subsequent iterations.

**Honest evaluation.** Pilots that are designed to confirm rather than to test produce no learning. Independent, methodologically credible evaluation is essential.

**Willingness to modify or stop.** Some pilots will reveal that the reform doesn't work or doesn't work as designed. Be willing to modify substantially or stop, rather than defending the original design.

**Scale based on findings, not on political timeline.** Some pilots will produce strong evidence supporting scale-up; others will reveal that scale-up is not justified. Distinguish.

The contrast is with the big-bang or design-and-rollout model. The latter assumes that the right design can be identified through pre-design analysis and rolled out at scale. The track record of this approach across development reforms is poor. Sophisticated reforms designed in advance and rolled out at national scale have repeatedly failed in implementation. The iterative approach produces better outcomes by learning as it goes.

The connection to PDIA is direct. PDIA is the application of experimental iteration to state-capability building. Other applications exist in education reform, health system reform, public-sector reform, and beyond.

## How to Apply

1. **Structure reform with iteration built in.**
2. **Pilot with multiple variations** where design uncertainty is high.
3. **Use rapid cycles** for learning.
4. **Evaluate honestly** and be willing to modify substantially.
5. **Scale based on evidence**, not political timeline.

## Examples

**Situation:** A proposed national rollout of a new education curriculum.

**Application:** Pilot in selected districts with multiple curriculum variations, structured implementation differences, and honest evaluation. After 18 months, evidence on which version works best, what implementation conditions enable it, what modifications matter. Scale based on this evidence rather than on the original design.

**Result:** A reform programme designed for learning.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Confuse iteration with indecision.
**Why:** Iteration is structured learning; indecision is the absence of action.

**Don't:** Use experimentation to delay reform indefinitely.
**Why:** Pilots that never scale are theatre. Design scaling triggers and apply them.
