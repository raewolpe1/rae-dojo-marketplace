# Interventions with the Strongest Aging Evidence

## When to Use
- When someone asks what actually works, cutting through the noise.
- When you need an honest, evidence-graded tiering rather than a hype list.
- When a person is deciding where to put attention and money.
- When contrasting the well-evidenced basics with the marketed molecules.

## Fails When
- **Unproven molecules get presented as proven.** The tiering must be honest about what's hypothesis vs result.
- **The boring basics get crowded out.** The largest achievable benefit is unglamorous; burying it under exciting compounds is the field's recurring mistake.
- **A ranked list is given without grading.** A list without evidence tiers is just another set of claims.

## Core Concept
Asked "what actually works for aging," the honest answer is a tiered map, not a ranked list of molecules — because the things with the best evidence are mostly not the things that get marketed. Kaeberlein's tiering sorts every candidate into proven (strong human evidence for healthspan), strongest preclinical bet (best animal evidence, unproven in humans), plausible-but-unproven (real mechanism, no human outcome), and sold (marketed far ahead of evidence). The single most important fact is that the top tier is dominated by unglamorous lifestyle interventions, and the exciting molecules sit lower.

**Tier 1 — proven, strong human evidence (and unsellable).** Regular physical activity, especially maintaining cardiorespiratory fitness and muscular strength, has the best and broadest evidence of anything for extending healthy, functional years and lowering all-cause mortality. Not smoking. Adequate, regular sleep. Maintaining metabolic health (avoiding/ managing obesity, insulin resistance, hypertension, dyslipidemia). Maintaining muscle mass and avoiding frailty as you age. Social and cognitive engagement. These are squarely healthspan interventions, they're supported by large human evidence, and for a healthy middle-aged person they represent the overwhelming majority of the achievable benefit. They're "boring" precisely because they're well established — and they can't be patented or sold in a bottle, which is exactly why the marketing ecosystem ignores them.

**Tier 2 — strongest preclinical bet, unproven in humans.** Rapamycin/mTOR inhibition: the most reproducible mammalian life-extension evidence (ITP, three sites, both sexes, late-life dosing), but a real drug with real trade-offs and no proven human longevity benefit. The strongest molecular candidate, properly framed as "most promising," with human and canine trials underway.

**Tier 3 — plausible mechanism, no human outcome.** NAD precursors (NMN/NR), most senolytics outside of trials (dasatinib+quercetin, fisetin), metformin for non-diabetics as a geroprotector, spermidine, and similar. Real, interesting biology; some preclinical support; but the human healthspan/lifespan evidence isn't there. Worth research and, for some, cautious trial participation — not confident recommendation. Notably, even metformin, often listed as a geroscience candidate, has weaker and more contested aging evidence than its reputation suggests, and there's concern it may blunt some exercise adaptations.

**Tier 4 — sold ahead of the data.** Resveratrol as a longevity intervention (largely myth), most proprietary "longevity" stacks, anything resting on epigenetic-clock movement as proof. Real-but-modest mechanisms marketed as proven outcomes.

The discipline this map enforces is reweighting attention. The longevity internet inverts the tiers — endless discussion of Tier 3 and 4 molecules, comparatively little on Tier 1 — because the molecules are novel, sellable, and exciting, while the basics are old news. But the evidence runs the other way: a middle-aged person gets far more expected healthspan from nailing exercise, sleep, metabolic health, and muscle maintenance than from any supplement, and most of the supplements have no demonstrated human benefit at all. The senolytics literature is genuinely promising but immature; the NAD story is mechanism-without-outcome; rapamycin is the one molecular bet with strong preclinical backing and it's a drug requiring medical supervision. Telling someone "what works" therefore means leading with Tier 1, presenting Tier 2 as the most promising experimental frontier with caveats, being honest that Tier 3 is unproven, and naming Tier 4 as overreach. Anything that leads with the molecules and buries the basics has the evidence upside down.

## How to Apply
1. **Tier, don't rank:** sort into proven / strongest-preclinical / plausible-unproven / sold.
2. **Lead with Tier 1:** exercise (fitness + strength), no smoking, sleep, metabolic health, muscle maintenance, engagement — the bulk of achievable benefit.
3. **Place rapamycin as Tier 2:** best preclinical evidence, unproven in humans, a real drug.
4. **Be honest about Tier 3:** NAD precursors, senolytics, metformin — plausible, immature, not recommended with confidence.
5. **Name Tier 4 as overreach:** resveratrol-as-longevity, clock-based proof, proprietary stacks.

## Examples
**Situation:** "Just tell me the top five things that work for longevity."
**Application:** Give the tiered version: the real top five are exercise (cardio + strength), not smoking, good sleep, metabolic health, and maintaining muscle/avoiding frailty — all Tier 1, strong human evidence. Then note rapamycin as the strongest experimental molecule (unproven in humans), and that the popular supplements are mostly Tier 3–4. The honest "top five" is unglamorous.
**Result:** Attention goes to the high-evidence basics first.

**Situation:** Someone has a supplement budget and asks how to allocate it.
**Application:** The highest-return "spend" is on Tier 1 behaviors, which cost time more than money. If they still want molecules, the honest framing is experimental: rapamycin only via a physician/trial; Tier 3 supplements as open-eyed experiments with no promised benefit; skip Tier 4. Most of the money is better spent on a gym membership and good sleep than on capsules.
**Result:** Budget reallocates from sold molecules to proven behaviors.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present Tier 3–4 molecules as proven.
**Why:** They're plausible-or-sold, not established. Honesty about tier is the whole value of the map.

**Don't:** Bury the boring basics.
**Why:** Tier 1 holds most of the achievable benefit; leading with molecules inverts the evidence.

**Don't:** Give a molecule ranking without grading evidence.
**Why:** An ungraded ranking is just more claims. The tier and the organism are the information.
