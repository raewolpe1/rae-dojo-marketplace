# Supplement-Industry BS Detection

## When to Use
- When someone asks whether a specific longevity supplement is worth taking.
- When teaching a repeatable method to see through anti-aging marketing.
- When a product cites impressive-sounding mechanisms or studies and the person can't tell if it's real.
- When separating the small amount of signal from the large amount of noise in the supplement market.

## Fails When
- **It becomes reflexive cynicism.** "It's all snake oil" is as lazy as credulity. The job is to grade the specific claim.
- **You dismiss without engaging the evidence.** Some compounds have real preclinical data; refusing to look is not skepticism.
- **You flatten all supplements together.** A few have genuine mechanistic support; most don't. The grading must be specific.

## Core Concept
The supplement-longevity industry runs on a single repeatable move, and once you see it you can't unsee it: take a real mechanistic finding, extrapolate it past what the evidence supports, attach a product, and market the mechanism as if it were a proven outcome. Detecting BS is therefore not about cynicism — it's about reliably finding where in that chain the overreach happens. Kaeberlein's method is a small set of questions applied to any claim.

**First: what's the evidence tier, and in what organism?** Locate the claim on the ladder — cell culture → yeast → worms → flies → mice → larger mammals/dogs → human observational → human RCT. Most products live near the bottom while advertising as if they were near the top. "Shown to extend lifespan" almost always means "in worms or mice," which is a hypothesis for humans, not a result.

**Second: is this a mechanism or an outcome?** "Activates X," "raises Y," "supports Z pathway" are mechanistic claims. "Slows aging," "extends healthspan," "makes you biologically younger" are outcome claims. The signature dishonesty is presenting the first as if it established the second. Raising a biomarker is not improving a life.

**Third: has it replicated, and where?** A single paper — especially one from a lab or company with a financial stake — is provisional. The gold standard is independent replication (the ITP's three-site model for mice; multiple independent RCTs for humans). Ask whether anyone unaffiliated has reproduced the key result.

**Fourth: who benefits if you believe it, and what's the conflict?** If the people citing the evidence also sell the product, that doesn't make them wrong, but it raises the standard of proof you should demand rather than lowering it. Watch for founders citing their own un-replicated studies as if settled.

**Fifth: what's the biomarker being used, and is it validated?** A great deal of supplement marketing now leans on epigenetic "biological age" clocks. These are unvalidated as endpoints — moving a clock has not been shown to mean slowing aging — so "lowered biological age by X years" is not the proof it appears to be.

Run a claim through those five questions and almost every overhyped product resolves the same way: real-but-modest mechanism, organism far from human, no independent replication, conflicted source, unvalidated biomarker. That's not "snake oil" in the sense of containing nothing real — it's something real being sold for far more than it has earned. And occasionally the questions reveal genuine signal (rapamycin clears more of them than anything else, though it's a drug, not a supplement), which is exactly why the method has to be applied specifically rather than as blanket dismissal.

The discipline cuts both ways, and that's what separates Kaeberlein from a mere debunker. The credulous error treats every plausible mechanism as proven. The cynical error treats the whole category as fraud and refuses to engage. Both skip the actual work, which is to grade this specific claim against this specific evidence and to say, plainly, where it lands: proven, plausible, or sold. Most land on "plausible but sold." A few are genuinely proven. The skill is telling them apart, every time, without prejudice in either direction.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask the organism + tier question:** where on the evidence ladder does this actually sit?
2. **Sort mechanism from outcome:** is the claim about a pathway/biomarker, or about how long/well you live?
3. **Check replication:** independent reproduction, or a single conflicted paper?
4. **Check the incentive:** does the source sell the product? Raise the bar accordingly.
5. **Interrogate the biomarker:** is it a validated endpoint (usually no) or a marketing proxy?
6. **Deliver a specific grade:** proven / plausible-but-unproven / sold — not a blanket verdict.

## Examples
**Situation:** A product page says "clinically proven to reduce biological age, activates longevity pathways, backed by published research."
**Application:** Run the questions. "Activates longevity pathways" — mechanism, not outcome. "Reduces biological age" — relies on an unvalidated clock. "Published research" — check organism, tier, and whether it's the company's own un-replicated study. Almost always this resolves to: real mechanism, low tier, conflicted source, unvalidated biomarker.
**Result:** The product is regraded from "clinically proven" to "mechanistically themed, clinically unproven."

**Situation:** Someone insists "you just think all supplements are garbage."
**Application:** No — the method is specific, not cynical. Some compounds have real mechanistic data; rapamycin (a drug) clears the evidence bar better than anything. The point isn't that nothing works; it's that most products are sold far ahead of their evidence, and the way to know which is which is to grade each claim, not to dismiss or believe the category wholesale.
**Result:** The position is correctly understood as calibration, not blanket rejection.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Default to "it's all snake oil."
**Why:** That's the cynical error. Some claims have real evidence; refusing to grade is not skepticism.

**Don't:** Accept "backed by research" without checking organism, tier, replication, and conflict.
**Why:** Those four words hide the entire overreach. The detail is where the truth is.

**Don't:** Let an epigenetic clock stand in as proof.
**Why:** Clocks are unvalidated endpoints. "Lowered biological age" is a marketing proxy, not an outcome.
