# The Translation Gap from Mouse to Human

## When to Use
- When a claim rests on worm, fly, or mouse data presented as if it were human.
- When explaining why most animal longevity interventions don't pan out in people.
- When calibrating how much weight a preclinical result deserves.
- When teaching the single most ignored fact in the popular longevity conversation.

## Fails When
- **The gap becomes blanket dismissal.** Preclinical evidence is how serious candidates are found; it's the strongest evidence the field has. Don't trash it.
- **You ignore the successes.** Some model-organism biology does translate; the discipline is about probability and timescale, not impossibility.
- **You equate "unproven in humans" with "false."** It means the human question is open, not closed.

## Core Concept
The translation gap is the systematic, well-documented fact that most interventions which extend lifespan or improve healthspan in model organisms do not produce useful benefits in humans. It is the most reliably ignored fact in the popular longevity conversation, and it is the single most important calibration tool for evaluating any aging claim. The reason it gets ignored is that it's inconvenient: it stands between an exciting mouse result and the supplement you'd like to sell or believe in.

There are concrete reasons the gap exists. Short-lived organisms — yeast, worms, flies — are evolutionarily distant from humans and have far simpler physiology; an intervention that doubles a worm's three-week lifespan tells you about worm biology and offers, at most, a hypothesis about a conserved pathway. Even mice, mammals though they are, differ from humans in metabolism, immune function, disease patterns, dosing scale, and lifespan by factors that matter; laboratory mice also live in artificial, pathogen-controlled, sedentary conditions unlike any human life. And the effect sizes typically attenuate as you climb the evolutionary ladder: a robust result in worms is often smaller in mice and smaller still, or absent, in longer-lived animals — the same attenuation seen in the caloric-restriction primate studies. So as a rule of thumb, each step from worm to fly to mouse to dog to human both lowers the probability that the effect survives and shrinks its likely size.

This is why Kaeberlein insists on naming the organism every time and on grading preclinical results as hypotheses rather than conclusions. An effect in C. elegans is a hypothesis worth pursuing. An effect in mice is a stronger hypothesis, especially if it survives independent replication. Neither is a human result. The history of the field is full of compounds that looked transformative in models and did nothing in people — which is not a sign that the science was bad, but a sign that translation is genuinely hard and most candidates don't make it. The honest expectation for any new preclinical finding is that it probably won't translate, and the burden is on showing that it does.

Crucially, the gap is a discipline, not a nihilism, and this is where Kaeberlein parts ways with reflexive debunkers. The point is not that preclinical evidence is worthless — it's the opposite. Model organisms are how the field finds serious candidates; rapamycin, the strongest bet in all of aging biology, rests on mouse data. The point is that preclinical evidence is the beginning of the evidentiary process, not the end. It justifies the next, harder, more expensive steps — larger animals, dogs (the Dog Aging Project exists precisely to narrow this gap), and ultimately human trials — rather than justifying a product launch. The strongest candidates earn translation studies; the gap tells you to demand them before believing the human claim.

Two errors to avoid sit on either side. The credulous error treats a mouse result as a human result and buys the supplement. The cynical error treats the gap as proof that nothing translates and dismisses the whole enterprise. Both are wrong. The correct stance is probabilistic and patient: a preclinical result raises the probability that something real is there, the probability is far below certainty, it shrinks with evolutionary distance and grows with independent replication, and only human evidence settles the human question. Rapamycin illustrates the right posture exactly — strongest preclinical evidence in the field, treated by the people who know it best as a most-promising hypothesis still awaiting human confirmation, with serious translation studies (dogs, humans) underway rather than a confident claim of human benefit.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the organism every time** and grade the result as a hypothesis, not a conclusion.
2. **Explain the attenuation:** effects typically shrink and may vanish moving worm → fly → mouse → dog → human.
3. **State the base rate:** most model-organism interventions don't translate; expect non-translation by default.
4. **Keep it a discipline, not nihilism:** preclinical evidence finds candidates and justifies the next harder step.
5. **Demand the translation studies** (dogs, human trials) before accepting a human claim — and note where they're underway.

## Examples
**Situation:** "This compound doubled lifespan in worms — shouldn't I be taking it?"
**Application:** Worm lifespan is a hypothesis about a possibly conserved pathway, not a human result, and effects this large in worms routinely shrink or vanish in mammals. What's needed is mouse data, ideally independently replicated, then larger-animal and human evidence. Until then it's an interesting lead, not a recommendation.
**Result:** The result is correctly weighted as an early hypothesis.

**Situation:** Someone dismisses rapamycin as "just mouse data, so meaningless."
**Application:** That's the opposite error. Mouse data isn't meaningless — it's how real candidates are found, and rapamycin's is unusually strong (independent replication across ITP sites, both sexes, late-life). The gap means human benefit is unproven and translation studies are needed, not that the evidence is worthless. It's the best hypothesis the field has.
**Result:** The cynical over-correction is rebalanced toward calibrated weighting.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use the gap to dismiss all preclinical evidence.
**Why:** Model organisms are how serious candidates are identified; rapamycin rests on them. The gap calibrates weight, it doesn't zero it.

**Don't:** Treat a mouse or worm result as a human result.
**Why:** Most don't translate, and effects attenuate with evolutionary distance. It's a hypothesis until human data exists.

**Don't:** Read "unproven in humans" as "impossible."
**Why:** It means the human question is open. Translation is hard and slow, not foreclosed.
