# The Dog Aging Project

## When to Use
- When explaining why companion dogs are a serious model for human aging.
- When someone asks what the Dog Aging Project and TRIAD are and why they matter.
- When bridging the gap between mouse longevity data and human translation.
- When teaching what a well-designed translational aging trial looks like.

## Fails When
- **The user wants finished answers.** TRIAD and the larger project are ongoing; the headline lifespan-and-healthspan results are still maturing. Don't manufacture conclusions.
- **You overstate the dog data.** Early signals (e.g., cardiac function) are interesting, not definitive proof of life extension.
- **You equate dogs with humans.** Dogs are a better bridge than mice, but they are still not humans.

## Core Concept
The Dog Aging Project, which Kaeberlein co-founded and directed, is the largest companion-animal longevity study ever conducted — tens of thousands of dogs enrolled, with owners and veterinarians contributing longitudinal health, environment, and lifestyle data. Its scientific logic rests on what makes pet dogs an unusually good model for human aging: they share our physical environment (homes, air, water, often diet and activity patterns), they develop many of the same age-related diseases (cancers, cognitive decline, cardiac and musculoskeletal disease), they have sophisticated veterinary care that allows real clinical assessment, and — crucially — they age much faster than humans, so a longevity study can return answers in years rather than decades. They also carry enormous genetic and size diversity, which lets researchers study how aging varies across very different biology within one species.

Embedded in the project is TRIAD — the Test of Rapamycin in Aging Dogs — a randomized, double-masked, placebo-controlled, multi-site clinical trial in healthy, middle-aged, medium-to-large companion dogs. Its primary question is whether rapamycin extends lifespan and improves healthspan, with cognitive function as a key secondary outcome. This is not a casual observational exercise; it is a rigorously designed trial, the kind whose structure (randomization, blinding, placebo control, independent sites) is exactly what distinguishes evidence from anecdote. That design is the point: it imports the rigor that the supplement world lacks into a naturally aging mammal closer to humans than mice are.

Why this matters for the whole field: rapamycin's strongest evidence comes from the ITP in genetically heterogeneous mice. The obvious objection is that mice are not humans. Dogs sit in between — larger, longer-lived, naturally aging in the human environment, genetically diverse. A positive, well-controlled rapamycin result in companion dogs would substantially strengthen the case that the mouse signal points at something real and translatable; a null result would be important information in the other direction. Either way, TRIAD is a genuine test, not a marketing exercise.

The honest status report is that the data are still maturing. Earlier Dog Aging Project work suggested rapamycin can improve measures like cardiac function in dogs, which is encouraging but is a healthspan-relevant signal, not a demonstration of life extension. The larger lifespan-and-healthspan questions are what the expanded trial is powered to address, and those answers are still coming. The project has also navigated real-world funding turbulence, including a period of jeopardy before additional NIH support arrived — a reminder that even the most credible translational aging science depends on fragile funding.

For someone calibrating longevity claims, the Dog Aging Project is a useful touchstone in two ways. First, it models what serious translational aging research actually looks like: large samples, rigorous trial design, naturally aging subjects, patient timelines, honest interim reporting. Second, it draws the line cleanly — even here, with the strongest candidate (rapamycin) in a good model (dogs) under a rigorous design, the responsible scientists say the results are still maturing rather than declaring victory. That restraint, in the face of genuinely promising biology, is the contrast against which the supplement industry's instant confidence should be judged.

## How to Apply
1. **Explain why dogs are a good bridge:** shared environment, shared diseases, faster aging, genetic diversity, real veterinary assessment.
2. **Describe TRIAD's design** — randomized, double-masked, placebo-controlled, multi-site — as the marker of rigor.
3. **Position it between mouse and human** as a translational test of the rapamycin signal.
4. **Report status honestly:** encouraging early signals (e.g., cardiac function), headline results still maturing.
5. **Use it as a rigor benchmark** against which to judge instant supplement confidence.

## Examples
**Situation:** Someone asks, "Aren't dog studies just cute? Why should I care for my own health?"
**Application:** Dogs share your home, your environment, and many of your age-related diseases, and they age fast enough to give answers in years. A rigorous rapamycin trial in dogs is one of the best available tests of whether the mouse longevity signal translates toward mammals like us. It's not cute — it's one of the more serious translational bets in the field.
**Result:** The person sees the scientific logic rather than novelty.

**Situation:** A marketer claims "studies show rapamycin extends mammalian lifespan, so our rapamycin-mimic supplement works."
**Application:** The mammalian lifespan evidence is in mice; the dog trial designed to test translation is still maturing. And a "rapamycin-mimic" supplement is not rapamycin and carries none of that evidence. Two layers of overreach.
**Result:** The borrowed credibility is removed.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Report TRIAD as having proven rapamycin extends life.
**Why:** It's an ongoing trial; headline results are still maturing. Early signals are not final conclusions.

**Don't:** Treat dog results as human results.
**Why:** Dogs are a better bridge than mice, but still not humans. The translation discipline still applies.

**Don't:** Ignore the rigor of the design when contrasting with supplements.
**Why:** Randomization, blinding, and placebo control are exactly what makes TRIAD evidence and an influencer protocol anecdote.
