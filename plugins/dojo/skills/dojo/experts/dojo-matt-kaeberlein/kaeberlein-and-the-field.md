# Kaeberlein and the Field

## When to Use
- When situating Kaeberlein relative to other longevity voices.
- When someone is triangulating among Sinclair, Johnson, Attia, and the institutions.
- When mapping who agrees and disagrees with him and why.
- When teaching the intellectual landscape of longevity science.

## Fails When
- **The question is really about one framework.** "What does Kaeberlein think of X" is different from "where does he sit in the field."
- **You reduce it to optimists vs skeptics.** The real disagreements are about specific evidence standards and claims, not temperament.
- **You use his authority as a substitute for the analysis.** Kaeberlein himself would say grade the evidence, don't defer to a name.

## Core Concept
Kaeberlein occupies a distinctive and deliberately uncomfortable position: a credentialed academic biogerontologist who is simultaneously the field's most trusted scientific voice and its sharpest internal critic of hype. He is "pro-aging-research, anti-aging-hype," which makes him an awkward fit for almost every camp — too rigorous for the influencers, too willing to defend the legitimacy of aging research for the reflexive debunkers, and too blunt about weak evidence for the supplement industry. Mapping him against the other voices clarifies the whole landscape.

**Versus David Sinclair.** The defining contrast. Same founding lab (Guarente, MIT), opposite public postures. Sinclair is the optimistic, commercially-engaged promoter of NAD/sirtuin interventions and the information theory of aging; Kaeberlein is the skeptic who calls the resveratrol longevity story largely myth and NMN's human benefit unproven. Both know the biology intimately; they differ on how far ahead of the evidence one should speak publicly. This is an evidence-standards disagreement inside the field, not a fight between an expert and an outsider.

**Versus Bryan Johnson.** Kaeberlein treats Blueprint as a "signal-to-noise problem" and an n=1 case study presented with the production values of a clinical trial. The disagreement is methodological: self-experimentation, however elaborate, is the lowest evidence tier, and "science is not n=1." He credits the well-evidenced lifestyle core while discounting the unproven molecular tail and the clock-based rejuvenation claims.

**Versus Peter Attia.** Closer to an ally than an opponent, but with a clear division of labor. Attia operates as a clinician — individualized risk management, lipids/ApoB, training prescription, personal medical decision-making at the bedside. Kaeberlein operates as a bench scientist and evidence-grader — what the aging biology actually shows, what's reproducible, what translates. They overlap heavily on the primacy of exercise and on skepticism of supplement hype; they differ in register (clinical vs basic-science) more than in substance. For a given person, Attia answers "what should I do given my numbers," Kaeberlein answers "what does the evidence actually support."

**Versus the supplement/influencer industry broadly.** This is the clearest opposition. The industry's move — mechanism marketed as outcome, biomarkers as proof, n=1 as evidence — is precisely what his method exists to catch. He is its most credible counterweight because he can't be dismissed as scientifically uninformed.

**Versus mainstream geriatric medicine.** A subtler tension. Conventional medicine treats age-related diseases one at a time, after they appear; the geroscience hypothesis Kaeberlein champions says aging itself is the shared upstream target and should be treatable. Here he's the radical relative to the medical establishment — arguing the target is legitimate — even as he's the conservative relative to the supplement world on what's been proven.

**Where he's broadly aligned.** With the geroscience research community (the ITP, the biology-of-aging field), with rigorous translational efforts (his own Dog Aging Project), and with anyone insisting on reproducibility and human evidence. His institutional homes — formerly UW and its Healthy Aging and Longevity Research Institute, now Optispan as CEO — reflect the attempt to do and communicate aging science rigorously, including translating it for the public without overselling.

The throughline placing him in the field: aging is a real, serious, tractable scientific target (against the cynics and against disease-by-disease medicine), AND most of what's sold under "longevity" is unproven (against Sinclair's specific claims, the supplement industry, and n=1 influencer protocols). He's the calibration instrument precisely because he holds both at once. The right way to use him is not to invoke his authority as a trump card — he'd reject that — but to adopt his procedure: grade the specific claim, name the organism and tier, demand replication and human outcomes, and watch the incentives. Triangulating the other voices against that procedure is more useful than asking which guru to follow.

## How to Apply
1. **Place him on both axes:** pro-aging-research AND anti-hype; that's why he fits no single camp.
2. **Map the contrasts:** Sinclair (evidence-standards opposite, same lab), Johnson (methodological/n=1), Attia (allied, clinical-vs-bench division of labor), the supplement industry (clear opposition), geriatric medicine (he's the radical on the target).
3. **Name the alignments:** geroscience community, reproducibility, translational rigor (Dog Aging Project), Optispan.
4. **State the throughline:** real target + mostly-unproven products, held together.
5. **Use the procedure, not the authority:** triangulate other voices by grading their claims, not by ranking gurus.

## Examples
**Situation:** "I follow Sinclair, Attia, and Johnson and they seem to disagree — where does Kaeberlein fit?"
**Application:** He's the evidence-grader the others get measured against. He's near-opposite Sinclair on specific supplement claims (same biology, different evidence standards), methodologically critical of Johnson (n=1), and largely allied with Attia (clinical vs bench division of labor, shared exercise-first skepticism of hype). Use his procedure to grade what each is claiming rather than picking a favorite.
**Result:** The user gets a map and a method instead of a guru.

**Situation:** Someone wants to cite "Kaeberlein says so" to win an argument.
**Application:** He'd resist that. His whole point is grade the evidence, not defer to a name — including his. The right move is to run his procedure on the specific claim (mechanism vs outcome, organism, replication, biomarker, incentive) and let the grading carry the argument.
**Result:** Authority is replaced with method, which is the Kaeberlein move.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce the field to optimists vs skeptics.
**Why:** The disagreements are about specific evidence standards and claims, not psychological temperament.

**Don't:** Treat Attia and Kaeberlein as opponents.
**Why:** They're largely allied with a clinical-vs-bench division of labor; flattening that loses the useful distinction.

**Don't:** Use Kaeberlein's authority as a substitute for grading the claim.
**Why:** His own method rejects authority-as-proof; invoke the procedure, not the name.
