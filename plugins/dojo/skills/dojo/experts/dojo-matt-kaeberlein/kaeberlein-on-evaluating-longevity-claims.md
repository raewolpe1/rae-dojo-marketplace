# Evaluating Longevity Claims

## When to Use
- When someone wants a general, portable method for judging any aging claim.
- When a new compound, study, headline, or protocol needs assessment.
- When teaching a person to think like Kaeberlein rather than handing them a verdict.
- When the goal is to make the user their own calibrated BS-detector.

## Fails When
- **You give a verdict without the procedure.** The value is the transferable method, not a one-off ruling.
- **You collapse to one pole.** Pure skepticism and pure credulity both skip the actual grading.
- **You skip a check.** Organism, replication, incentive, and biomarker validity each catch a distinct failure mode.

## Core Concept
Evaluating any longevity claim is a procedure, not an intuition, and the whole point is that it's portable: the same small set of questions resolves a supplement ad, a viral study, an influencer protocol, or a new drug. Kaeberlein's method is essentially a structured evidence-grading that ends in one of three verdicts — proven, plausible-but-unproven, or sold — with the reasoning shown so the person can run it themselves next time.

**Question 1: Mechanism or outcome?** Separate "it does something biological" from "it makes you live longer or healthier." "Activates sirtuins," "raises NAD," "reduces senescent cells," "supports autophagy" are mechanistic claims. "Slows aging," "extends healthspan," "reverses biological age" are outcome claims. The signature error of the field is presenting the first as proof of the second. A real mechanism with no demonstrated outcome is, at best, plausible-but-unproven.

**Question 2: What organism, and what evidence tier?** Place the actual evidence on the ladder: cell culture → yeast → worms → flies → mice → larger mammals/dogs → human observational → human RCT. Most claims advertise high and evidence low. An effect in worms or mice is a hypothesis for humans, not a result — the translation gap means most such effects never translate, and they attenuate with evolutionary distance.

**Question 3: Has it replicated, and where?** A single study, especially from a lab or company with a stake, is provisional. Independent replication is the dividing line between provisional and real — the ITP's three-site standard for mice, multiple independent RCTs for humans. Ask who, unaffiliated, has reproduced the key result.

**Question 4: What's the outcome measure — and is it validated?** Survival, function/disease, or a biomarker? If it's a biomarker, is it a validated endpoint? Epigenetic "biological age" clocks are not validated as response-to-intervention readouts, so "lowered biological age" is not the proof it appears to be. A claim resting on an unvalidated biomarker is weaker than it sounds.

**Question 5: Who benefits, and what's the conflict?** If the people citing the evidence also sell the product or have built a brand on it, that doesn't make them wrong, but it raises the standard of proof you should demand. Self-citation of un-replicated work is a particular flag.

Run those five and the verdict usually writes itself. Proven: strong human evidence, ideally replicated, with real outcomes (this is rare, and mostly lifestyle). Plausible-but-unproven: real mechanism, low-tier or non-human evidence, no replicated human outcome (most supplements). Sold: mechanism marketed as outcome, unvalidated biomarkers as proof, conflicted single-source evidence (resveratrol-as-longevity, proprietary stacks). The discipline that makes this Kaeberlein rather than a debunker is that the procedure runs both ways: it catches credulity (treating plausible as proven) and cynicism (treating the whole field as fraud) equally, because it forces you to grade this specific claim against this specific evidence rather than reacting to the genre. Occasionally the questions reveal genuine strength — rapamycin clears more of them than anything else, which is exactly why it's the strongest bet — and that's the proof the method isn't rigged toward "no." It's rigged toward "show me the evidence, at the right tier, replicated, with a real outcome, from a source whose incentives don't lower the bar."

## How to Apply
1. **Mechanism vs outcome:** is the claim about biology done, or about life lived?
2. **Organism + tier:** where on the ladder is the actual evidence, and how far from human?
3. **Replication:** independently reproduced, or single conflicted source?
4. **Outcome measure:** real outcome or biomarker — and is the biomarker validated?
5. **Incentive:** does the source sell it? Raise the bar.
6. **Verdict:** proven / plausible-but-unproven / sold — with the reasoning shown.

## Examples
**Situation:** A viral headline: "New compound reverses aging, study finds."
**Application:** Run it. "Reverses aging" — outcome claim; check the study. Organism — usually mice or cells, so a hypothesis, not a human result. Replication — one paper, often the originating lab. Outcome measure — frequently a biomarker or clock, possibly unvalidated. Incentive — is a company attached? Verdict almost always: plausible mechanism, low tier, unreplicated — interesting, not actionable.
**Result:** The headline is regraded from "reverses aging" to "early single-study hypothesis."

**Situation:** Someone asks how this differs from "just being skeptical of everything."
**Application:** Because the same procedure also certifies strength when it's there. Rapamycin passes the replication test (ITP, three sites, both sexes) where almost nothing else does, so the method grades it the strongest bet, not "more snake oil." It's calibration, not reflexive doubt — it can say yes, it just demands the evidence first.
**Result:** The method is understood as two-directional grading, not blanket cynicism.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Hand over a verdict without the procedure.
**Why:** The transferable method is the gift; a one-off ruling teaches nothing for the next claim.

**Don't:** Default to skepticism or credulity.
**Why:** Both skip the grading. The method must be able to say both "proven" and "sold."

**Don't:** Skip the organism, replication, or biomarker checks.
**Why:** Each catches a distinct, common failure mode; dropping one lets that overreach through.
