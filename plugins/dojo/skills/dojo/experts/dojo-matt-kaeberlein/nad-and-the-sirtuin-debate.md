# The NAD and Sirtuin Debate

## When to Use
- When someone asks whether NMN, NR, or resveratrol slows aging.
- When explaining why the sirtuin/NAD story is real biology but weak as a supplement claim.
- When separating a plausible mechanism from a proven human outcome.
- When grounding the most commercially aggressive corner of the longevity market.

## Fails When
- **You dismiss the biology entirely.** Sirtuins and NAD metabolism are real, important, and worth studying. The critique is of the supplement leap, not the science.
- **You let the mechanism imply the outcome.** "Raises NAD on a blood test" is not "slows your aging."
- **You let a single organism settle it.** Worm and yeast results launched the story; mammalian and human data are where it weakened.

## Core Concept
The NAD/sirtuin program is, in Kaeberlein's case, an inside story — he trained in the Guarente lab at MIT, the birthplace of the sirtuin-longevity hypothesis, and worked on the founding biology himself. That biology is real. Sirtuins are NAD-dependent enzymes involved in metabolism, stress responses, and gene regulation; NAD is a central metabolic coenzyme; NAD levels decline with age in many tissues. In simple organisms, manipulating Sir2/sirtuins and the pathways around them can affect lifespan. None of that is in dispute, and dismissing it as worthless would be its own error.

The problem is the chain of extrapolation that the supplement industry built on top of it. The popular narrative runs: NAD declines with age → sirtuins need NAD → caloric restriction works partly through sirtuins → therefore raising NAD (via NMN or NR) or "activating sirtuins" (via resveratrol) will reproduce CR's benefits and slow human aging. Every link in that chain is treated as if it were established. Most are not. The leaps from "associated with" to "causes," from yeast and worms to mammals, and from "moves a biomarker" to "extends healthspan," are exactly the moves the evidence does not support.

Take the two pieces separately. Resveratrol as a potent sirtuin activator in mammals: the evidence is thin and contested. Early in vitro activation results were confounded by the assay; mouse lifespan studies did not confirm benefit in healthy animals; human results are mixed and modest. Kaeberlein's blunt assessment is that resveratrol as a longevity intervention is largely a myth that continues to mislead the public. Now the NAD precursors, NMN and NR: these can raise measured NAD levels in blood and some tissues — that part is a genuine, measurable mechanism. But whether raising NAD that way slows human aging or extends healthspan is unproven. Human trials are small, short, and mostly powered to detect biomarker changes, not meaningful clinical outcomes. So the honest grade for NMN/NR is: real mechanism, no demonstrated outcome.

This is the cleanest example in the whole field of the central distinction Kaeberlein insists on — mechanism is not outcome. A compound can plausibly engage a real pathway, move a real biomarker, and still do nothing for how long or how well you live. The supplement marketing collapses that distinction on purpose, because "raises NAD, the molecule that declines with aging" sells, while "raises a biomarker with no proven effect on aging" does not. The fact that NAD biology is genuinely interesting is what makes the overreach effective: it's real science wearing a price tag it hasn't earned.

The calibrated position has both halves. The underlying NAD and sirtuin biology is legitimate and worth continued research; it is not snake oil at the level of the science. But the consumer claims built on it — resveratrol activates your sirtuins and slows aging, NMN/NR raises your NAD and extends your healthspan — run far ahead of the human evidence, and Kaeberlein, who knows this biology from the inside, says so plainly and does not take these supplements himself. The disagreement with the most prominent proponent of this story, David Sinclair, is the subject of its own file; here the point is the general one: this is the canonical case of a real mechanism marketed as a proven outcome.

## How to Apply
1. **Affirm the real biology:** sirtuins, NAD metabolism, age-related NAD decline are legitimate science.
2. **Expose the extrapolation chain** and mark which links are unestablished (association→causation, worm→mammal, biomarker→outcome).
3. **Grade resveratrol bluntly:** weak/contested as a mammalian sirtuin activator; the longevity claim is largely myth.
4. **Grade NMN/NR precisely:** raises NAD (real mechanism); slows human aging (unproven outcome).
5. **Name the move:** this is mechanism-sold-as-outcome, the field's signature overreach.

## Examples
**Situation:** "NAD drops as we age and NMN raises it back up, so it must slow aging — right?"
**Application:** The first two clauses are roughly true and the third doesn't follow. Raising a biomarker that declines with age is not the same as slowing aging; you'd need outcome data showing healthspan or mortality benefit, and that data isn't there. It's a plausible idea being sold as a proven one.
**Result:** The mechanism-to-outcome leap is exposed.

**Situation:** A stack pairs resveratrol and NMN as "clinically backed sirtuin support."
**Application:** "Clinically backed" is doing unearned work. Resveratrol as a mammalian sirtuin activator is weak and contested; NMN's clinical evidence is biomarker-level, short, and small. "Sirtuin support" is a mechanistic phrase, not an outcome. The pairing markets real biology it hasn't validated.
**Result:** Regraded from "clinically backed" to "mechanistically themed, clinically unproven."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Trash the underlying NAD/sirtuin science.
**Why:** It's legitimate, important biology. The critique targets the supplement leap, not the field.

**Don't:** Accept "raises NAD" as evidence of slowed aging.
**Why:** That's a biomarker movement, not an outcome. The whole error lives in conflating them.

**Don't:** Treat early worm/yeast results as settling mammalian or human benefit.
**Why:** The story launched in simple organisms and weakened in mammals and humans. Organism matters.
