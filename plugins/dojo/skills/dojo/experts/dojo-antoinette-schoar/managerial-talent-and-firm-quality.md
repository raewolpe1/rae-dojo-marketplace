# Managerial Talent and Firm Quality

## When to Use
- When capital has been provided and growth has not followed.
- When designing management or BDS support for the growth segment.
- When ownership structure (family firms, business groups) is relevant.

## Fails When
- **You need the experimental evidence on whether management training works.** Route to McKenzie; Schoar frames the constraint, McKenzie measures the treatment.

## Core Concept

Alongside regulation and capital, Schoar identifies **managerial talent and governance** as a determinant of whether transformational entrepreneurship occurs. The point is structural rather than motivational: capital deployed into a firm without managerial capability produces a larger instance of the same firm, not a better one. Growth is a management problem before it is a finance problem.

This has three implications policy tends to miss.

**First, quality is distributed, not universal.** The distribution of managerial talent in an economy is wide. Most firms are badly managed and remain so. A small number are well managed and it shows up in everything — productivity, survival, employment. This is a selection statement as much as a treatment statement.

**Second, ownership structure conditions performance.** Schoar's work on family firms and on Thai business groups shows that who owns and who runs a firm materially shapes what it does. Family succession, in particular, is a governance event with measurable performance consequences. In emerging markets, where business groups and family ownership dominate the mid-market, a strategy that assumes professionalised arm's-length management is describing a firm population that does not exist locally.

**Third, capability constrains absorption.** A firm that cannot manage twenty people cannot usefully be financed to employ twenty people. The sequencing question — capability first, or capital first — is real, and the honest answer is that they are complements, which is inconvenient for programme design because complements must be delivered together.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask whether the firm can absorb the resource.** Capital, contracts, and market access all require managerial capacity to convert.
2. **Look at the ownership form.** Family firm? Group affiliate? Owner-manager with no second layer? Each implies a different growth constraint.
3. **Treat the second management hire as the growth threshold.** The move from owner-does-everything to a management layer is the discontinuity that matters.
4. **Do not separate capability support from capital support** in the growth segment. They are complements.
5. **Hand the effect-size question to the evidence.** Whether a given management intervention works is McKenzie's question; that capability binds is Schoar's.

## Examples

**Situation:** A growth-finance programme reports that recipients used the capital but did not add employment.

**Application:** The likely reading is an absorption constraint, not a misuse of funds. The firm bought equipment because equipment does not need managing; it did not hire because hiring requires supervision, payroll, compliance, and a management layer the owner does not have. The design error was delivering capital as a standalone. Growth-segment finance should be bundled with the capability to absorb it — and the eligibility screen should have tested for a management layer or the intent to build one.

**Result:** A redesigned instrument where capital and capability move together, and where absorption capacity is an eligibility criterion.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat capability and capital as sequential pillars.
**Why:** They are complements. Delivered separately, each is under-productive.

**Don't:** Assume the professionalised firm is the norm.
**Why:** In emerging markets the mid-market is dominated by family firms and business groups with different governance and different constraints.

**Don't:** Read "capital used, no jobs added" as fraud or as failure of intent.
**Why:** It is usually an absorption constraint, which is a design failure upstream.
