# Entrepreneurial Selection and Targeting

## When to Use
- When designing screening for growth-oriented support.
- When asked whether high-growth potential can be identified in advance.
- When a programme's results are being attributed to its treatment.

## Fails When
- **The intervention is universal by design** (e.g. a regulatory change) — selection is not the operative variable.

## Core Concept

If the two populations are distinct and the transition between them is negligible, then **selection carries most of the weight in any growth-oriented programme**. The question is not primarily what you deliver but to whom.

This has an uncomfortable corollary. Where a selective programme produces growth firms, the causal contribution of the treatment and the causal contribution of the selection are confounded in the way people usually read the result. A programme that finds transformational entrepreneurs and gives them capital has done two things: identification and financing. The identification may be the larger part of the value — and it is the part that cannot be scaled by increasing the budget.

Selection also runs in the other direction. **Self-selection into entrepreneurship is shaped by the downside.** Where failure is catastrophic — no safety net, no bankruptcy regime, social stigma, household ruin — the only people who start growth ventures are those who can afford to lose. That selects on wealth, not on talent. A regulatory environment that provides a safety net attracts more entrepreneurs, and disproportionately successful ones, because it changes who finds it rational to try.

Practical implications: screening instruments (business plans, panels, scored applications, prior track record) have real information value and should be treated as an asset in their own right, not as administrative overhead. And a programme's eligibility criteria are its most consequential design decision — more consequential than its curriculum.

## How to Apply

1. **Treat selection as the primary design variable.** Spend the design effort there.
2. **Ask what the screen actually screens on.** Literacy? Confidence? Existing formality? Each of these selects on something other than growth potential.
3. **Separate identification value from treatment value.** If the programme's main achievement is finding the firms, say so — and consider whether the finding could be made available more cheaply than the funding.
4. **Examine the downside for the population you want.** If failure is ruinous, the talented are not applying. Fix the downside before concluding the talent is absent.
5. **Accept a small programme.** Selectivity and scale are in tension. A selective growth programme reaching a few hundred firms is the right shape.

## Examples

**Situation:** A province wants to identify and support its high-growth firms but has no basis for choosing them.

**Application:** Design the screen first and the support second. A competitive, scored, panel-assessed application process is not bureaucratic friction — it is the mechanism that does most of the work. Build the scoring on observable growth intent and capability, not on existing size or formality (both of which select on incumbency). Then be honest that the resulting programme will be small.

**Result:** A selective instrument whose value derives from identification as much as from finance, and whose scale is matched to the size of the transformational population.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Design the curriculum before the eligibility criteria.
**Why:** Selection dominates. You are optimising the second-order variable.

**Don't:** Screen on current formality or current size.
**Why:** These select incumbents, not growth potential — and exclude exactly the constrained transformational entrepreneurs you are looking for.

**Don't:** Assume low participation by talented people means the talent is missing.
**Why:** It usually means the downside of failure is too high for anyone without a cushion.
