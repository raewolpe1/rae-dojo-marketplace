# The Divide: Subsistence vs Transformational Entrepreneurship

## When to Use
- Whenever a policy document uses "entrepreneur" without qualification.
- When a segmented strategy is being designed or reviewed.
- When self-employment or TEA rates are being read as indicators of economic health.

## Fails When
- **The unit is a single firm.** The divide is a population-level distinction.
- **The question is about worker rights or informal livelihoods as an end in themselves.** That is Chen's terrain, and Schoar has little to add.

## Core Concept

Two entirely distinct sets of entrepreneurs are conflated under one word.

**Subsistence entrepreneurs** are concerned solely with survival. They run tiny businesses — typically own-account, often informal — and are unlikely to grow or to create jobs. They are numerous: in most developing countries they are the overwhelming majority of everyone counted as an entrepreneur. They remain an important economic pillar, absorbing labour and sustaining households.

**Transformational entrepreneurs** are a considerably smaller group. They strive for growth, are generally larger business owners, and provide relatively secure employment to others. They are the catalysts of innovation, job creation, productivity and competitiveness.

The two groups vary in three respects: their **economic objectives**, their **skills**, and their **role in the economy**.

**The non-transition finding.** Only a negligible fraction of subsistence entrepreneurs ever transition to transformational entrepreneurship. They are not an early stage of the same thing. This is the finding that breaks most enterprise strategy architecture, because most such architecture is built as a ladder.

**The differential response.** The two groups respond very differently to policy changes and to economic cycles. A recession pushes people into subsistence entrepreneurship (no wage jobs available) and out of transformational entrepreneurship (demand collapses, credit tightens). Aggregate self-employment therefore rises in bad times — which makes it a perverse indicator if read as entrepreneurial vitality.

**The policy consequence.** Most development policy aiming to foster entrepreneurship focuses on subsistence entrepreneurship in the hope of creating transformational entrepreneurs. Unless the difference between the two is understood clearly, many policy interventions might have unintended consequences, or even impact the economy adversely from what they were intended to do.

## How to Apply

1. **Classify before instrumenting.** State which population each pillar, initiative, or indicator addresses.
2. **Audit for the smuggled transition.** Search the document for progression language — "graduate", "move up", "pathway", "pipeline", "ladder", arrows between segments. Each instance needs transition evidence or removal.
3. **Separate the outcome sets.** Subsistence outcomes: income stability, consumption smoothing, survival, labour absorption, reduced vulnerability. Transformational outcomes: employment growth, productivity, innovation, firm survival at scale.
4. **Re-read the aggregate indicators.** If TEA or self-employment rates are being used as success measures, note that they move with distress.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial strategy with three segments — micro/informal, traditional SME, high-growth — and an employment target attached to the whole suite.

**Application:** The three-way split maps well onto the divide (segment 1 is subsistence; segment 3 is transformational; segment 2 is a mixed middle where the interesting policy questions actually live). But the employment target cannot be carried by segment 1. Re-allocate the employment rationale to segments 2 and 3, and give segment 1 an honest set of livelihood and absorption objectives.

**Result:** A strategy whose targets are attached to the populations that can deliver them, and whose micro-segment support survives evaluation because it is measured against what it actually does.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Draw the segments as a progression.
**Why:** The transition rate is negligible. A design premised on movement between populations is premised on something that does not happen at scale.

**Don't:** Abandon the subsistence segment because it does not create jobs.
**Why:** It remains an important economic pillar and the primary income source for a large share of households. The error is the growth rationale, not the support.

**Don't:** Read rising self-employment as a good sign.
**Why:** It rises in downturns. The two populations move in opposite directions across the cycle.
