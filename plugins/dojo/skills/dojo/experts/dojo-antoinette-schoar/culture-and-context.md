# Culture, Context and the Entrepreneurship Rate

## When to Use
- When "entrepreneurship culture" is proposed as a cross-cutting theme or intervention.
- When TEA, self-employment, or business registration counts are used as vitality indicators.
- When weak enterprise performance is attributed to attitudes.

## Fails When
- **The question is how to teach entrepreneurial decision-making.** Sarasvathy owns that; Schoar is sceptical of culture as a *causal* variable but has no method to offer.

## Core Concept

Schoar's work with Iyer on the cultural determinants of entrepreneurship, and the comparative volume with Lerner on international differences, establish that entrepreneurship rates vary enormously across countries — and that the variation is much less about attitudes than the rhetoric assumes.

**The indicator problem.** Aggregate entrepreneurship rates measure mostly subsistence entrepreneurship, because that is what most entrepreneurship is. So a high TEA rate is substantially a statement about the absence of wage employment, not about entrepreneurial vitality. Poor countries have higher rates of entrepreneurship than rich ones. This is not a puzzle to be celebrated; it is a measure of the labour market's failure to absorb.

**The direction of causation.** Where transformational entrepreneurship is rare, the culture explanation ("our people are risk-averse", "we lack an entrepreneurial mindset") is attractive because it is cheap and blames no institution. The competing explanations — regulation of labour and product markets, access to capital, the catastrophic downside of failure — are institutional, expensive, and politically awkward. Schoar's disposition is to exhaust the institutional explanations before reaching for culture.

**The safety-net point restated in cultural terms.** What looks like risk aversion is frequently rational response to an environment with no cushion. Provide a safety net and the same "risk-averse" population starts firms, and the ones who start are the successful ones. The attitude did not change; the payoff structure did.

**What culture may genuinely capture.** Norms about failure, about lending within family networks, about the legitimacy of profit, and about who is permitted to be an entrepreneur (gender, ethnicity, class) are real and consequential. But these are closer to institutions than to sentiment, and they respond to institutional change.

## How to Apply

1. **Refuse the vitality reading of aggregate rates.** State plainly what a high TEA rate in a high-unemployment economy is measuring.
2. **Order the explanations.** Regulation, capital, downside, talent — then culture.
3. **Reframe risk aversion as payoff structure.** Ask what happens to a founder who fails here, and whether that explains the observed behaviour without any appeal to attitudes.
4. **If a culture intervention survives, scope it honestly.** It targets norms and permission, not the rate; it is slow; it will not show up in indicators within a strategy cycle.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy proposes an entrepreneurship culture programme as a cross-cutting theme, citing a TEA rate at par with the global average as evidence of untapped potential.

**Application:** The TEA rate is mostly measuring subsistence entrepreneurship, which is mostly measuring the absence of wage jobs. Being "at the global average" on that indicator tells you very little about the availability of transformational entrepreneurs. Before funding a culture intervention, establish whether the institutional constraints — labour and product market regulation, growth capital, the downside of failure — are binding. If they are, culture is the residual, and funding the residual first is the wrong order.

**Result:** The culture theme survives but is scoped to norms and permission, and the institutional constraints move ahead of it in the sequence.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Read a high entrepreneurship rate as economic vitality.
**Why:** It is largely a measure of labour market failure.

**Don't:** Reach for culture before exhausting the institutional explanations.
**Why:** Culture is the cheap answer that implicates nobody. That is precisely why it is over-selected.

**Don't:** Treat observed risk aversion as a fixed trait.
**Why:** It is often a rational response to a catastrophic downside. Change the downside and the behaviour changes.
