# Policy Mistargeting

## When to Use
- When a programme's justification (jobs, growth, innovation) sits above a delivery model that reaches survival enterprises.
- When explaining why an enterprise support programme produced participation but no performance.
- When reach targets and outcome targets are both being set.

## Fails When
- **The programme is honestly framed as livelihood support.** Then there is no mistargeting to expose.

## Core Concept

The dominant failure mode of entrepreneurship policy in developing countries is a mismatch between the population targeted and the outcome promised.

The structure recurs: the **rationale** is transformational (we need firms that employ people, innovate, raise productivity); the **delivery** is subsistence-facing (we will reach 200,000 small enterprises). These are incompatible commitments. The firms reachable at that scale are survival businesses that will not employ anyone; the firms that would employ people number in the low thousands and require a different, more selective, and more politically uncomfortable programme.

Schoar's claim is stronger than "this is wasteful". Unless the difference between the populations is understood, interventions **might have unintended consequences or even impact the economy adversely from what they were intended to do**. Mechanisms include: subsidising entry into already-saturated survival markets, thereby displacing incumbent survivalists; diverting scarce capable people into micro-retail and away from wage employment or growth ventures; and crowding out the far smaller, better-targeted programmes that would have reached transformational firms, because the mass programme consumes the budget and the political credit.

The diagnostic question is therefore not "did the programme work?" but "on whom was it ever going to work?"

## How to Apply

1. **Read the rationale and the reach target side by side.** If reach is in the tens or hundreds of thousands and the rationale is employment, name the incompatibility.
2. **Ask what the counterfactual firm does.** For a survivalist, the counterfactual to the programme is usually a slightly smaller version of the same business — not non-existence.
3. **Check for displacement.** In a saturated local market, supporting entry means someone else exits. Net employment may be zero even where gross participation is high.
4. **Recommend the split.** Two programmes, two rationales, two evaluation frameworks. Resist the merged programme that promises both.

## Examples

**Situation:** An evaluation finds that a large enterprise support programme raised participation and satisfaction but produced no measurable employment growth.

**Application:** This is not an implementation failure. It is the predicted result of aiming a job-creation rationale at a population that does not create jobs. The programme may still have been worth running — for income stability and labour absorption — but it should never have been evaluated on employment.

**Result:** The evaluation is reframed against livelihood outcomes; a separate, smaller, selective instrument is designed for the transformational segment.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Conclude the programme was badly implemented.
**Why:** The target was wrong before implementation began. Fixing delivery will not fix targeting.

**Don't:** Assume mass reach is politically necessary and therefore analytically acceptable.
**Why:** It may be politically necessary. Say so explicitly and adjust the promised outcomes rather than pretending the economics work.

**Don't:** Treat displacement as a second-order concern in saturated markets.
**Why:** In dense informal retail, it may be the first-order effect.
