# The Pandemic Glimpse

## When to Use

- When using the COVID-19 pandemic as a lens to examine what a post-growth economy might feel like in practice
- When discussing the politics of "building back better" versus "returning to normal"
- When a concrete, lived example is needed to make abstract post-growth arguments tangible

## Core Concept

In the early months of 2020, the global economy contracted at a speed and scale unprecedented in peacetime. GDP collapsed. Air travel ceased. Commuting stopped. Factories fell silent. And for a brief, disorienting moment, something unexpected happened. The skies cleared over Delhi and Beijing. The canals of Venice ran clear. Birdsong returned to cities. Carbon emissions fell by the largest amount ever recorded in a single year. People baked bread, tended gardens, called their neighbours, and — despite the fear and grief and isolation — discovered that some version of a slower, quieter, more local life was not only possible but, in specific ways, preferable.

Jackson calls this the "glimpse" — not a model of the post-growth economy, but an accidental revelation of what the growth economy suppresses. The pandemic did not deliver post-growth prosperity. It delivered crisis, suffering, and savage inequality. But embedded within the crisis were signals about an alternative way of living that the noise of the growth economy normally drowns out.

The signals were specific and instructive. The revaluation of care work — suddenly, nurses, cleaners, delivery drivers, and supermarket workers were "essential," while much of the financial economy proved dispensable. The rediscovery of locality — with travel restricted, people rediscovered their immediate environment, their neighbours, their local ecosystems. The demonstration that emissions can fall dramatically when economic activity is redirected — not through technological wizardry but through simply doing less of the ecologically destructive things. And the revelation of what busyness costs — the commutes, the meetings, the flights that consumed time without producing wellbeing.

But the glimpse was also a warning. The pandemic exposed the brutal inequalities that the growth economy produces and that a post-growth transition must not replicate. Those who could work from home in comfortable houses with gardens experienced something quite different from those in overcrowded housing, precarious employment, or countries without social safety nets. The glimpse was unevenly distributed, and any honest post-growth vision must reckon with this.

For development practitioners, the pandemic is a case study in involuntary degrowth — and in why voluntary, planned, equitable post-growth transition is infinitely preferable to crisis-driven contraction. The lesson is not "lockdown was good" but "the speed with which governments returned to growth-at-all-costs, abandoning every lesson from the crisis, reveals the depth of the structural and mythological lock-in that post-growth thinking must address."

## How to Apply

1. **Use the pandemic as a diagnostic, not a prescription.** The pandemic was not a post-growth experiment — it was a catastrophe that accidentally revealed certain truths. Use these revelations to identify what the growth economy suppresses (ecological quality, community connection, time, care) without romanticising the crisis itself.

2. **Examine the "return to normal" critically.** When post-pandemic recovery is framed as restoring GDP growth, ask: which elements of "normal" are worth restoring, and which were already broken? The rush to return to 2019 is a refusal to learn from the glimpse.

3. **Track what was revalued and what was re-marginalised.** During the pandemic, care workers were heroes. Within months of the "recovery," they were back to low pay and poor conditions. This pattern — brief revaluation followed by structural re-marginalisation — reveals the depth of the growth economy's hierarchy of value.

4. **Design for voluntary transition, not crisis contraction.** The pandemic showed that involuntary economic contraction falls hardest on the most vulnerable. A post-growth transition must be planned, equitable, and democratically governed — the opposite of a pandemic lockdown. Use the pandemic to argue for the urgency of proactive transition over reactive crisis.

## Examples

**Situation:** A city government is developing a post-pandemic economic recovery strategy. The default approach is stimulus spending aimed at restoring pre-pandemic GDP levels as quickly as possible. An adviser wants to propose an alternative "build forward" strategy that retains some of the pandemic's accidental benefits.

**Application:** Identify the specific pandemic-era changes that improved quality of life and could be made permanent through policy rather than crisis. Remote and hybrid work reduced commuting emissions and gave workers time — make it structural through planning policy that supports distributed work hubs and reduces car-dependent commuting. Cleaner air resulted from reduced traffic — lock it in through low-emission zones, cycling infrastructure, and public transport investment. Community mutual aid networks emerged spontaneously — support them with small grants, community spaces, and institutional recognition. The revaluation of care workers was rhetorical — make it material through a care economy investment programme with professional wages, training pathways, and public funding. Frame the strategy not as rejecting recovery but as recovering the right things: the community solidarity, the ecological quality, and the revaluation of care that the pandemic briefly made visible. Ensure the strategy explicitly addresses equity — the pandemic's benefits were concentrated among the privileged, and the "build forward" strategy must direct investment toward those who bore the heaviest costs.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Romanticise the pandemic. People died, livelihoods were destroyed, mental health suffered, and existing inequalities were savagely amplified. Using "dolphins in Venice" as a feel-good story without acknowledging the human cost is offensive and undermines credibility.
**Why:** Jackson is careful to hold both truths simultaneously: the pandemic was a catastrophe, and it revealed things about alternative futures that the growth economy normally conceals. Losing either half of this makes the argument either callous or dishonest.

**Don't:** Treat the pandemic's emission reductions as evidence that degrowth is easy. Emissions fell because of forced economic contraction that devastated lives. Planned, equitable post-growth transition looks nothing like a lockdown. Using the pandemic to suggest that emission reductions are simple misrepresents the challenge and alienates anyone who suffered economically.
**Why:** The argument is not "we should do this again." The argument is "the fact that we rushed to undo every ecological and social gain from the pandemic, rather than asking which gains were worth keeping, reveals how deeply the growth imperative is embedded — and why a deliberate, just transition is necessary."
