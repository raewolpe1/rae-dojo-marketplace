# The Mythology of Growth

## When to Use

- When the conversation reveals unexamined assumptions about growth being natural, inevitable, or self-evidently desirable
- When a policy discussion treats GDP growth as the default objective without interrogating why
- When helping someone understand the cultural and psychological — not just structural — forces that sustain the growth paradigm

## Core Concept

Economic growth is not merely a policy objective. It is a mythology — a story so deeply embedded in modern consciousness that it has become invisible as a story. We experience it as reality, as the natural order of things, as common sense. But it is none of these. It is a particular narrative, with a particular history, serving particular interests, and it can be told differently.

The mythology runs something like this: human beings are insatiably acquisitive by nature. Progress means producing and consuming more. Technology will always find solutions. The market is the most efficient allocator of resources. Growth lifts all boats. And the arc of history bends toward abundance. Each element of this story is contestable, and several are demonstrably false. But they function not as hypotheses to be tested but as articles of faith — the catechism of the growth economy.

Jackson traces this mythology through several layers. There is the Enlightenment story of linear progress — the conviction that tomorrow will be better than today, measured in material terms. There is the capitalist story of creative destruction — Schumpeter's insight that growth requires the relentless displacement of the old by the new, a process that generates enormous dynamism and enormous waste. There is the consumer story of identity-through-acquisition — the idea that who we are is expressed through what we buy, a story relentlessly amplified by advertising and social media. And there is the political story of growth as legitimacy — the implicit social contract in which governments deliver rising living standards in exchange for democratic consent.

These stories are not incidental to the growth economy. They are its operating system. The growth economy does not merely produce goods; it produces the desire for goods. It does not merely generate income; it generates the anxiety that more income might relieve. Consumer capitalism is, at its core, a system for the manufacture of dissatisfaction — because satisfied people do not need to consume more.

For development practitioners, recognising growth as mythology is operationally important. It means that resistance to post-growth ideas is not simply about evidence or interests — it is about identity, meaning, and the stories people live inside. Changing the economic paradigm requires changing the story, and that is cultural work as much as technical work. It also means noticing when the mythology is doing the thinking — when "we need growth" functions as a conversation-stopper rather than an argument, when growth is treated as a premise rather than a conclusion.

## How to Apply

1. **Name the story as a story.** When growth is invoked as self-evident necessity, gently surface the narrative: "That is one story about how economies work. What is the evidence for it, and what alternative stories are available?" This is not dismissive — it is an invitation to think rather than assume.

2. **Trace the mythology to its sources.** When someone says "people will always want more," ask: is that human nature, or is it the product of an advertising industry that spends hundreds of billions annually manufacturing desire? When someone says "growth lifts all boats," ask: whose boats, and at what ecological cost?

3. **Identify the political function.** Growth-as-legitimacy is a powerful force. Governments fear that abandoning the growth target will be punished electorally. Acknowledging this political reality — rather than dismissing it — opens space for discussing alternative bases of political legitimacy: wellbeing improvement, ecological stewardship, reduced inequality, expanded time.

4. **Offer counter-narratives.** Mythology is not defeated by data — it is replaced by better stories. The post-growth narrative is a story of homecoming: from the restless, anxious pursuit of more to the recognition that enough is not a limitation but a liberation. From the treadmill to the garden. From the supply chain to the community.

## Examples

**Situation:** A national planning commission is debating whether to retain GDP growth as the headline target in its next five-year plan. Proponents argue that growth is necessary to fund public services, reduce poverty, and maintain investor confidence. Opponents cite ecological limits but struggle to articulate an alternative that sounds credible to the commission.

**Application:** Surface the mythology operating in the room. The claim that "growth is necessary to fund public services" is not an empirical finding — it is a structural feature of the current fiscal architecture, which is designed to depend on growth. Redesign the architecture (progressive taxation, financial transaction levies, public banking, reformed pension systems) and the dependency dissolves. The claim that "growth reduces poverty" conflates aggregate output with distribution — in most growing economies of the past forty years, the gains have accrued overwhelmingly to the top decile while poverty has proved stubbornly persistent. The claim about "investor confidence" names a real political constraint but one that depends on investors believing the growth story. The strategic task is not to argue against growth in the abstract but to show the commission that they are inside a story — and that a different story, with different institutional design, can deliver the outcomes they actually want (poverty reduction, public service funding, stability) without the ecological costs that growth imposes.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat people who believe in growth as stupid or brainwashed. The growth mythology is powerful because it contains genuine truths — growth has delivered real material improvements for billions of people. The critique is not that growth was always wrong but that it has reached the limits of its ecological and social viability.
**Why:** Condescension is the fastest way to lose an audience. Jackson's effectiveness comes from respecting the intelligence and lived experience of people who disagree with him. The mythology is compelling because it captures something real. Honouring that before showing its limits is essential.

**Don't:** Reduce the growth critique to anti-capitalism. Post-growth has implications for capitalism, but the ecological arithmetic applies to any system that pursues perpetual material expansion — state socialism grew ecologically destructive economies too. The issue is throughput, not ownership.
**Why:** Collapsing post-growth into anti-capitalism narrows the audience to those already persuaded and alienates the mainstream policymakers and development practitioners who are Jackson's primary interlocutors. The argument is biophysical and anthropological, not primarily ideological.
