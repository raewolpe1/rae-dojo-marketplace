# The Economy of Care, Craft, and Culture

## When to Use

- When identifying the economic activities that form the foundation of a post-growth society
- When challenging the assumption that productivity growth is always desirable
- When a development strategy needs to value care work, artisanal production, or cultural activity as core economic contributions rather than residual sectors

## Core Concept

The growth economy has a structural bias toward activities where labour productivity can be increased — where machines replace human time, where scale reduces unit costs, where automation drives output per worker upward. This bias is so deeply embedded that we barely notice it. We call productivity growth "efficiency" and treat it as an unqualified good. But there is a vast domain of economic activity where productivity growth is neither possible nor desirable, and this domain is not marginal. It is the foundation of human flourishing.

Care — looking after children, the elderly, the sick, each other — cannot be made more productive without degrading the quality of the care itself. You cannot halve the time a nurse spends with a patient and call it an efficiency gain. You cannot automate the presence of a parent with a frightened child. The same applies to craft — the skill and attention of the woodworker, the small-scale farmer, the repairer — and to culture — teaching, music, theatre, storytelling, community ritual. These are what the economist William Baumol called activities with a "cost disease": their costs rise relative to the rest of the economy precisely because they resist productivity improvement. In the growth economy, this makes them look like problems. In a post-growth economy, they are the solution.

Care, craft, and culture share three characteristics that make them the core of post-growth economics. They are labour-intensive, creating meaningful employment without requiring material throughput growth. They are relationally rich, delivering wellbeing through human connection rather than commodity consumption. And they are ecologically light, contributing minimally to carbon emissions and material extraction relative to the wellbeing they provide.

The post-growth economy is not an economy without work. It is an economy where work is reoriented toward care, craft, culture, and ecological restoration — activities where human time and attention are the primary inputs and where the quality of the outcome depends on the quality of the relationship. For development practitioners, this reframe opens new strategic territory: investing in the care infrastructure, supporting artisanal and small-scale production, funding cultural institutions and practices, and valuing these not as supplements to the "real" economy but as its core.

## How to Apply

1. **Audit the productivity assumption.** When a development strategy emphasises productivity growth, ask: in which sectors? If the target sectors are care, education, or culture, productivity growth is the wrong metric. Quality of service, accessibility, and relational depth are the appropriate measures.

2. **Value care work explicitly.** Unpaid care work — disproportionately performed by women — is the largest unvalued economic activity on earth. A post-growth strategy makes this work visible, valued, and supported through public investment, time policies, and institutional recognition.

3. **Design for craft and repair.** Support economic structures that favour durability, repairability, and artisanal quality over disposable mass production. This means different intellectual property regimes, different procurement standards, different definitions of value for money.

4. **Invest in cultural infrastructure.** Libraries, community centres, public spaces, festivals, arts programmes, and local media are not luxuries to be cut when budgets tighten. They are the infrastructure of a post-growth economy — the places where non-material wellbeing is produced and shared.

## Examples

**Situation:** A municipality is redesigning its economic development strategy after a major employer — a manufacturing plant — closed. The instinct is to attract a replacement employer of similar scale. An alternative approach is needed.

**Application:** Instead of seeking a single large employer, design a diversified local economy around care, craft, and ecological restoration. Invest in a community care hub that integrates childcare, eldercare, and health services — creating skilled, meaningful employment while addressing the municipality's demographic needs. Support a maker space and artisan incubator that helps local craftspeople develop sustainable livelihoods in repair, small-batch production, and adaptive reuse. Fund a community cultural programme — a local theatre, a music school, a storytelling initiative — that builds social connection and civic identity. Commission an ecological restoration programme for degraded local landscapes, creating green jobs in habitat management, water quality improvement, and urban greening. None of these individually replaces the output of the factory. Together, they create a more resilient, more equitable, and more ecologically sound local economy — one that does not depend on attracting the next footloose manufacturer but builds prosperity from the capabilities and relationships of the community itself.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Romanticise care work as inherently fulfilling while ignoring its material conditions. Care work is often underpaid, precarious, physically demanding, and disproportionately borne by women and minorities. Valuing care means paying for it properly, providing decent conditions, and distributing it more equitably.
**Why:** A post-growth economy that celebrates care without funding it is just austerity with better rhetoric. Jackson's argument is structural — the care economy needs public investment, professional recognition, and institutional support, not just cultural revaluation.

**Don't:** Treat care, craft, and culture as a complete replacement for all other economic activity. Modern societies need energy systems, digital infrastructure, food production, housing, and transport. The post-growth argument is about rebalancing — shifting the centre of gravity from throughput-intensive to care-intensive — not about eliminating material production.
**Why:** Overclaiming invites easy rebuttal. The care-craft-culture thesis is strongest when presented as a reorientation of economic priorities, not as a fantasy of a world without material production.
