## When to Use

Use this when a team or organization feels stuck — low energy, slow decision-making, poor follow-through — or conversely, when a leader wants to understand how to build and protect a sustained period of high performance. Also useful when a leader is considering a major change initiative and needs to think about the sequencing of early moves.

## Fails When

Momentum is not a substitute for strategy or substance. A team with high energy moving in the wrong direction is not better off than a stuck team — it is more efficiently heading toward the wrong outcome. Maxwell's momentum framework assumes the direction is correct and addresses the question of how to build and sustain the energy to execute it.

## Core Concept

Maxwell's Law of Big Mo (from *The 21 Irrefutable Laws of Leadership*) states: momentum is a leader's best friend. It is easier to steer a moving vehicle than a stationary one. When an organization has momentum, everything works better: problems seem manageable, people feel energized, recruiting is easier, decisions move faster. When an organization lacks momentum, everything feels harder: the same problems feel overwhelming, people are depleted, and even good decisions get bogged down in doubt.

Several properties of momentum are important:

**Momentum requires a catalyst.** It doesn't appear on its own. Someone — usually the leader — has to generate the first movement, and the first movement is almost always harder than all subsequent movements. Maxwell uses the image of a boulder: it takes enormous force to get it rolling from a dead stop, but once rolling, the same force that started it can barely slow it down.

**Early wins are disproportionately valuable.** The first victories in a new initiative or a turnaround situation do not need to be the most important victories — they need to be wins. Visible, real, attributable to the team's effort. A string of early wins creates the psychological and social conditions under which larger wins become possible.

**Momentum multiplies capacity.** A team with momentum accomplishes things it could not accomplish without it. Momentum is not just pleasant — it is genuinely enabling. It gives a team access to discretionary effort, creative problem-solving, and resilience that simply aren't available to a team operating from depletion.

**Momentum can be lost.** The conditions that produce momentum — trust, energy, clear wins, alignment — can erode. The primary enemies of momentum are prolonged ambiguity, visible failure that is unexplained and unaddressed, leadership inconsistency, and large projects with no intermediate wins.

**The leader's job is to make the first move.** Maxwell is explicit: organizations do not spontaneously generate momentum. Leaders create it. This means the leader has to be willing to act before certainty, to celebrate progress before completion, and to model energy even when they don't feel it.

Building momentum typically follows a sequence: (1) Leader commits clearly to a direction, (2) Team takes an early action that produces a visible result, (3) Leader celebrates and publicizes the win, (4) Team takes further action now motivated by success, (5) External observers recognize the momentum, which creates additional energy. The cycle reinforces itself. The leader's job is to start it and to protect it from disruption.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose the current state.** Is the team building, sustaining, or losing momentum? What specific evidence points in each direction? Slow decisions, declining energy, and growing complaint volume are warning signs. Fast execution, high volunteer engagement, and early deliveries are positive indicators.
2. **Design early wins deliberately.** If you are starting something new or trying to reverse a decline, identify three things you can accomplish in the first 60 days that are (a) real and (b) visible to the team. Sequence your effort to produce these first.
3. **Celebrate wins explicitly and publicly.** A win that goes unacknowledged does not contribute to momentum. When something works, name it, attribute it to the people who made it happen, and connect it to where you're going.
4. **Address momentum killers quickly.** Prolonged ambiguity, unaddressed failure, and public confusion about direction are disproportionately damaging when a team is trying to build momentum. None of these require resolution to be addressed — but they require acknowledgment and a plan.
5. **Protect high-momentum periods from disruption.** Major reorganizations, leadership changes, and priority shifts are expensive to momentum. Avoid them when momentum is high unless the alternative is worse.
6. **Move first.** If you are waiting for the team to feel energized before you act, you have inverted the causality. Energy follows action; it does not precede it.

## Examples

**Example 1: The Turnaround**
A nonprofit executive inherits an organization where morale is low and the board is concerned. Instead of launching a comprehensive strategic planning process (which would produce no wins for 6 months), she identifies five things that are already working and publicly invests in them, producing three visible improvements by the end of her second month. She celebrates each one. By month three, staff are proposing their own improvements. She didn't fix the strategic problems yet — she built the momentum needed to fix them.

**Example 2: The Death March Project**
A six-month software project hits month four with nothing shipping and the team exhausted. The project manager restructures deliverables to produce something working and demoable in three weeks, even if it's smaller than originally planned. The demo generates positive stakeholder feedback. The team visibly energizes. The final two months are more productive than the first four. The intermediate win wasn't on the original plan — it was a momentum decision.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Waiting for perfect conditions to start.** Momentum cannot begin in the abstract. It begins with a specific action that produces a specific result. Leaders who wait for certainty before moving never create the conditions under which certainty becomes more likely.
- **Ignoring early wins.** The wins must be celebrated to generate momentum. A team that accomplishes something and receives no acknowledgment does not feel the psychological benefit of the win. The energy dissipates.
- **Confusing busyness with momentum.** A team doing a lot of activity that produces no visible results is not building momentum — it is burning out. Momentum requires results, not just effort.
