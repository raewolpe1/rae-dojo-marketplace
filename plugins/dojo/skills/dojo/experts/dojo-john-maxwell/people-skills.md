## When to Use

Use this when someone is technically strong but struggling to build relationships, when a leader is told they need to "work on their soft skills" without a clear framework for what that means, or when an organization has friction between teams that is fundamentally interpersonal rather than structural.

## Fails When

People skills are not a substitute for competence or results. A leader who is warm and attentive but produces nothing will not retain trust. Maxwell's people skills framework assumes baseline technical competence — it is about adding relational capacity on top of professional capability, not replacing one with the other.

## Core Concept

Maxwell argues in *25 Ways to Win with People* and throughout his body of work that the ability to connect with people is not a personality gift — it is a learnable skill set. And it is arguably the most leveraged skill a leader can develop, because every leadership outcome is achieved through people. The leader who can make people feel valued, understood, and capable will always outperform the leader who can't, even if the second leader is technically superior.

Several specific behaviors appear repeatedly in Maxwell's teaching on this topic:

**Listening before speaking.** Most people listen to respond, not to understand. The leader who pauses, genuinely takes in what the other person said, and responds to what was actually communicated — rather than what was expected — creates an unusual and memorable experience for the other person. Maxwell says that people don't care how much you know until they know how much you care, and nothing communicates care faster than attentive listening.

**Making people feel valued.** Maxwell is direct: people follow leaders who make them feel significant. This is not about flattery — it is about genuine acknowledgment of what a person brings. The leader's job is to catch people doing things right and name it specifically: not "great job" but "the way you handled that client complaint by first acknowledging their frustration before problem-solving — that was exactly right, and it's not easy."

**Remembering what matters to people.** A leader who remembers that an employee's child had a recital, that a colleague is worried about a parent's health, that a team member just ran their first marathon — and asks about it — signals that the person is more than a function to them. This is a discipline, not a talent. It requires attention and follow-through.

**Avoiding the relational drains.** Criticism in public, taking credit that belongs to others, failing to say thank you, and dismissing concerns without genuinely engaging them all draw down the relational account. Maxwell uses the metaphor of a bank account — every positive interaction is a deposit, every negative one a withdrawal. Relationships can survive withdrawals, but not when they run a persistent deficit.

**Meeting people where they are.** Maxwell draws from John Dewey and Dale Carnegie in arguing that the deepest human need is to feel important. The leader who tries to impose their communication style, emotional register, and priorities on others creates resistance. The leader who asks "what matters to this person right now?" and adjusts accordingly creates connection.

## How to Apply

1. **Conduct a listening audit.** In your next three one-on-one conversations, track: how much time did the other person talk versus you? Was your response to what they actually said, or to what you expected them to say?
2. **Develop a recognition practice.** Identify one specific, behavior-based acknowledgment you can give to each person on your team this week. Not general praise — specific observation of a specific action.
3. **Build a relationship inventory.** For your five most important relationships at work, write down what you know about what matters to each person outside of work. If you can't fill it out, that is information.
4. **Identify your relational drains.** What habits do you have that consistently make withdrawals from relational accounts? (Late to meetings, distracted during conversations, slow to respond, dismissive of concerns?) Pick one to address.
5. **Ask before you tell.** When someone brings you a problem, ask one clarifying question before offering a solution. Nine times out of ten, they have already thought of the obvious solution. What they need is to be heard and helped to think clearly.
6. **Follow up on what people tell you.** If someone mentions something important in passing, write it down and ask about it next time you see them. This single habit distinguishes leaders who genuinely care from leaders who perform caring.

## Examples

**Example 1: The Technical Lead Who Couldn't Lead**
A software architect is promoted to engineering director. Her technical judgment is excellent; her team's morale is low. Exit interviews consistently mention "feeling like a resource, not a person." She begins two practices: she spends the first five minutes of every one-on-one asking about something non-technical the person mentioned last time, and she starts giving specific, behavior-based feedback rather than project-level ratings. Six months later, retention improves and team engagement scores rise. Nothing in her technical approach changed.

**Example 2: The Cross-Team Relationship**
A product manager who needs cooperation from an engineering team he doesn't manage is getting minimal engagement. Instead of escalating, he spends two weeks investing in the relationships: he attends their team standup once as an observer, asks each lead what would make handoffs from product less painful, and follows through on two of the answers they gave. The next time he brings a request, the dynamic is different. He brought something before he asked for something.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Performing interest without genuine attention.** People can distinguish between a leader who is asking about their family because they care and one who is asking because they read that they should. The technique without the authentic interest produces something worse than indifference — it produces the feeling of being managed.
- **Consistency failures.** Treating someone warmly one week and coldly the next is more damaging than consistent distance. It makes the relationship feel conditional and the person's status uncertain.
- **Confusing connection with agreement.** Some leaders think strong relationships require saying yes. Maxwell's people skills framework is not about agreeableness — it is about genuine regard. You can deliver hard feedback, decline requests, and hold firm positions while still making people feel respected and valued.
