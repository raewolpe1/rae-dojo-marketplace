## When to Use

Use this when someone is trying to clarify their purpose as a leader, when a leader is asking how to become more influential or respected, or when a team culture has drifted toward entitlement or internal competition. Also relevant when someone needs to move from positional authority to genuine authority.

## Fails When

Adding value is a leadership posture, not a tactic. It cannot be selectively deployed to extract influence and then withdrawn. A leader who adds value when they need something and stops when they have it will be recognized quickly, and the relational damage from that recognition is severe. This framework applies to leaders who are genuinely committed to serving others — not to those looking for a short-term influence technique.

## Core Concept

Maxwell teaches that the purpose of leadership is not to acquire authority, status, or resources — it is to add value to people and to the organizations they are part of. This appears repeatedly across his work: in *The 21 Irrefutable Laws of Leadership*, in *The 5 Levels of Leadership*, and most explicitly in *Intentional Living*. The leader's fundamental question, on his account, is: what am I contributing?

Several distinctions clarify what adding value actually means:

**Adding value vs. feeling valuable.** Many leaders work hard to appear valuable — they project competence, they hold information, they create dependency. Maxwell argues this is the inverse of leadership. Real leadership increases other people's capacity, not the leader's indispensability.

**Adding value is intentional.** It doesn't happen by accident. It requires the leader to actively ask: what does this person need? What would make a meaningful difference to them right now? What information, connection, or encouragement could I provide that would move them forward? This is a discipline, not a reflex.

**Adding value to people vs. adding value through people.** Many leaders add value to the organization through the work of their team — they extract capacity. Maxwell's distinction is between extracting capacity and genuinely increasing it. The leader who adds value to the people on the team (their skills, their confidence, their network, their opportunities) builds something that compounds. The leader who extracts capacity without investing in it depletes it.

**The multiplier question.** Maxwell asks leaders to consider: are the people around me better because of their association with me? Do they have more capacity, more confidence, more opportunity than they would have had otherwise? This is a demanding standard, but it is the one he holds himself to and challenges other leaders to apply.

Adding value is the foundation of the leadership influence that Maxwell describes in the Law of Influence and in Level 2 and Level 4 of the 5 Levels model. People give discretionary effort to leaders who have made genuine investments in them. The return on adding value is enormous — but it is not guaranteed by a single deposit. It is accumulated over time, and it can be destroyed by a sustained pattern of extraction.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the top three people you want to add value to this quarter.** Be specific — name them. What do they need that you could provide? Write it down.
2. **Ask before you tell.** In interactions with your team, start by asking what would be most helpful to them rather than providing what you think they need. You will be right less often than you expect, and asking before providing trains you to listen.
3. **Connect people to resources.** One of the highest-leverage ways to add value is to connect people to information, relationships, or opportunities you have access to and they don't. This requires knowing what each person is working toward and actively watching for things that match.
4. **Give credit generously.** When a team member's work makes a difference, make sure the right people know whose work it was. Credit is a resource you can give without losing any.
5. **Invest in their growth.** What skill, book, conversation, or experience would significantly advance someone on your team? Providing that is adding value in its highest form.
6. **Ask yourself weekly:** did I leave the people around me better off than I found them this week? Specifically and concretely — not in the abstract.

## Examples

**Example 1: The Investment That Changed a Career**
A senior leader notices that a high-potential analyst on her team has strong data skills but poor executive presence. Instead of waiting for a performance review, she invites the analyst to present a piece of work at the quarterly leadership meeting — and spends an hour beforehand coaching her on delivery. The presentation goes well. The analyst's confidence and visibility change. A year later she is promoted. The leader added value by putting the analyst in a position she wasn't yet in and preparing her to succeed there.

**Example 2: The Connected Mentor**
A consultant who is building a practice in a new sector realizes he doesn't have the right relationships. A senior colleague he respects invites him to lunch, learns what he is working toward, and introduces him to three people who would be valuable connections — not because the colleague needs anything from the consultant, but because he is in the habit of connecting people who should know each other. The consultant never forgets it. Every favor he can do for that colleague over the next decade, he does.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Transactional generosity.** Giving with an obvious expectation of immediate return is not adding value — it is negotiating. People can tell the difference, and it poisons the well.
- **Adding value on your terms, not theirs.** The mentor who assumes they know what the mentee needs and provides that regardless of what the mentee asks for is not adding value — they are satisfying their own sense of contribution. Genuine value addition starts with what the other person actually needs.
- **Stopping when things get hard.** Adding value during good times is easy. The moments that define a leader's character are when the team is struggling, morale is low, and the leader chooses to give something rather than demand something.
