## When to Use

Use this when someone is questioning the meaning or significance of their work, when a leader is at a transition point (midcareer, post-achievement, after a significant setback), or when someone has accomplished much of what they set out to do and finds themselves asking "what now?" Also relevant when a leader has been highly successful by external measures but privately feels hollow.

## Fails When

Intentional living requires action, not just reflection. This framework will not help someone who uses the significance question as a retreat from the difficulty of execution. The "move from success to significance" framing can be misapplied as a rationalization for stepping back from hard work into feel-good activities that have no actual impact. Maxwell is clear: significance is harder than success, not easier.

## Core Concept

Maxwell's *Intentional Living* (2015) distinguishes between two orientations that can drive a person's choices:

**Success** — achieving personal goals, acquiring resources and recognition, ascending in your field. Success is self-referential: the measure of success is whether you got what you set out to get.

**Significance** — adding value to others in ways that make a lasting difference. Significance is other-referential: the measure is whether the people and organizations you touched are better because you were there.

Maxwell's argument is not that success is wrong. He pursued it aggressively and achieved it. His argument is that success alone does not produce the deepest satisfaction, and that the most sustainable source of motivation is the pursuit of significance. "The secret to living a significant life," he writes, "is giving your life away." This is not sentimentality — it is a practical claim about what sustains effort over a lifetime.

Several principles govern intentional living on Maxwell's account:

**Intentionality means deciding in advance.** Most people live reactively — they respond to what comes at them and call the result their life. Intentional living means deciding what matters most, designing choices around those commitments, and protecting them from the urgent but unimportant demands that will otherwise crowd them out. This is not a weekend exercise — it is a daily discipline.

**The gap between knowing and doing.** Maxwell is honest that the biggest obstacle to intentional living is not lack of insight — most people know what they should be doing. The obstacle is the gap between knowing and doing. Closing that gap requires structure, accountability, and the willingness to say no to good things in order to say yes to the best things.

**Living in the gap between today and potential.** Maxwell introduces what he calls "the gap" — the distance between where you are and where you could be if you lived intentionally. The question is not whether the gap exists, but what you are doing about it each day.

**Legacy is built daily.** The leader who wants to leave a legacy does not build it in a final act. It is built in the accumulation of decisions made every day over a career. Maxwell urges leaders to ask not "what do I want to accomplish?" but "what do I want to be remembered for?" — and then to work backward to the daily choices that answer to that question.

**Growth compounds.** Maxwell's growth philosophy, articulated most fully in *The 15 Invaluable Laws of Growth*, holds that intentional daily growth — even small amounts — accumulates into transformational change over time. A person who grows one percent each day for a year is not one percent better — they are compounded better. The discipline is showing up for growth every day even when no single day feels significant.

## How to Apply

1. **Write your significance statement.** Finish this sentence: "I want to be known as someone who ___." If you can't finish it, that is your first project.
2. **Audit your calendar against your significance statement.** Does how you spend your time reflect what you say matters most? If not, where is the gap?
3. **Identify your biggest daily growth activity.** What is the single thing you could do every day that would compound most powerfully toward the person you want to become and the difference you want to make?
4. **Find your cause.** Maxwell is emphatic that significance is rarely achieved alone and rarely achieved without a cause larger than yourself. What problem in the world deserves your best effort?
5. **Build accountability.** An intention without accountability is a wish. Identify one person who will ask you monthly: are you living intentionally? Are you moving toward significance?
6. **Start where you are.** Maxwell resists the idea that significance requires a platform, resources, or a certain life stage. "You can make a difference right where you are, with what you have, starting today." The question is whether you will.

## Examples

**Example 1: The Post-Success Pivot**
A successful entrepreneur sells his company at 45, has more money than he'll spend, and finds himself deeply aimless. He has achieved every success goal he set. Working through the significance framework, he identifies that his deepest satisfaction over the previous 20 years came from mentoring young founders — something he did as a side activity, never as a priority. He restructures his life around building a mentoring program for first-generation entrepreneurs. The work is harder and less lucrative than running his company. He describes it as the most meaningful work he has ever done.

**Example 2: The Mid-Level Manager Who Started Where She Was**
A project manager in a mid-sized company realizes she has no platform to change the world but she does have a team of twelve people who look to her. She applies the intentional living framework to her immediate context: every week, she does something specific to invest in the growth of each team member. She starts a monthly lunch where she shares a book or article she found valuable. Three years later, five of her twelve direct reports have been promoted. Two credit her with transforming their careers. She changed the world by starting where she was.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Treating significance as a retirement project.** The shift from success to significance is not something that happens after you've finished accumulating success. It is a reorientation that can happen at any life stage. Leaders who defer it indefinitely typically find they have deferred it permanently.
- **Significance as self-congratulation.** Some leaders use the language of significance to describe activities that primarily benefit their own reputation — the charity gala, the keynote, the award. Genuine significance is measured by the effect on others, not by how the leader feels about their contribution.
- **Perfecting the plan instead of starting.** Intentional living cannot begin when your vision is fully articulate and your path is perfectly clear. It begins with the clearest vision you currently have, implemented imperfectly and adjusted along the way.
