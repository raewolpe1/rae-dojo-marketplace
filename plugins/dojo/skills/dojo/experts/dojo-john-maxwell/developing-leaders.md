## When to Use

Use this when a leader wants to grow beyond producing results through personal effort and begin multiplying their impact through others. It is the most important conversation for any leader who has achieved Level 3 competence and is asking what comes next. Also use it when organizations are facing succession gaps, talent retention problems, or scaling challenges.

## Fails When

This framework assumes the leader is already operating at Level 3 — producing results, with credibility and relationships established. Leaders who try to develop others before they have built trust and demonstrated competence will find the investment does not land. You cannot give away what you do not have.

## Core Concept

Maxwell's central conviction about leadership development: the highest call of a leader is not to produce results personally — it is to produce other leaders. "Leaders who develop followers need them. Leaders who develop leaders multiply their influence."

The logic is straightforward. A leader who develops ten good followers increases their organizational impact by a factor of ten. A leader who develops ten leaders who each develop ten followers increases their impact by a factor of one hundred. The multiplier effect of leader-development dwarfs the multiplier effect of direct effort, no matter how talented the individual leader.

But leader development is hard, and most leaders avoid it — not because they disagree with the principle but because they revert to behaviors that feel more productive in the short term.

**Why leaders don't develop leaders**
- It is faster to do the work yourself than to teach someone else to do it.
- Good developers give away their best assignments and their most capable people — it feels like losing.
- Developing someone means letting them fail while you watch, which is uncomfortable.
- Leaders who need followers to feel important will not develop leaders who might surpass them.

**Maxwell's development model**
Maxwell describes the development process as a progression of five phases, moving from doing everything yourself to having developed leaders who develop others:

1. *I do it* — you model the task or skill.
2. *I do it and you watch* — you narrate what you are doing and why.
3. *You do it and I watch* — the learner takes the lead; you observe and debrief.
4. *You do it* — full ownership transferred to the learner.
5. *You do it and train someone else* — the learner has become a developer.

The critical insight is that development cannot skip steps. Most leaders jump from step 1 to step 4 — they delegate without modeling or narrating, and then they are surprised when the result is inadequate. The learner never had the chance to see the thinking, not just the outcome.

**What to look for in potential leaders**
Maxwell identifies several markers: a proven track record of results (even modest ones), a positive attitude in the face of difficulty, a pattern of developing relationships rather than just networks, demonstrated ability to get things done through others (even informally), and — crucially — the desire to grow.

**The most common mistake in development**
Giving people tasks without giving them context, authority, or feedback. True development is not assigning more work. It is assigning work with intent — explaining why this assignment will stretch the person in a specific way, giving them the authority they need to succeed, and debriefing the experience afterward to extract the learning.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify your two or three top development candidates.** These are people who want to grow and have demonstrated the baseline competence and attitude to justify the investment.
2. **Match assignments to development gaps.** Don't just assign what is convenient — assign what will stretch the person in the specific direction they need to grow.
3. **Model before you delegate.** For high-stakes assignments, narrate your thinking explicitly before handing it over. "Here is how I would approach this and why."
4. **Let them fail safely.** Create assignments where failure is recoverable and instructive. Protect them from catastrophic failure but not from difficult ones.
5. **Debrief every significant experience.** Ask: What worked? What didn't? What would you do differently? What did you learn about leading?
6. **Celebrate their wins publicly and give them away freely.** When someone you developed succeeds, say so in front of others. Giving credit away is what earns you a reputation as a developer people want to work with.

## Examples

**Example 1: The Sales Director Who Built a Team**
A high-performing regional sales director is offered a VP role overseeing six regions. She knows she cannot manage all six regions the way she managed one. She spends her first six months identifying the best performer in each region and systematically developing each one: modeling her approach to difficult client relationships, narrating her thinking on pipeline reviews, giving each one a stretch assignment with real stakes, and debriefing weekly. Two years later, two of her six regional leaders are performing above her own personal best. She gets credit for the whole division's performance because she multiplied it rather than capping it.

**Example 2: The Manager Who Couldn't Let Go**
A technically brilliant engineering manager runs every significant code review himself. He justifies it as maintaining standards. But his team's two most talented engineers, frustrated by the constant oversight and lack of growth opportunity, have both quietly updated their LinkedIn profiles. Maxwell would diagnose this as a leader stuck at Level 3 — producing results through personal output rather than through developed people. The unwillingness to let go of the work means no one on the team is growing, and the best ones will leave for an environment that gives them room to develop.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Delegating tasks instead of developing people.** Development requires intent, narration, feedback, and debrief. Simply assigning more work to a subordinate is outsourcing, not developing.
- **Developing only the people who are most like you.** Leaders unconsciously favor developing people whose style matches their own, which perpetuates their limitations into the next generation of the team.
- **Treating development as an annual performance cycle activity.** Development happens in real work, in real time, through real assignments with real feedback. Annual reviews can reinforce it but cannot substitute for it.
