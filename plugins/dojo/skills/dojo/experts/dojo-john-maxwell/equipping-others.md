## When to Use

Use this when a leader consistently finds that tasks come back poorly done or not done, when a team is not growing in capability over time, when a leader is stuck doing work that should be done by others, or when someone wants to move from managing tasks to developing people.

## Fails When

Equipping requires patience and time investment that may feel economically irrational in the short term. It fails in environments that measure only quarterly output, that penalize leaders for mistakes made by developing team members, or where the leader has no genuine belief that the person being equipped is capable of growth. If you don't believe in the person, don't equip them — the skepticism will be communicated and will undermine the process.

## Core Concept

Maxwell distinguishes clearly between two leadership activities that are often conflated: **managing** and **equipping**. Managing produces results today by directing the efforts of others. Equipping produces results tomorrow by increasing the capability of others to generate results independently.

The principle he states plainly: "A leader is great not because of his or her power but because of his or her ability to empower others." Empowerment in Maxwell's framework is not a policy or a speech — it is the systematic process of transferring knowledge, skill, confidence, and responsibility to another person in a way that genuinely increases their capacity.

He articulates a specific model for equipping — the same 5-phase model covered in *Developing the Leader Within You* and elsewhere:

**Phase 1: I do it.** The leader performs the task. The person being equipped observes. This phase establishes what excellent looks like.

**Phase 2: I do it and you watch.** The leader performs the task with the learner present, narrating their thinking, their decisions, and the reasoning behind their choices. This is modeling with commentary — not just showing what, but explaining why.

**Phase 3: You do it and I watch.** The learner performs the task with the leader present. The leader observes but does not intervene unless necessary. After the task, the leader gives specific, behavioral feedback — what worked, what would work better, what to watch for next time.

**Phase 4: You do it.** The learner performs the task independently. The leader checks in at predetermined intervals but does not supervise the execution. This is where genuine empowerment begins.

**Phase 5: You do it and train someone else.** The learner, now competent, begins to transfer their knowledge to someone else. This phase multiplies leadership capacity. It is the hallmark of a Level 4 leader.

Maxwell is explicit about why leaders skip this model: it is slower in the short term. It is faster to do it yourself than to teach someone to do it. But this calculus is only right for the first time. After the first time, every repetition that goes through the equipping model instead of the "just do it myself" model returns permanent dividends. The leader who consistently equips rather than does builds a team whose ceiling rises over time. The leader who consistently does rather than equips builds a personal bottleneck.

Several common equipping failures appear in Maxwell's writing:
- Giving the assignment without the authority to execute it
- Providing the authority without the context to use it well
- Supervising so closely during Phase 4 that the person never actually takes ownership
- Skipping the debrief, which is where the learning is consolidated

## How to Apply

1. **Identify three tasks you regularly do that someone on your team could learn to do.** These are equipping opportunities you are currently bypassing.
2. **Start with Phase 2 for at least one of them this month.** Perform the task with a team member present and narrate your reasoning aloud. You will be surprised how much tacit knowledge you hold that they have never seen.
3. **Build in debrief time.** For every significant task you observe someone else doing, schedule 30 minutes to debrief specifically: what worked, what was harder than expected, what they would do differently.
4. **Assign with context, not just instruction.** When you give a task, share why it matters, how it connects to larger goals, and what a great outcome looks like — not just what to do. This makes the work meaningful and gives the person the judgment to handle variations you didn't anticipate.
5. **Let people make recoverable mistakes.** If you save people from every error, you prevent the learning that only comes from experience. Distinguish between mistakes that are recoverable (let them happen and debrief them) and mistakes that are unrecoverable (prevent them explicitly).
6. **Track growth, not just output.** For each person you are equipping, keep a record of what they can do now that they couldn't do six months ago. If that list is not growing, the equipping process has stalled.

## Examples

**Example 1: The Leader Who Couldn't Leave**
A department head prides himself on being indispensable — every major client interaction requires his involvement, every important document passes through him. He hasn't taken a vacation in three years. He begins applying the equipping model to his two senior managers over six months: Phase 2 in client meetings, then Phase 3 where they lead with him present, then Phase 4 where they manage independently and debrief with him afterward. At the end of six months, he takes two weeks off for the first time in his career. Nothing breaks. He realizes he has been creating dependency rather than capacity, and that the organization was fragile precisely because of his involvement, not despite it.

**Example 2: The Replicating Team**
A training director applies Phase 5 systematically: once a team member masters a module, their next assignment is to teach it. Within two years, the training team can deliver any component without the director's involvement, and three team members have been promoted to lead their own training functions at other divisions. The director contributed to multiple careers, not just multiple training deliveries.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Delegating without equipping.** Handing off a task without the development process is not equipping — it is load-shifting. The person may complete the task, but they haven't grown, and neither the task nor the growth will be reliable in the future.
- **Equipping selectively.** Some leaders invest heavily in their favorites and ignore their middle performers. Maxwell argues this is both a moral failure (everyone deserves development) and a strategic one (the biggest leverage often comes from lifting mid-tier performers, because there are more of them).
- **Taking back tasks after the handoff.** When a leader reclaims a task from someone they were equipping because the person made a mistake or did it differently than the leader would have, the message is that the equipping was conditional on perfection. This destroys the developing leader's willingness to try.
