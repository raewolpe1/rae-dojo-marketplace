## When to Use

Apply Progressive Summarization when your note-taking produces too many notes to meaningfully revisit, when you struggle to extract the useful content from long saved articles or passages, or when you want notes to become more valuable over time rather than gathering dust. It is the compression technique that makes captured material retrievable and usable in future projects.

## Fails When

Progressive Summarization fails when applied to every note immediately on capture (premature over-summarization exhausts attention and defeats the point of progressive refinement). It also fails when readers summarize their own summaries without having used the note in a project — you end up with a distilled version of something you do not yet know how to use. The signal for when to go deeper should come from use, not from the desire to feel organised.

## Core Concept

Progressive Summarization is a technique for compressing notes incrementally across multiple passes, with each pass revealing what is most essential. The goal is to make notes "retrieval-ready" — skimmable in seconds and comprehensible without re-reading the full original.

Forte describes four layers (plus the original):

**Layer 0 — The Source**: the original article, book passage, transcript, or podcast. Saved without modification. This is the starting point.

**Layer 1 — Saved Notes**: the note as initially captured. A highlighted passage, a key excerpt, a summary sentence. This is the first act of compression — choosing to save this rather than everything.

**Layer 2 — Bold Passages**: the first active summarization pass. You bold the most important sentences or phrases within your saved notes — the 15–20% that captures the core idea. Bolding is fast and reversible. You are not rewriting; you are pointing.

**Layer 3 — Highlighted Passages**: a second pass, usually done months later when the note resurfaces during a project. You highlight (in yellow or another color) the 10–15% of the bold passages that are most essential. These are the things you would say if someone asked you to summarize the note in two sentences.

**Layer 4 — Executive Summary**: an optional final layer added when a note has been used repeatedly and its essence is clear. A few sentences at the top of the note in your own words, distilling the entire thing for future-you to read in fifteen seconds.

The power of Progressive Summarization is that **refinement happens over time and through use**. You do not decide what is most important when you first encounter something — you cannot, because you do not yet know what projects or questions will make it relevant. The signal for going deeper is returning to a note: if you come back to something three times, it earns a highlight. The system responds to repeated use rather than upfront judgment.

Progressive Summarization also works because it preserves the original. Unlike rewriting (which destroys the source texture), bolding and highlighting are non-destructive overlays. You can always drop back to the full text. The layers are guides, not replacements.

A critical distinction: Progressive Summarization is for **reference material** — notes you might use in future projects. It is not for atomic permanent notes (Zettelkasten-style), to-do lists, or meeting minutes. It lives in the Resource and Project layers of PARA, not in your task manager.

## How to Apply

1. **Start with a capture habit**: before Progressive Summarization can work, you need a reliable way to save interesting material — a read-later app, a web clipper, a voice recorder. Do not attempt to summarize at capture; just save.
2. **Add Layer 1 on first review**: when you read a saved article, highlight the key passages and save the excerpt to your notes app. Do not bold yet.
3. **Bold during the next review**: the next time this note appears (in a project search, a weekly review), bold the phrases that still seem important. Spend no more than 60 seconds.
4. **Highlight when it resurfaces again**: if the note comes up during active project work, take 30 seconds to highlight the single most essential idea. This usually happens naturally — you returned because you needed it.
5. **Add an executive summary sparingly**: only for notes you have used in multiple projects and whose value is proven. Write 2–3 sentences at the top of the note capturing the core insight in your own words.
6. **Trust the process over perfection**: most notes will never go past Layer 1 or 2, and that is correct. The depth of summarization should reflect how useful the note has been, not how interesting it seemed when you first captured it.

## Examples

**Example 1: An essay on attention saved for future use**
A writer saves an essay on attention economics. Layer 1: she saves three key paragraphs into Notion. Six months later, while writing a chapter on distraction, she revisits it — Layer 2: she bolds two sentences about how attention is finite while information is infinite. Three months after that, she uses the note again in a talk. Layer 3: she highlights one phrase — "the attention economy is a zero-sum war for a finite resource." Layer 4: she adds an executive summary: "Attention is zero-sum; information is not. This asymmetry is exploited by platforms designed to capture attention, not deliver value." The note is now readable in under ten seconds.

**Example 2: A book chapter on negotiation**
A consultant captures five quotes from a negotiation book. He bolds them during a deal preparation session but does not highlight anything because the quotes feel equally relevant. Two months later, on a different deal, he returns and highlights just one — the one about anchoring first. He does not add an executive summary; the single highlighted sentence is enough. The other bolded passages remain for context. Progressive Summarization surfaced the most actionable idea without requiring him to delete the rest.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Summarizing on capture**: bolding everything when you first save it converts Progressive Summarization into a chore and eliminates the signal value of the highlight. Bold is meaningful precisely because it is reserved for re-encounter.

2. **Over-summarizing inert notes**: some people progressively summarize every note in their database in a dedicated "review session." This misunderstands the technique. The trigger for going deeper should be use, not calendar. Notes that never get returned to do not need highlights — their inactivity is information.

3. **Rewriting instead of highlighting**: some readers rewrite notes in their own words at Layer 2 or 3, which collapses Progressive Summarization into atomic note-making (a different and more demanding practice). Forte's technique is deliberately lightweight. Bolding and highlighting cost almost nothing; they preserve the original and are fast enough to be done in passing.
