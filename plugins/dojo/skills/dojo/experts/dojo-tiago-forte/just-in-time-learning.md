## When to Use

Apply this concept when a user is caught in a learning loop — consuming courses, books, and articles without applying the knowledge — or when they are paralysed by feeling they need to know more before they can start producing. Just-in-time learning also applies when a user is building a knowledge management system and asks whether they should be learning things systematically in advance.

## Fails When

Just-in-time learning fails in domains where foundational knowledge is genuinely prerequisite — surgery, structural engineering, or any field where acting without a minimum knowledge base causes irreversible harm. It also fails when the user confuses "just-in-time" with "not at all" — the model requires that learning actually happen when triggered by a real project, not that it be deferred indefinitely.

## Core Concept

Just-in-time learning (JIT learning) is Forte's answer to just-in-case learning — the habit of consuming vast amounts of information in anticipation of a future need that may never arise. Most knowledge workers, operating in an environment of information abundance, have been trained by education systems built for information scarcity. The instinct to read comprehensively, to "get on top of" a subject before acting, is a residue of an era when information was hard to find and you needed to stockpile it in your head.

Just-in-time learning inverts this model: learn what you need, when you need it, in the context of a real project or goal. The shift has several important consequences:

**Retention and application improve dramatically**: Information learned in the context of a live problem is retained and applied at a much higher rate than information learned in the abstract. When you are trying to solve a real problem, your brain is primed to recognise patterns, make connections, and transfer learning immediately. Decontextualised learning — reading about project management without a project — requires a second cognitive step (retrieval into application) that just-in-time learning skips entirely.

**Learning is scoped by need**: Just-in-time learning naturally limits scope. When you have a specific question — "how do I structure a series of interviews for a research project?" — you learn precisely what you need and stop. Just-in-case learning has no natural stopping point; there is always more to know about any subject.

**The Second Brain enables JIT learning**: Forte's Second Brain system is designed to support just-in-time learning. Rather than capturing information comprehensively and systematically by subject, you capture information based on relevance to your current projects and interests. When a project triggers a learning need, you can search your Second Brain first — you may have already captured the answer in a previous context. When you cannot, you learn what you need and capture it in your Second Brain, where it becomes available for future projects.

**Projects are the engine of learning**: In the JIT model, projects drive learning rather than learning enabling projects. This is a philosophical reversal: instead of waiting until you feel ready to start a project, you start the project and let the project tell you what you need to learn. The discomfort of not knowing enough is reframed as useful signal — it tells you exactly what to learn next.

**Information abundance as the context**: Forte frequently observes that we live in the first era of human history where information is not scarce. The relevant question is no longer "where can I find this information?" but "when should I access this information, and how do I organise and apply it when I do?" Just-in-time learning is the appropriate response to information abundance. It treats knowledge as on-demand infrastructure rather than pre-positioned inventory.

**The role of resonance-based capture**: Just-in-time learning does not mean consuming only what a current project demands. Forte's capture model includes capturing material that resonates even without an immediate application. This creates a stockpile of distilled knowledge in your Second Brain that you access just-in-time rather than holding in your head. The stockpile is on your hard drive, not in your memory. This is the synthesis: you consume broadly based on resonance, capture selectively, and access just-in-time when a project creates demand.

## How to Apply

1. **Audit your current learning queue**: identify how much of your learning consumption is genuinely triggered by a current project versus accumulated just-in-case. The ratio is informative. Most knowledge workers find 80% or more is just-in-case.
2. **Start projects before you feel ready**: the moment you feel you need to learn more before starting, treat that feeling as the signal to start rather than wait. Define what "good enough to start" means and begin.
3. **Let the project create learning needs**: as you work on the project, you will encounter specific gaps. These are your just-in-time triggers. Research precisely what the project needs and capture it in your Second Brain.
4. **Search your Second Brain before searching the internet**: many just-in-time learning needs can be met from material you have already captured. Build the habit of searching your notes first.
5. **Capture learning back into your Second Brain in project context**: notes made in the context of a live project include rich context that makes them more useful when retrieved for future projects.
6. **Set a completion standard for learning sprints**: when just-in-time learning is triggered, define what "enough" looks like before you start. Learning sprints without a stopping criterion drift back into just-in-case.

## Examples

**Example 1: The perpetual student**
A freelance consultant has been meaning to improve her proposal writing for two years. She has read three books on persuasion, bought a copywriting course, and has a folder of saved articles on business writing. She has not written a single improved proposal because she does not feel ready. She adopts the JIT model: she starts a real proposal for a real client tomorrow. As she writes, she identifies a specific gap — she does not know how to structure an executive summary. She searches her Second Brain (finding one relevant captured article), reads specifically about executive summaries, captures three key points, and applies them immediately. Her proposal is better. She has learned more, in less time, with more retention than any of the resources she collected just-in-case.

**Example 2: The knowledge hoarder**
A product manager has 3,000 saved articles, 400 bookmarks, and a half-finished note-taking system. He consumes information enthusiastically but rarely produces anything. He reframes his consumption rule: he will not save any new resource unless he is currently working on a project that it serves, or unless it genuinely resonates with him right now (not hypothetically). Within a month, his capture rate drops by 90% and his output doubles. The pressure to consume "in case I need it" dissolves when he trusts that he can find information just-in-time when a real need arises.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Using JIT as justification for never learning anything**: just-in-time learning requires that the learning actually happen when triggered. It is not a licence to remain perpetually ignorant. The commitment is to learn promptly and specifically when a project demands it, not to defer learning indefinitely.

2. **Learning just-in-time without capturing**: if you learn something just-in-time but do not capture it in your Second Brain, you cannot access it just-in-time in the future. The capture step closes the loop. JIT learning without capture is learning that does not compound.

3. **Treating every learning sprint as a full course**: when a project triggers a specific learning need, research that need and stop. Do not allow just-in-time research to expand into a comprehensive treatment of the subject. Comprehensive learning is a just-in-case habit wearing a just-in-time disguise.
