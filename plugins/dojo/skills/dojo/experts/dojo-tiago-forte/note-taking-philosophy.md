## When to Use

Reach for this topic when a user asks foundational questions about note-taking — what to capture, how to structure notes, which app to use, whether their existing notes are useful, or why their notes never seem to help them when they need them. Also apply when a user is choosing between note-taking systems or feels overwhelmed by competing methodologies.

## Fails When

Forte's note-taking philosophy fails when applied to domains that require precise, complete, or formal records — legal documentation, meeting minutes, scientific lab notebooks, medical records. His approach is optimised for personal knowledge work and creative output, not for institutional record-keeping or compliance purposes. It also fails for users who have no output goals — if you are capturing information purely for reference with no production intent, the output-oriented nature of his philosophy will feel ill-fitting.

## Core Concept

Forte's philosophy of note-taking is best summarised by a single governing principle: **notes are not for storage; they are for thinking and output**. This deceptively simple idea has radical consequences for every practical decision in a note-taking practice.

**The conventional model of note-taking**: Most people who take notes approach the practice as information management — capturing what they encounter so they can find it later. This model treats notes as a filing system. The result is notes that are complete, accurate, and rarely consulted. Forte calls these "museum notes" — carefully preserved, seldom visited.

**Forte's model**: Notes are thinking tools. Their primary function is not to store information but to externalise and develop your thinking. A note is not a copy of what you read; it is the beginning of something you will eventually produce. The test of a note is not "does it accurately represent the source?" but "does it serve my future creative work?"

**Write for your future self**: Forte's most repeated note-taking instruction is to write notes as if you are writing a message to a future version of yourself who has forgotten the context entirely. This means including why you captured something, what you were thinking about when you encountered it, and what project or problem it might serve — not just the information itself. A note that says "interesting idea about incentives" is useless six months later. A note that says "when designing the evaluation framework for the Ghana project, this incentive model might explain why previous interventions underperformed" is immediately actionable.

**Notes as building material, not reference**: Forte draws the distinction between notes as reference (you consult them when you need to look something up) and notes as building material (you assemble them into output). The Second Brain model is fundamentally about building material. When you are writing a report, you should be able to search your notes and pull fragments that assemble into the report, rather than reading your notes and then writing separately.

**Progressive elaboration**: Notes become more useful over time as you interact with them, add to them, and distil them. A note you captured two years ago and have marked, highlighted, and added comments to is often more valuable than a note you captured this morning. The Second Brain accumulates value through progressive elaboration.

**The app is not the system**: Forte is deliberately app-agnostic. The principles — capture, organise, distil, express, PARA, progressive summarisation — work in any notes application. He recommends choosing a tool that (1) works across devices, (2) supports plain text or standard formats, (3) is fast to capture into, and (4) has good search. Everything else is secondary. Changing apps is a common form of productive procrastination; the system matters more than the tool.

**Good enough notes**: Forte explicitly rejects perfectionism in note-taking. Notes do not need to be well-written, complete, or beautifully formatted. They need to be useful. A rough note that captures the essential insight in your own words is more valuable than a polished transcription of someone else's.

## How to Apply

1. **Add context to every note at the time of capture**: before closing a note, add one sentence explaining why you saved it and what project or problem it might serve. This investment pays enormous dividends when you encounter the note again months later.
2. **Write notes in your own words**: even when capturing from a source, paraphrase rather than transcribe. The act of paraphrasing forces you to understand what you are capturing and encodes it in language that is naturally connected to your existing thinking.
3. **Date your notes**: always include a date. Notes without dates lose one of their most useful attributes — you can no longer understand the sequence in which you encountered and developed ideas.
4. **Connect notes to active projects**: when you capture a note, ask whether it belongs in a current Project folder. Notes that are connected to active work are processed and used; notes that live in a general reference folder are often never revisited.
5. **Review and distil incrementally**: do not plan a comprehensive note review. Instead, whenever you encounter an old note for any reason, spend thirty seconds adding a layer of Progressive Summarisation — highlighting, annotating, or summarising. This distributes the distillation work across time.
6. **Accept low capture quality in favour of high capture volume**: a rough note that captures the idea is infinitely better than no note. Set your quality threshold at "good enough to understand in three months" rather than "polished enough to share."

## Examples

**Example 1: The non-retrieving note-taker**
A consultant has been using Evernote for eight years and has 14,000 notes. She almost never searches them because past experience has shown that the notes she finds are not useful when she finds them. She audits a sample of 50 notes and finds the problem: they are transcriptions and summaries with no personal context, no dates on older notes, and no connection to projects. She rebuilds her practice: every note she now creates includes a sentence about why she is saving it and what it might be used for. After three months, she begins finding her notes useful for the first time.

**Example 2: The app-switcher**
A researcher has used six different note-taking apps in four years — Notional, Bear, Obsidian, Roam, Logseq, and now Notion — each time hoping the new app will solve his note-taking problems. His notes never serve him well regardless of which app he uses because his underlying practice is unchanged: he captures comprehensively, never distils, and never connects notes to his writing projects. He stops switching apps and instead applies Forte's principles to his current tool. Within a month, his notes are more useful than they have ever been. The system was always the problem; the app was never the solution.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Transcription masquerading as note-taking**: copying large amounts of text from a source into a notes app. This produces a searchable archive of other people's words that you will never use in output because it is not your thinking. Capture selectively and in your own words.

2. **Infinite nested folders**: building a complex hierarchical taxonomy for your notes. This makes every capture decision expensive (where does this go?) and produces a system that is navigated rather than searched. Flatten your note structure and rely on search and PARA for organisation.

3. **Notes without output intent**: taking notes about everything you read without any connection to what you are trying to produce. Note-taking without output intent is a form of collector's fallacy — the feeling of productivity without actual productivity. Ask, before every capture: "Is this useful for something I am trying to build?"
