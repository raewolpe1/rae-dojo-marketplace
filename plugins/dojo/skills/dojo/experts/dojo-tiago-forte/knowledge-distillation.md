## When to Use

Reach for this concept when a user has a large archive of notes they never use, when they feel overwhelmed by their own captured material, or when they are preparing to write or present and cannot extract the signal from their stored knowledge. Distillation is the bridge between capturing material and expressing ideas.

## Fails When

Knowledge distillation fails when applied uniformly — summarising everything at the same level of detail regardless of how much you actually use it. Notes that you revisit frequently deserve deeper distillation; notes you have never touched again do not. Distilling indiscriminately wastes time and creates the illusion of productivity without serving actual output.

## Core Concept

Knowledge distillation is the D in Forte's CODE framework (Capture, Organise, Distil, Express). It refers to the process of progressively extracting the most valuable signal from a note each time you revisit it, using a layered technique Forte calls Progressive Summarization.

The core insight is that distillation should be triggered by use, not by a distillation schedule. You do not sit down once a month and distil all your notes. You distil a note when you are about to use it — when you are writing a related piece, building a related presentation, or thinking through a problem where the note seems relevant. This use-triggered distillation means you only invest effort in notes that are actively earning their place.

**Why notes are hard to use raw**: Most notes are captured at the moment of reading or listening, which means they contain not just the useful signal but also the surrounding context, transitions, and scaffolding that existed in the source material. When you return to a raw note weeks later, you must re-process it almost from scratch: re-read it, identify what was important, reconstruct why you saved it. Progressive Summarization compresses this retrieval work into a series of short passes, each of which layers on the previous.

**The four layers of Progressive Summarization**:

*Layer 0 — The raw source*: everything you saved, in its original form. May be long, include irrelevant context, require full re-reading to extract value.

*Layer 1 — The saved note*: the specific excerpt or passage you captured from the source. This is the note itself — already shorter than the source, already filtered by resonance.

*Layer 2 — Bold passages*: on your second encounter with the note (typically when it becomes relevant to a project), bold the sentences or phrases that seem most important. Aim for roughly ten to twenty percent of the note. You are not summarising; you are marking what stands out.

*Layer 3 — Highlighted passages*: on your third encounter (usually when you are actively writing or using the note), highlight the single most important sentence or phrase in the bolded material. One to five percent of the original. This is the distilled essence.

*Layer 4 — The executive summary*: for notes you use repeatedly, write a one-to-three sentence summary in your own words at the top of the note. This is rare — only for notes that become genuine reference anchors in your work.

**Non-destructive layering**: Each layer preserves the previous. You do not delete the original passage when you bold; you do not delete the bolded text when you highlight. The layers are cumulative. This means that if your bold or highlight turns out to be wrong — if future context reveals that a different passage was actually more important — you can simply revise without losing the original material.

**Distillation as a thinking act**: The act of bolding and highlighting is not mechanical extraction. It requires you to ask, at each pass, what is most essential here. This act of selection is itself a form of thinking — it forces you to develop and refine a view about what matters. Regular distillers often report that the process of distilling a note teaches them more than the original reading did.

## How to Apply

1. **Never distil at the point of capture**: save the raw note, tag it minimally, and move on. Distillation happens on the second encounter, not the first.
2. **Use the note's second encounter as the first distillation trigger**: the next time you open a note because it seems relevant to something you are working on, spend two minutes bolding the key passages before you start using it.
3. **Aim for ten to twenty percent bolding**: if you bold more than a quarter of the note, you are bolding indiscriminately. If you bold less than five percent of a long note, you may be being too cautious. The goal is that someone who read only the bolded text would capture the core idea.
4. **Highlight only on active use**: reserve the highlighting layer for moments when you are writing, presenting, or making a decision right now. Ask: "If I could keep only one sentence from this note, which one would it be?"
5. **Write executive summaries only for notes you use more than three times**: these are your working reference notes. Everything else can stay at Layer 2 or 3.
6. **Review your distillation when output is weak**: if your writing feels thin or derivative, open the underlying notes and check whether they have been distilled. Undistilled notes produce undistilled expression.

## Examples

**Example 1: The report writer**
A management consultant has four hundred saved notes on organisational change. She is about to write a report on change management for a client. She opens the ten notes that seem most relevant, bolds the key passages in each (taking about thirty seconds per note), and highlights one sentence in each. She now has ten distilled anchor points. Her report almost writes itself: she is not re-reading four hundred notes; she is composing from ten distilled sentences. The distillation she did weeks ago on each note — when she first revisited them — compressed her preparation time from three hours to forty minutes.

**Example 2: The podcast listener**
A product designer listens to industry podcasts and captures two or three quotes per episode. Over a year he accumulates three hundred podcast notes, but they sit unread. He adopts a rule: whenever he opens a note, he spends thirty seconds bolding. Over three months, his most-revisited notes develop visible layers — bolded passages from the second encounter, a highlighted sentence from the third, and occasional short summaries at the top. His weekly review now takes fifteen minutes instead of forty, because he is reading layers rather than full notes.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Summarising on capture**: writing a one-paragraph summary of an article the moment you finish reading it. This conflates capture and distillation, and it produces summaries based on immediate impressions rather than future relevance. The note you write in the moment of reading will not be the note you need six months later when the article becomes relevant to a specific project.

2. **Over-highlighting**: bolding or highlighting sixty to eighty percent of a note. This defeats the purpose — you are not identifying signal, you are just re-reading with a marker. If almost everything is important, nothing is. Extreme selectivity is the entire point of distillation.

3. **Distilling as a task instead of as a trigger**: scheduling "distillation sessions" where you process all new notes. This imposes the wrong rhythm. Notes should be distilled when they become relevant to work, not on a calendar schedule. Distillation-as-task produces busy work; distillation-as-trigger produces useful work.
