## When to Use

Reach for this concept when the user's capture is inconsistent or anxiety-producing — capturing too much from some sources, nothing from others, and unable to remember what they captured or why. Also useful when someone asks how to decide what is worth saving, or how to build the front end of a PKM system without it becoming a burden.

## Fails When

Capture habits fail when treated as a completeness exercise. The goal is not to capture everything; it is to capture what resonates. Systems that try to capture everything become noise-filled databases that feel punishing to open. The resonance filter — saving only what genuinely moves you — is the key discipline.

## Core Concept

Capture is the first step in Forte's CODE framework (Capture, Organise, Distil, Express), and it is both the most visible and the most misunderstood. The common failure mode is to treat capture as data collection: saving articles, highlights, bookmarks, and quotes because they might be useful someday. Forte calls this just-in-case capture, and it produces bloated, demoralising systems.

The alternative is resonance-based capture. Forte argues that you should save things that resonate — ideas that produce a reaction, that you want to remember, that feel generative. This is a subjective and intuitive filter, not a systematic one. The question is not "Could I ever use this?" but "Does this move me now?"

**Why resonance works as a filter**: Resonance is a reliable proxy for relevance. If something catches your attention strongly enough to produce a physical or emotional reaction — a sharp recognition, excitement, discomfort, or a sense that this changes how you see something — it is more likely to be useful than material you collected out of duty or possibility. Resonance-filtered notes also have more energy: when you open them later, the feeling of resonance tends to return, making them easier to work with.

**Where to capture from**: Forte identifies several input types that knowledge workers draw from:
- *Inspired passages* from books, articles, and podcasts — specific sentences or paragraphs, not whole documents
- *Meeting notes* and conversation insights — what was said, what was decided, what surprised you
- *Fleeting thoughts* — ideas that arise spontaneously in the shower, on a walk, or mid-conversation
- *Reference material* — facts, data, definitions, or frameworks you will need again
- *Creative sparks* — images, examples, phrasings, or metaphors that might feed future work

**The capture tool should be frictionless**: Forte recommends having one universal inbox — a single place where everything lands before being organised. This could be a note-taking app with a quick-capture hotkey, a voice note app, or a physical notebook. What matters is that the friction of capture is as low as possible. If capturing an idea requires more than two taps or a few seconds of setup, the habit will fail.

**Progressive capture, not complete capture**: You do not need to capture everything from a source. You can read an entire book and save only three sentences. You can attend a three-hour workshop and capture two ideas. The selection process itself — identifying what is most worth saving — is a form of distillation that happens at the moment of capture.

**Tagging vs folders at the capture stage**: At the point of capture, Forte recommends keeping organisation minimal. Do not spend time tagging, categorising, or filing during capture. The cognitive overhead of organisation at the capture stage introduces friction that kills the habit. Organise later, in a separate session.

## How to Apply

1. **Designate a single capture inbox**: choose one app — Readwise, Notion, Apple Notes, Obsidian — and route all captures there. Resist the urge to have different notebooks for different topics.
2. **Install the resonance filter consciously**: before saving, pause for one second and ask whether this genuinely resonates or whether you are saving it out of obligation. If the latter, skip it.
3. **Capture the spark, not the whole source**: highlight one paragraph, not the entire article. Record the three sentences from the podcast that surprised you, not the episode description.
4. **Add a sentence of context when possible**: if you have thirty seconds, add one sentence explaining why you saved something. "Useful for explaining X to clients" or "Check whether this contradicts Y." This sentence saves future-you significant time.
5. **Review your inbox weekly**: move items from the inbox to their PARA locations during a weekly review. Never let the inbox become the permanent resting place.
6. **Protect the capture habit from perfectionism**: a messy inbox with real content is better than an empty inbox because you only capture when you have time to file properly. File when you review, not when you capture.

## Examples

**Example 1: The book reader**
A leadership consultant reads heavily but retains almost nothing. She starts highlighting only two or three passages per chapter — the ones that produce a genuine "yes, that's it" reaction — and captures them in Readwise, which sends them to her Obsidian inbox. After three months she has four hundred high-resonance notes, all of which she actually remembers having saved. Her previous system had thousands of highlights she had never revisited. Fewer captures, more utility.

**Example 2: The meeting heavy**
A product manager spends eight hours per day in meetings and has no capture habit for them. He begins keeping a simple notes template: three fields — decisions made, open questions, ideas sparked. At the end of each meeting he spends two minutes filling the template and saves it to his inbox. Within a month his inbox has forty high-value meeting notes. He can now retrieve a decision made six weeks ago in under thirty seconds, which has made him substantially more effective in follow-up conversations.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Indiscriminate bookmarking**: saving every article you intend to read, every link that looks interesting, every tool recommendation — with no resonance filter. The result is a bookmarks folder with four thousand items and zero retrievability. If you are not saving it because it resonates, you are not really capturing; you are deferring.

2. **Filing during capture**: stopping to think about where something should go, what tags to apply, or how it relates to existing notes — at the moment of capture. This cognitive overhead causes capture friction to skyrocket and the habit to collapse. Capture first, organise at your weekly review.

3. **Capturing everything from a source**: highlighting an entire book, downloading a full transcript, exporting all your email to notes. These habits create volume without selectivity. A Second Brain full of unfiltered material is not a Second Brain; it is a data warehouse. The selectivity of capture is what makes the system human-scale and navigable.
