topic: Knowledge Sharing and Publishing
expert: Tiago Forte

## When to Use

Apply this topic when a user has accumulated significant knowledge — in a Second Brain, through professional experience, or through years of learning — but is not sharing it, or is sharing it ineffectively. Also use it when someone asks about building an audience, writing publicly, or turning their expertise into something others can use. Forte treats sharing not as self-promotion but as an ethical obligation and a creative necessity.

## Fails When

Forte's knowledge-sharing framework fails in professional contexts where sharing expertise publicly creates genuine competitive, legal, or confidentiality risks — where sharing proprietary methods, client data, or sensitive research would be inappropriate. It also fails for users who are genuinely in early learning phases and have not yet developed the perspective that would make their sharing valuable. The injunction to "teach what you know" requires actually knowing something.

## Core Concept

Forte's position on knowledge sharing is more philosophically grounded than most productivity writers. He argues not just that sharing is useful but that it is an obligation — that hoarding knowledge is a form of wastefulness that the information abundance of our era has made indefensible.

**The creative necessity of sharing**: Forte's most important claim about sharing is that it is not a downstream activity — something you do after your knowledge is fully developed. Sharing is part of the process of developing knowledge. When you write publicly about something you are learning or thinking about, you are forced to make your implicit understanding explicit, to test your reasoning against the response of others, and to encounter challenges and extensions you would never have generated alone. Sharing accelerates learning in ways that private study cannot.

**Teaching as the most powerful form of distillation**: Forte draws on the learning science concept of the protégé effect — explaining something to someone else forces a deeper encoding than reading alone. When you write a post, record a video, or give a talk, you are performing the most intensive form of distillation available. You cannot explain what you do not understand. Attempting to explain reveals the gaps.

**The obligation to share**: Forte is unusually direct about the ethical dimension. He argues that if you have developed expertise that could help others, keeping it private is a choice with moral weight. The information abundance of the internet means that the marginal cost of sharing knowledge is near zero; the potential benefit is unbounded. The asymmetry is stark. He acknowledges that most people's hesitation is not about ethics but about fear — of being wrong, of being criticised, of not being good enough. But he does not let that hesitation off the hook.

**"Narrate your work"**: One of Forte's most practical injunctions is to narrate your work as you do it — to share the process, not just the polished result. Most people wait until they have something finished and perfect before sharing. Forte argues this is exactly backwards: the most valuable thing you can share is often the messy, in-progress thinking that most experts hide. "I am working on X and here is what I am discovering" is more valuable than a polished summary because it is rarer, more honest, and more useful to people who are earlier in the same journey.

**Building in public**: Forte popularised (though did not originate) the concept of building in public — sharing the development of a project, a business, or a body of knowledge as it happens. This approach builds an audience before the product is finished, generates feedback that improves the product, and creates a record of the process that has standalone value.

**Intermediate Packets as shareable units**: Forte's concept of Intermediate Packets is partly a knowledge-sharing concept. Each IP is a potentially shareable unit. A distilled note can become a tweet. An outtake can become a blog post. A work-in-progress section can become a newsletter. By designing your work as a sequence of packets rather than a single monolithic deliverable, you create sharing opportunities at every stage.

**The creator's obligation**: Forte's most expansive version of this argument is that the Second Brain methodology is ultimately in service of a creative vocation — that building a Second Brain without producing and sharing output is incomplete. The system exists to support expression. Expression that does not reach others is private journaling; expression that reaches others is knowledge contribution.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify what you know that others do not**: make a list of ten things you know that most people in your field or community do not know. These are your natural sharing topics. Do not wait for expertise you have not yet acquired; start with what you already know.
2. **Adopt the narrate-as-you-go practice**: begin sharing your current learning journey, not just your finished expertise. A weekly note about what you are working on and what you are discovering is valuable and low-stakes.
3. **Turn Intermediate Packets into published units**: whenever you distil a note, write an outline, or draft a section, ask whether this packet is shareable in its current form. Many Intermediate Packets require minimal editing to become publishable posts, threads, or newsletter entries.
4. **Choose a minimum viable sharing format**: commit to the simplest sharing format you will actually use. This might be a weekly email, a Twitter/X thread, a short LinkedIn post, or a brief blog post. The format matters less than the habit of sharing regularly.
5. **Share before you feel ready**: identify the moment you think "I should share this once I know more / once it's better / once I have thought it through more." That moment is your signal to share now. The gap between what you know and what you feel comfortable sharing is where your most valuable work lives.
6. **Treat public response as learning data**: every piece of public sharing generates signal — what resonates, what confuses, what generates useful pushback. Treat this signal as input into your Second Brain. Sharing is not just output; it is research.

## Examples

**Example 1: The reluctant expert**
A monitoring and evaluation practitioner has fifteen years of experience designing impact evaluations in Sub-Saharan Africa. She has extensive notes, frameworks, and hard-won lessons. She has never shared them publicly because she does not think she is "enough of an expert" compared to academics. She begins a monthly newsletter narrating one case study or lesson from her practice — anonymised and generalised. Within six months, she has more professional connections and more inbound work enquiries than in the previous three years. The expertise she undervalued was genuinely rare.

**Example 2: The perpetual drafter**
A researcher has seventeen unpublished essays in his Second Brain. Each one is "almost ready." He has been adding, refining, and revising them for years. He adopts a rule: every essay that he has touched in the last 90 days will be published in its current form within 30 days — even if he considers it incomplete. He publishes six essays in a month. Three generate significant engagement and one leads to an unexpected collaboration. The other three generate no response. All six have been available for others to use or build on. The unpublished essays had generated nothing.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Waiting for expertise**: postponing sharing until you have enough knowledge, experience, or credentials. This bar is infinitely deferrable. The "curse of knowledge" means the people who most need your sharing are precisely those who are earlier in the journey than you, for whom your current level of understanding is already expert. Share from where you are.

2. **Sharing only finished products**: sharing only polished, complete work eliminates the most valuable part of your potential contribution — the process, the failures, the unexpected discoveries, and the in-progress thinking. Finished products compete with all other finished products; work-in-progress thinking is genuinely rare.

3. **Sharing without distilling**: broadcasting everything you encounter (retweets, reposts, link dumps) rather than sharing your own synthesis and perspective. Curation without commentary is not knowledge sharing; it is redistribution. Your value as a sharer comes from your perspective and synthesis, not from being a relay station.
