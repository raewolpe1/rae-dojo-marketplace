## When to Use

Reach for this concept when a user is struggling with large, monolithic creative projects — starting a report, presentation, or article from scratch each time — or when they feel their knowledge system is accumulating material without feeding their output. Intermediate Packets make the connection between a Second Brain and productive creative work explicit and actionable.

## Fails When

Intermediate Packets fail when they are too small (individual sentences are not re-usable packets) or too large (a complete draft is not an intermediate packet — it is a finished product). The concept also fails when packets are created but not organised where they can be found. A packet buried in a folder without context is nearly impossible to retrieve.

## Core Concept

An Intermediate Packet (IP) is a discrete, reusable chunk of work — smaller than a finished deliverable, larger than a raw note — that can be combined with other packets to produce output. The term comes from the idea that creative work need not flow from scratch to finished product in a single linear session. Instead, it can be assembled from pre-built components over time.

Forte identifies five types of Intermediate Packets:

**1. Distilled notes**: notes that have been put through at least one layer of Progressive Summarization. These are the most granular packets — individual insights, well-formed ideas, or reference facts that are ready to be dropped into an argument.

**2. Outtakes**: material you created for one project that did not make the final cut. A section you drafted and then deleted, a framework you developed but found too complex, a slide deck element that did not fit the presentation. These outtakes are not failures; they are packets waiting for a project where they fit.

**3. Work-in-progress**: documents, outlines, or drafts that represent thinking-in-progress but are not finished. A half-written essay, a half-built model, an outline with some sections detailed and others just headings. These can be picked up, continued, or cannibalised for parts.

**4. Final deliverables**: completed work products — published articles, delivered presentations, completed client reports. These are the densest packets: they represent the full investment of research, drafting, and refinement, and they often contain ideas and arguments that can be adapted for new audiences or purposes.

**5. Documents created by others**: reports, articles, frameworks, and models created by colleagues, clients, or researchers that you have processed and stored. These become packets when you have distilled them enough to know what is in them and how to use them.

**The economic case for Intermediate Packets**: Every time you start a project from zero, you are ignoring the value of your previous work. Forte argues that knowledge workers systematically under-exploit their intellectual capital. They produce a good analysis for one client and then re-do equivalent analysis for the next, because the first analysis was not stored in a form that made it retrievable and re-usable. Intermediate Packets are the storage format that makes past work compound into future output.

**Packets and serendipitous combination**: One of the most valuable properties of a Second Brain full of well-formed packets is that unexpected combinations become possible. You open a project folder and find three packets from different sources — a distilled note from a book, an outtake from a previous presentation, and a framework you built for a client two years ago — and you realise they form the spine of the new thing you are trying to build. This serendipitous combination is almost impossible when working from scratch but becomes frequent in a mature system.

**Packets and divergence**: Intermediate Packets also support the divergence phase of creative work — the phase where you gather, combine, and explore before converging on a final form. Having a library of packets means divergence can draw on real material rather than requiring you to research from zero each time you start a new project.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the packets you already have**: review your note archive with packet-eyes. What distilled notes are dense enough to be used directly in an argument? What outtakes from past projects might fit a different project? You probably have more packets than you think.
2. **Tag or organise packets by type**: in your note-taking system, mark the difference between raw captured material and packet-ready material. This could be a tag (e.g., `#packet`), a dedicated folder, or a naming convention.
3. **Create packets intentionally from finished work**: when you complete a project, spend fifteen minutes identifying what in it could be re-used. Extract the key sections, frameworks, or arguments into standalone notes. Store them in Resources, not in the project folder that will be archived.
4. **Save your outtakes**: when you cut material from a document, do not delete it. Move it to an Outtakes section in the project note or to a dedicated Resources folder. Many writers report finding that their best work came from outtakes of previous projects.
5. **Build with packets, not from scratch**: when starting a new project, before you open a blank document, search your Second Brain for relevant packets. Assemble a rough collection of what you already have before you decide what you still need to create.
6. **Measure packet utilisation**: periodically check how many packets from your Second Brain you have used in recent projects. If the answer is close to zero, either your packets are not well-formed, not well-organised, or your project start-up habit is bypassing the system.

## Examples

**Example 1: The consultant with the frameworks library**
A strategy consultant builds a dedicated folder called "Frameworks Library" in his Resources section. Every time he develops a framework for a client — a 2×2 matrix, a five-step model, a diagnostic checklist — he saves a clean version to the library after the project ends. Over three years he accumulates forty frameworks. When a new client engagement begins, his first step is to search the library for anything relevant. He reports that at least one existing framework applies to every new engagement, saving him an average of six hours of framework development per project.

**Example 2: The writer with outtakes**
A newsletter writer cuts approximately thirty percent of her first drafts. She used to delete the cuts. After adopting Intermediate Packets, she saves all cuts to an "Outtakes" note organised by theme. Three months later, when she is struggling to fill an issue on a related theme, she opens her outtakes folder and finds two paragraphs from a previous draft that — with minor editing — become the core of the new piece. What had been waste became raw material.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Treating raw notes as packets**: adding captured passages directly to project documents without any distillation. Raw notes are source material, not packets. They require re-processing before use, which negates the efficiency benefit of having stored them. A packet is pre-processed: someone (future-you) can pick it up and use it without needing to re-read and re-interpret the whole source.

2. **Filing packets in project folders**: storing a useful framework or argument inside the folder for the specific project that produced it. When that project gets archived, the packet disappears. Packets that belong to the future (reusable material) should live in Resources, not in the project where they were created.

3. **Perfecting packets before storing them**: waiting until a packet is "finished" before saving it to the system. Most packets are valuable in their rough form. An outline is a packet. A list of bullet points is a packet. A three-sentence argument is a packet. If you only save polished material, you will save almost nothing, because most intermediate work is inherently rough.
