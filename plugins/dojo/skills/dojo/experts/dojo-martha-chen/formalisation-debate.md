# The Formalisation Debate

## When to Use
- Any formalisation target, registration drive, or titling proposal.
- When de Soto is cited, explicitly or implicitly.
- When formalisation is justified by tax or by finance access.

## Fails When
- **The question is what formalisation experiments have found empirically.** Route to McKenzie — he has run them. Chen contests the frame; McKenzie measures the effect. Use both.

## Core Concept

**The question that is never asked: what does the worker gain?**

Formalisation agendas are almost always justified by benefits to someone other than the person being formalised — tax revenue for the fiscus, data for the planner, a level playing field for the formal competitor, a tidier city for the municipality. When the benefit to the worker is stated at all it is stated as a presumption: access to finance, access to markets, access to protection. Each of these is a claim that must be checked against what the specific registration actually confers, and often it confers none of them.

**The legalist premise.** De Soto's account holds that informal operators are entrepreneurs held back by a hostile legal order; give them title and registration and dead capital becomes live. This is one school of four (see `schools-of-thought.md`). It fits some own-account operators with growth intent. It does not fit the outworker, the casual wage worker, or the contributing family worker, for whom title to nothing is worth nothing and whose constraint is a power relation with a counterparty.

**The heterogeneity of "formal".** Formalisation is not one act. A worker may be registered as an enterprise, registered for tax, covered by labour law, covered by social protection, licensed to trade in a space, or none of these in any combination. **These are separable, and the ones that benefit the worker are usually not the ones the state is pursuing.** Social protection coverage benefits the worker; tax registration benefits the fiscus. A drive that delivers the second and calls it formalisation has delivered the state's objective under the worker's name.

**The reframe.** Support to the informal economy should seek to **extend social protection, including occupational health and safety, to informal workers**, and to **increase the productivity of informal enterprises and informal workers through an enabling environment and support services**. Note that neither of these requires registration as a precondition. **Formalisation is one possible route to worker benefit, not the definition of it** — and usually a slow, expensive and ineffective one.

**The paradigm shift.** What is needed is a change in how the informal economy is understood: not as a deviation to be corrected, but as more than half of non-agricultural employment in most developing regions, contributing to the overall economy and providing pathways to the reduction of poverty and inequality. Policy that begins from correction will always produce formalisation targets. Policy that begins from the reality produces protection and productivity.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask what the worker gains.** Specifically, from this registration, in this jurisdiction. If the answer is nothing, the target is fiscal.
2. **Decompose "formal".** Which registration? Tax, enterprise, labour, social protection, trading licence? They separate, and they benefit different parties.
3. **Pursue the benefit directly.** If the goal is social protection coverage, extend social protection. Do not route it through a registration that may not confer it.
4. **Check the school.** A formalisation drive is legalist. Name the segments it therefore cannot help.
5. **Get the empirical record from McKenzie.** The experiments are his. They show registration is inducible and tax registration is not, and that the promised benefits mostly do not follow.
6. **Do not make protection conditional on formalisation.** That is a policy choice, not a constraint.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy sets a target of formalising 20,000 informal township enterprises, justified by tax base broadening and improved access to finance.

**Application:** Ask the question the target does not answer: what does the trader gain? Tax registration confers a liability, not a benefit. Enterprise registration may confer access to a bank account, which is a real gain if — and only if — a bank product exists that the trader can actually use, which is an empirical matter and usually not the case. Access to markets follows registration only where a specific buyer requires it, which is a procurement question, not a registration question. So the target as constructed delivers a fiscal benefit and a presumed worker benefit that has not been specified. Reframe: name the specific benefit sought, pursue it directly, and treat registration as an instrument only where it is demonstrably the route to that benefit.

**Result:** A chapter about what informal workers gain rather than about what the state collects.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Set a formalisation target without specifying the worker's benefit.
**Why:** It is a fiscal target wearing a development label.

**Don't:** Treat "formal" as one status.
**Why:** Tax, enterprise, labour, social protection and licensing registration are separable, and they benefit different parties.

**Don't:** Make protection conditional on registration.
**Why:** Social protection and labour standards can be designed in ways appropriate for informal workers. The conditionality is a choice.
