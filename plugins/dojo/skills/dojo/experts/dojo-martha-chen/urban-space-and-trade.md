# Urban Space, Street Trade and Tenure

## When to Use
- Street trading, market, or precinct policy.
- Township economy chapters.
- Any spatial intervention affecting where informal work happens.

## Fails When
- **The question is regional spatial economics.** Different scale, different expert.

## Core Concept

**For traders, tenure dominates every other constraint.** A trader whose stall can be removed on Tuesday will not invest on Monday. No amount of business training, working capital, or market linkage compensates for the absence of secure trading space, because every one of those instruments assumes a place to trade from.

This is the single most reliable omission in township and informal economy policy. Chapters discuss support to street trade at length and never use the words *tenure* or *eviction* — which reveals that they were written from the municipality's side of the table, where the trader is a management problem rather than a worker with a workplace.

**The workplace framing.** The street is a workplace. The dump is a workplace. The home is a workplace. Once that is accepted, a series of consequences follow that are otherwise invisible: the workplace has occupational health and safety risks that should be analysed and addressed; it requires infrastructure (water, sanitation, storage, shelter, waste removal) as any workplace does; and its occupants have a claim on it that is a labour claim, not a favour.

**Infrastructure over training.** For own-account operators in public space, the binding constraints are typically: security of the site; storage (so that stock need not be transported daily); shelter; water and sanitation; electricity; waste removal; and freedom from confiscation and harassment. These are municipal capital and enforcement decisions. They are cheap relative to enterprise support programmes and they are almost never in the enterprise chapter, because the enterprise chapter belongs to the economic development department and the street belongs to the municipality.

**The eviction dynamic.** Relocation to a purpose-built market frequently destroys the business, because the value of the site *is* the footfall, and the new site does not have it. A market built without traders' involvement, off the pedestrian route, at a rental the trader cannot carry, is not an upgrade. It is an eviction with a ribbon.

**The technology angle.** WIEGO's research on technology, informal workers and cities covers **Durban** alongside Ahmedabad and Lima — examining what technologies are used by, and impact on, the working poor in the informal economy. This is South African evidence on a South African city and should be used.

**The organising link.** Traders' organisations exist and have positions on all of this. Where they are absent from the planning, the plan will get the site wrong.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask about tenure first.** Before any instrument. If tenure is insecure, tenure is the intervention.
2. **Treat the street as a workplace.** Then apply workplace logic: OHS, infrastructure, services.
3. **Cost the infrastructure against the programme.** Storage and shelter are usually cheaper per trader than training and more effective.
4. **Never relocate without the traders.** The footfall is the asset. They know where it is; the planner does not.
5. **Find the by-law.** There is one, it criminalises them, and it is doing more damage than any market failure.
6. **Use the Durban evidence.** It exists and it is local.

## Examples

**Situation:** A township economy chapter proposes trader training, micro-finance, and the construction of two new market facilities.

**Application:** The chapter has no tenure provision, which means every instrument in it rests on a site the trader may lose. The market facilities are the highest-risk element: purpose-built markets fail routinely when they are sited off the pedestrian route, priced above what traders can carry, or designed without traders' involvement — and a failed market is an eviction, because the old site is invariably cleared as part of the project. Recommendations: lead with tenure and confiscation policy; involve trader organisations in siting with real authority, not consultation; audit the by-laws that criminalise trading; and reallocate a portion of the market capital budget to storage, shelter, water, sanitation and waste at existing trading sites, which is cheaper per trader and does not require anyone to move.

**Result:** A chapter that addresses the constraint that actually binds.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Write a street trade section without tenure.
**Why:** Everything else in the section assumes a place to trade from.

**Don't:** Build a market and call it upgrading.
**Why:** The site's value is its footfall. A market off the route, at an unaffordable rent, designed without traders, is an eviction.

**Don't:** Leave the street out of the OHS section.
**Why:** It is a workplace with real and analysable risks.
