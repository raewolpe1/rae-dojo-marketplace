# The Regulatory Environment — Missing, Not Excessive

## When to Use
- When red-tape reduction is proposed as informal economy policy.
- When designing regulatory reform touching informal work.
- When occupational health and safety is being scoped.

## Fails When
- **The subject is regulation binding on growth firms inside the formal system.** That is Schoar's terrain and she is right about it.

## Core Concept

The standard formulation inverts the problem for most segments.

**The claim:** informality is caused by excessive regulation; reduce the burden and firms will formalise.

**The reality:** **the regulatory environment often overlooks whole categories of the informal economy.** It does not burden them — it does not see them. A home-based garment worker in a subcontracting chain is not evading a regulation; no regulation contemplates her. A waste picker is not avoiding compliance; the municipal waste framework has no category for her except, perhaps, trespass. A domestic worker in a jurisdiction without domestic worker legislation is not outside the rules by choice.

**This is a missing regulatory environment**, and the remedy for absence is not further absence. Deregulation gives these workers nothing, because they were never regulated. What they need is the **extension of appropriate regulation and protection to work that has never been recognised**.

**Two further points that reframe the debate.**

**Bypassing rules is not unique to the informal economy.** It is also characteristic of businesses in the formal economy. The difference between the street trader and the firm with an aggressive tax structure is not compliance — it is which non-compliance gets policed, and against whom enforcement is directed. That asymmetry is a political choice presented as a technical fact.

**The colonial inheritance.** The categories, the by-laws and the enforcement architecture governing informal work in much of the global South inherit a legal order designed to control the movement and livelihoods of colonised populations. This is why the case for **decolonising policies and laws for the informal economy** is not rhetorical but specific: the instruments are literally the old instruments.

**What appropriate regulation looks like.** Occupational health and safety extended to informal workers, based on analysis of their **actual work places and actual work risks**. Trading space allocated with tenure. Recognition of the employment relationship where one exists in substance. Social protection detached from the formal employment contract. None of these require formalisation first.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask, per segment: what regulation currently recognises this work?** If the answer is none, the reform is extension, not reduction.
2. **Separate burden from absence.** Two entirely different chapters with different instruments.
3. **Locate the criminalising by-law.** For street trade and waste picking there is almost always one, and it is doing more work than any compliance burden.
4. **Extend OHS.** The most dangerous workplaces are informal ones; they are almost never in the OHS section.
5. **Note the asymmetry of enforcement** where it is relevant. Formal firms bypass rules too.
6. **Do not require formalisation as the precondition** for protection. It is not needed and it will not happen.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy's regulatory pillar proposes reducing compliance burden to encourage informal enterprises to formalise.

**Application:** For most of the informal workforce this addresses nothing, because they face no compliance burden — they face no regulation at all. The chapter should be split. One part: reducing burden on firms inside the formal system that want to grow, which is a real constraint (see Schoar). Another part: extending appropriate protective regulation to the categories currently unrecognised — a domestic work framework, waste picker integration into the municipal waste system, street trader tenure, OHS coverage. The second part is the one that reaches the population the chapter claims to be about, and it is the one currently absent.

**Result:** A regulatory chapter that reaches its stated beneficiaries.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Offer deregulation to the unregulated.
**Why:** They gain nothing. The environment overlooks them; removing more of it changes nothing.

**Don't:** Treat informality as illegality.
**Why:** Most informal work is licit work outside a framework that never contemplated it. The framing licenses enforcement against the poor.

**Don't:** Scope OHS to formal firms only.
**Why:** The most dangerous workplaces are informal ones, and extending coverage does not require formalisation first.
