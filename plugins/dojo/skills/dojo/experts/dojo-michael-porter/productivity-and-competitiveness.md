# Productivity and Competitiveness

## When to Use

- When defining competitiveness for government clients, development agencies, or economic development strategies — to prevent the use of misleading indicators.
- When a government measures competitiveness by trade surplus, export volume, or market share and needs to be redirected to productivity.
- When the conversation overemphasises macroeconomic policy and underemphasises the microeconomic business environment.
- When explaining why some nations and regions achieve high and rising living standards while others stagnate despite apparent economic activity.
- When challenging mercantilist thinking — the view that competitiveness is about winning in trade rather than about productivity.

## Fails When

- **You dismiss macroeconomic stability.** Sound fiscal and monetary policy, stable institutions, and open markets are necessary conditions for productivity growth. The point is not that macroeconomics is irrelevant but that it is insufficient — the real drivers are microeconomic.
- **You ignore distribution.** Aggregate productivity can rise while the benefits accrue to a narrow elite. Competitiveness in the fullest sense requires that productivity gains translate into rising living standards broadly, not just for the most productive segments.
- **You treat productivity as the only policy objective.** Employment creation, social cohesion, environmental sustainability, and equity are legitimate policy objectives that sometimes tension with narrow productivity maximisation. Productivity is the right measure of competitiveness; it is not the only thing that matters.

## Core Concept

What does it mean for a nation or region to be competitive? The question seems straightforward, but most popular answers are wrong. Competitiveness is not about trade surpluses — countries can run large trade deficits while enjoying high and rising living standards. It is not about export volume — a country can export enormous quantities of low-value commodities while its citizens remain poor. It is not about exchange rate management — devaluation makes exports cheaper but also makes imports more expensive, reducing purchasing power. It is not about market share in specific industries — a country can dominate a sector while its citizens earn subsistence wages.

Competitiveness, at the national or regional level, is defined as the ability to achieve a high and rising standard of living for its citizens. The only concept of competitiveness that is meaningful at the national level is productivity — the value of goods and services produced per unit of the nation's human, capital, and natural resources. A nation's standard of living is determined by the productivity of its economy, measured by the value of its output per unit of input.

Productivity depends on both the value of products and services — measured by the prices they command in the market — and the efficiency with which they are produced. A nation's firms can be productive by producing sophisticated goods and services that command premium prices, or by producing standard goods with superior efficiency, or both. What matters is the value of output, not the volume. A country that produces large quantities of low-value output is not productive; it is busy.

The productivity framework has several important implications. First, competitiveness is about raising productivity, not about suppressing wages. A nation that becomes "competitive" by reducing wages and working conditions is not genuinely more competitive — it has achieved a lower standard of living, which is the opposite of the objective. Genuine competitiveness raises both productivity and wages, because wages are ultimately determined by productivity.

Second, the microeconomic business environment is more important than macroeconomic policy. Sound monetary and fiscal policy, stable political institutions, open trade, and functioning capital markets are necessary conditions — they create the platform on which productivity can grow. But they are not sufficient. Many developing countries have achieved macroeconomic stability while remaining poor. The real drivers of productivity are microeconomic: the sophistication of company operations and strategy, the quality of the business environment (infrastructure, education, regulation, institutional quality), and the vitality of clusters.

Third, the relevant unit of analysis is the industry or cluster, not the economy as a whole. Aggregate national productivity is the weighted average of productivity across all industries. Improvement comes from raising productivity within existing industries and from shifting resources toward higher-productivity industries. Both require microeconomic interventions — improving the business environment, strengthening clusters, upgrading firm capabilities — not just macroeconomic management.

Fourth, competitiveness is not a zero-sum game. When one nation's firms become more productive, they create value — they do not merely capture value from other nations. Trade enables mutual gains because countries specialise in areas of comparative productivity advantage. The mercantilist framing — that one country's gain is another's loss — is fundamentally wrong.

## How to Apply

1. **Define competitiveness correctly.** When working with government clients or development agencies, insist on the productivity definition. Reject trade surplus, export volume, or exchange rate as competitiveness metrics. The test: is the standard of living — for citizens broadly, not just for the elite — rising because productivity is rising?

2. **Measure productivity, not activity.** Output volume, employment numbers, and GDP growth can all rise without productivity improvement — through more hours worked, more resources consumed, or currency depreciation. The relevant metric is value of output per unit of input.

3. **Diagnose the microeconomic foundations.** When a country or region underperforms despite macroeconomic stability, the diagnosis is almost always microeconomic: poor business environment quality, weak clusters, unsophisticated firm strategies, inadequate infrastructure, or dysfunctional regulation. The diamond model and cluster analysis are the diagnostic tools.

4. **Distinguish productivity-raising from wage-suppressing competitiveness.** If the strategy for becoming "more competitive" involves reducing wages, weakening labour standards, or degrading working conditions, it is not competitiveness — it is impoverishment by another name. Challenge this framing explicitly.

5. **Track productivity by industry and cluster.** Aggregate national productivity numbers conceal enormous variation across industries and regions. Diagnose at the industry and cluster level, where the specific drivers of productivity — and the specific constraints — can be identified and addressed.

## Examples

**Situation:** A national government cites its growing trade surplus as evidence that the country is becoming more competitive. The surplus is driven by commodity exports whose prices have risen due to global demand.
**Application:** A trade surplus driven by commodity price increases is not evidence of improved competitiveness. The volume of output has not changed; the world price has. When commodity prices decline — as they inevitably will — the surplus will disappear, and the country will have made no progress on the underlying productivity of its economy. Genuine competitiveness improvement would show up as higher productivity per worker in the commodity sector (more value extracted per unit of input) and as growth in higher-productivity non-commodity sectors.
**Result:** The government should measure competitiveness by productivity per worker across sectors, not by trade balance. The commodity windfall should be invested in the microeconomic foundations — infrastructure, education, cluster development — that will sustain productivity growth when commodity prices normalise.

**Situation:** A province reports that it has attracted significant foreign direct investment through tax incentives and subsidised industrial land. It claims this proves the province is competitive.
**Application:** Attracting FDI through subsidies does not demonstrate competitiveness — it demonstrates the willingness to pay firms to locate. The test is whether the firms would have come without the subsidies, based on the quality of the business environment, the availability of skilled labour, the strength of the cluster ecosystem, and the productivity advantages of the location. If they came only for the subsidies, they will leave when the subsidies expire or when a competing jurisdiction offers more.
**Result:** The province should assess whether the FDI has resulted in genuine productivity gains — through technology transfer, skills upgrading, supplier development, and cluster strengthening — or whether it has merely relocated economic activity that would have occurred elsewhere. Competitiveness is about the permanent business environment, not temporary incentives.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use GDP growth as a proxy for competitiveness improvement.
**Why:** GDP can grow through population growth, resource extraction, currency effects, or government spending — none of which represent productivity improvement. GDP per capita is closer but still imprecise. Productivity — output value per unit of input — is the correct metric.

**Don't:** Frame competitiveness as countries competing against each other for a fixed pool of economic value.
**Why:** Competitiveness is not zero-sum. When one country's firms become more productive, they create value that benefits trading partners through better products, lower prices, and new technologies. The mercantilist framing — that one country's gain requires another's loss — is analytically wrong and leads to protectionist policies that reduce everyone's productivity.

**Don't:** Ignore the distribution of productivity gains.
**Why:** Aggregate productivity improvement that accrues entirely to capital owners while workers' real wages stagnate is not competitiveness in any meaningful sense. The definition — achieving a high and rising standard of living — requires that productivity gains translate into broad-based prosperity. Track wages and living standards alongside productivity.
