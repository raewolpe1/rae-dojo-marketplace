# Strategic Positioning

## When to Use

- When defining the competitive position of a firm, organisation, or development programme — what unique value it delivers, to whom, through what different activities.
- When evaluating whether a stated position is genuinely strategic — i.e., based on different activities and protected by trade-offs — or merely a marketing claim.
- When designing activity systems that deliver and protect a chosen position.
- When diagnosing why a firm's position is being eroded — typically because trade-offs have been abandoned or fit has loosened.
- When a client asks "how should we position ourselves?" and needs the analytical framework, not just a tagline.

## Fails When

- **You treat positioning as a communications exercise.** Positioning is not a slogan or a brand promise. It is a choice about which activities to perform and how to configure them. A position that exists only in marketing materials and not in the activity system is not a position.
- **You assume positioning is permanent.** Positions must evolve as customer needs, technologies, and competitive dynamics change. But evolution should refine the position, not abandon it. The distinction is between strategic evolution and strategic abandonment.
- **You apply it to genuinely exploratory organisations.** Start-ups searching for product-market fit may need to experiment with multiple positions before committing. Premature commitment to a position can be as damaging as never choosing one.

## Core Concept

Strategic positioning means choosing a different set of activities from rivals to deliver a unique mix of value. It is the foundation of competitive strategy — without a unique position, a firm is competing on operational effectiveness alone, which leads to competitive convergence and declining profitability for all.

Strategic positions emerge from three distinct sources, which are not mutually exclusive.

**Variety-based positioning** means producing a subset of an industry's products or services. The positioning is based on the choice of product or service varieties rather than customer segments. A firm serves a wide array of customers but only meets a subset of their needs. The position makes economic sense when a firm can best produce particular products or services using distinctive activities. Jiffy Lube provides only auto lubricants — no repairs, no tyre rotations — and serves a broad range of customers who choose it for that specific service because its specialised activity system delivers it faster and more conveniently than general auto repair shops.

**Needs-based positioning** means serving most or all the needs of a particular group of customers. This is closest to traditional customer segmentation, but it is only strategic when the activities required to serve that customer group are different from those required to serve other groups. If the same activities serve all customer groups equally well, there is no strategic significance to the segmentation. IKEA serves young, budget-conscious furniture buyers who are willing to trade service for price — and delivers this through an activity system (self-service, flat-pack, in-store childcare, suburban warehouse format) that is fundamentally different from traditional furniture retailers.

**Access-based positioning** means segmenting customers who are accessible in different ways — by geography, customer scale, or anything that requires a different set of activities to reach them. A cinema chain in rural areas requires a different activity system from one in urban centres, even if the product is the same. Serving very small businesses requires a different distribution and service model from serving large enterprises.

The critical insight is that a strategic position is not defined by the value proposition alone but by the activity system that delivers it. Two firms can articulate similar-sounding value propositions, but if one has an activity system tailored to deliver it and the other performs generic activities, only the first has a strategic position.

**Activity systems** make the position concrete and defensible. An activity system is the set of interconnected activities through which a firm delivers its value proposition. Each activity reinforces others, creating a system that is greater than the sum of its parts. The activity system map — showing the core strategic themes, the supporting activities, and the connections between them — is the visual representation of a strategy.

The defensibility of a position comes from three sources: trade-offs (competitors cannot match the position without giving up their own), fit (replicating one activity is easy; replicating ten interlocking activities is exponentially harder), and continuity (deep investment in the position over time creates capabilities and relationships that new entrants cannot quickly replicate).

## How to Apply

1. **Define the value proposition.** What unique value does the organisation deliver, to which customers, at what relative price? The proposition must be different from competitors' — not just better.

2. **Identify the basis of positioning.** Is it variety-based (a subset of products/services), needs-based (a specific customer group), or access-based (customers accessible in a distinctive way)? The basis determines the type of activities required.

3. **Design the activity system.** Map the activities required to deliver the value proposition. Identify the core strategic themes (3–5 activities that define the position) and the supporting activities that enable them. Show the connections — where activities reinforce each other.

4. **Identify the trade-offs.** What activities must be given up or performed differently to sustain this position? What would a competitor have to sacrifice to replicate it? If there are no trade-offs, the position is not defensible.

5. **Test for fit.** Does each activity reinforce the others? Are there three types of fit — consistency, reinforcement, and optimisation? The more deeply the activities are interlocked, the more sustainable the position.

6. **Protect continuity.** Resist the temptation to reposition in response to every competitive move. Maintain the core positioning while continuously improving how the position is delivered. Monitor for strategic drift — the gradual loosening of trade-offs and fit under competitive pressure.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African development consultancy competes with both large international firms (McKinsey, Deloitte, PwC) and small local freelancers. It struggles to win tenders against either.
**Application:** The firm is stuck in the middle — too small to match the international firms' brand and bench strength, too expensive to match freelancers' price. The solution is needs-based positioning: identify a specific client segment (e.g., provincial government economic development departments) whose needs are poorly served by both internationals (too expensive, too generic, limited local context) and freelancers (too narrow, limited analytical depth). Design an activity system tailored to this segment: senior-led teams with deep local knowledge, proprietary analytical frameworks relevant to government planning, established relationships with government decision-makers, and a pricing model that offers value between the international and freelance options.
**Result:** The positioning creates trade-offs: the firm deliberately does not pursue private-sector corporate strategy work (the internationals' strength) or high-volume, low-cost tender work (the freelancers' space). The trade-offs protect the position: competitors would have to sacrifice their own strengths to replicate it.

**Situation:** A regional tourism destination tries to position itself as simultaneously a budget family destination, a luxury eco-tourism experience, and an adventure sports hub.
**Application:** Three positions, each requiring a different activity system. Budget family tourism requires affordable accommodation, family entertainment, and mass-market marketing. Luxury eco-tourism requires exclusive lodges, curated nature experiences, and premium service. Adventure sports requires specialised equipment, safety infrastructure, and thrill-seeking marketing. These activity systems are inconsistent — investing in luxury exclusivity contradicts mass-market affordability; adventure marketing contradicts family safety messaging. The destination has no position because it has refused to choose.
**Result:** The destination must select the position where its natural assets, infrastructure, and competitive advantages are strongest. If it has pristine natural areas and proximity to affluent markets, luxury eco-tourism is the defensible position. This means deliberately not investing in mass-market family entertainment or adventure sports infrastructure — accepting the trade-off in exchange for a coherent, defensible position.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Define positioning in terms of marketing messages rather than activities.
**Why:** A position that exists in the brochure but not in the activity system is not a position. The test is whether the activities are configured differently from competitors, not whether the tagline sounds different.

**Don't:** Pursue multiple positions simultaneously in the same business unit.
**Why:** Each position requires a different activity system. Pursuing multiple positions dilutes all of them because the activities required are contradictory. A firm can serve multiple segments only if the same activity system serves all of them — which usually means the segmentation is not strategically significant.

**Don't:** Change positioning in response to every competitive move.
**Why:** Frequent repositioning prevents the development of fit, confuses customers and employees, and erodes the deep capabilities that sustain the position. Respond to competitive moves by deepening and improving the chosen position, not by abandoning it. Change positioning only when the fundamental basis — customer needs, technology, or industry structure — has permanently shifted.
