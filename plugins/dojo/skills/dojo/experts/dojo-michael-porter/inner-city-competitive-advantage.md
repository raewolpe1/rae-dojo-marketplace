# Competitive Advantage of the Inner City

## When to Use

- When designing economic development strategy for disadvantaged urban areas — townships, inner cities, informal settlements — where past welfare-based approaches have not created sustainable economic bases.
- When a government or development agency asks how to create jobs and businesses in communities characterised by high unemployment, poverty, and economic exclusion.
- When evaluating enterprise development programmes — asking whether they are built on genuine competitive advantages or on subsidised dependency.
- When the conversation about underserved areas is framed entirely in terms of needs, deficits, and problems, and requires reframing around assets, advantages, and opportunities.
- When connecting urban economic development to the broader regional cluster strategy.

## Fails When

- **You ignore structural barriers.** Inner cities and townships face real constraints — crime, inadequate infrastructure, regulatory burden, skills deficits, capital access barriers. The competitive advantage framework does not deny these constraints; it argues that addressing them through a business lens is more effective than addressing them through a welfare lens. But the constraints are real and must be addressed.
- **You assume the private sector operates in a vacuum.** Private-sector-led development still requires public investment in infrastructure, safety, education, and regulatory reform. The framework argues for private-sector *leadership*, not private-sector isolation.
- **You apply it without historical context.** In South Africa, the spatial economics of townships and inner cities reflect deliberate policies of racial exclusion. The competitive advantage framework must be applied with awareness that current economic structure is not the result of market forces alone.
- **You treat it as a substitute for social investment.** The framework argues that economic development should be the primary strategy, not that social programmes are unnecessary. Health, education, and social safety nets are complements to economic development, not substitutes for it.

## Core Concept

The economic distress of disadvantaged urban areas is among the most pressing development challenges. The lack of businesses and jobs fuels cycles of poverty and compounds social problems. Past approaches — income assistance, housing subsidies, social programmes, government-directed investment — have failed to create sustainable economic bases because they treat distressed areas as charity cases rather than business locations. They focus on the needs of residents rather than on the competitive advantages of the place.

The competitive advantage framework proposes a fundamental reorientation: inner cities and disadvantaged urban areas possess genuine competitive advantages that can support profitable businesses and sustainable job creation, if these advantages are recognised and exploited through a for-profit economic development model.

Four competitive advantages characterise disadvantaged urban areas. **Strategic location:** inner cities and townships are often located near city centres, major transport corridors, ports, or commercial districts. Proximity to business concentrations, customers, and transport infrastructure creates logistical advantages that suburban or rural locations cannot match. **Local market demand:** high population density compensates for lower average incomes, creating aggregate demand that supports viable businesses — particularly in retail, food service, personal services, financial services, and construction. Underserved local markets represent opportunities for entrepreneurs who understand local preferences and needs. **Integration with regional clusters:** disadvantaged areas are part of larger metropolitan economies with established clusters. Businesses in these areas can serve as suppliers, subcontractors, and service providers to regional cluster participants — but only if the linkages are actively developed. **Human resources:** urban areas concentrate labour forces with relevant skills and work motivation, often at lower cost than suburban alternatives.

The model of inner-city economic development rests on several principles. First, the private sector, not government or social service organisations, must be the primary driver. Government's role is to create a favourable business environment — addressing crime, improving infrastructure, streamlining regulation — not to direct investment or manage businesses. Second, the focus must be on for-profit businesses, not subsidised social enterprises. Businesses that survive only through subsidy do not create sustainable jobs or economic multipliers. Third, development strategy should be integrated with the broader regional economy, not isolated. Inner-city businesses should compete in and connect to mainstream markets, not just serve local demand. Fourth, the approach must build on genuine competitive advantages, not on artificial advantages created by subsidies.

The framework has particular resonance in the South African context, where the spatial legacy of apartheid created economically isolated townships that were deliberately excluded from the mainstream economy. Reconnecting these areas to the regional economy through cluster linkages, infrastructure investment, and business environment improvement is a direct application of the competitive advantage approach. The challenge is more severe than in the American inner-city context because the distances are greater, the infrastructure gaps are wider, and the legacy of exclusion is more systematic — but the underlying economic logic is the same.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the genuine competitive advantages.** What does this specific area offer that other locations do not? Strategic location relative to transport, markets, and clusters? Underserved local market demand? Proximity to regional cluster participants? Available workforce? Be specific — the advantages must be real, not aspirational.

2. **Identify the binding constraints.** What specific business environment problems suppress the exploitation of these advantages? Crime and safety? Infrastructure quality? Regulatory burden? Skills gaps? Capital access? These are the barriers that government must address to make the competitive advantages usable.

3. **Design for-profit business opportunities.** Based on the competitive advantages, identify the specific business opportunities that can be profitable without permanent subsidy. Local retail and services serving underserved demand? Supplier and subcontracting relationships with regional cluster firms? Logistics and distribution leveraging strategic location?

4. **Connect to the regional economy.** Design the economic development strategy to integrate the area with the broader metropolitan economy, not to isolate it. Develop cluster linkages, facilitate supplier development relationships, and invest in transport connectivity that enables firms in the area to participate in regional value chains.

5. **Address the business environment.** Government must play its role: invest in safety and security, upgrade infrastructure, streamline regulation, support skills development, and facilitate access to capital. These are the public-goods investments that make private-sector-led development viable.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African metropolitan municipality is designing an economic development strategy for a large township on its periphery. The current approach consists of social grants, a subsidised business incubator, and a small enterprise development fund. Unemployment exceeds 40%.
**Application:** The current approach treats the township as a welfare case. The competitive advantage framework asks different questions: What are the township's genuine competitive advantages? Proximity to the N2 transport corridor (strategic location), population of 300,000 creating substantial aggregate demand (local market), location within the metropolitan economy that includes food processing and light manufacturing clusters (cluster integration), and a labour force that includes experienced construction, retail, and service workers (human resources). The binding constraints are crime and safety, unreliable electricity and water, poor internal road networks, and complex regulatory requirements for small businesses.
**Result:** The strategy should shift from subsidised incubation to business environment improvement: invest in safety and infrastructure, streamline business registration and licensing, develop supplier linkages between township firms and metropolitan cluster participants, and support entrepreneurs pursuing for-profit opportunities in underserved local markets (retail, food service, construction, personal services) and regional value chains (food processing suppliers, light manufacturing subcontractors).

**Situation:** A development finance institution is evaluating whether to fund a township shopping centre or a supplier development programme linking township manufacturers to regional automotive OEMs.
**Application:** The shopping centre exploits the local market demand advantage — high population density supports retail. The supplier development programme exploits the cluster integration advantage — connecting township manufacturers to the regional automotive cluster. Both are legitimate applications of the competitive advantage framework. The shopping centre creates retail employment and captures spending that currently leaks to suburban centres. The supplier development programme creates higher-value manufacturing employment and integrates township firms into the regional economy. The supplier programme has higher developmental impact because it creates exportable capabilities and stronger cluster linkages, but it requires more sustained investment in skills and quality systems.
**Result:** Both can be funded, but the supplier development programme should receive priority because it addresses the more fundamental challenge — integrating township firms into the productive economy rather than only serving local consumption demand.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Frame disadvantaged areas only in terms of needs and deficits.
**Why:** The needs lens leads to welfare-based responses that create dependency. The competitive advantage lens leads to business-based responses that create sustainability. Both perspectives are true, but the development strategy should be built on the advantages, with the deficits addressed as barriers to exploiting those advantages.

**Don't:** Assume that any business in a disadvantaged area is development.
**Why:** Businesses that survive only through subsidy, serve only the subsidising agency, or employ no local workers are not creating sustainable economic value. The test is whether the business would be commercially viable in the medium term without permanent subsidy, connected to genuine competitive advantages.

**Don't:** Isolate the area's economy from the regional economy.
**Why:** Economic isolation is the problem, not the solution. Strategies that focus only on local-serving businesses without connecting to regional clusters and value chains perpetuate the marginalisation that created the problem. Integration, not isolation, is the development pathway.
