# The Diamond Model

## When to Use

- When diagnosing why a country or region is internationally competitive in some industries but not others — the diamond explains the pattern.
- When designing an economic development strategy — the diamond identifies the specific facets that need strengthening.
- When evaluating government competitiveness policy — the diamond provides the diagnostic framework against which policy can be assessed.
- When a region claims competitive advantage based on natural resources or cheap labour — the diamond reveals why inherited advantages are insufficient.
- When comparing the competitive position of different locations to inform investment or development decisions.
- When advising governments on how to improve the business environment for specific clusters.

## Fails When

- **You treat the four facets as independent variables.** The power of the diamond is in the interactions — the facets are a system with reinforcing feedback loops. Analysing each facet in isolation misses the dynamic.
- **You use it to justify government picking of winners.** The diamond diagnoses competitive advantage; it does not prescribe which industries government should subsidise or protect. Government's role is to strengthen the diamond, not to direct it.
- **You apply it at the wrong level of aggregation.** The diamond operates at the level of specific industries and clusters, not at the level of the national economy as a whole. "South Africa is competitive" is meaningless; "South Africa's wine industry has strong diamond conditions" is diagnostic.
- **You ignore chance and government.** While the four facets are the core determinants, chance events (technological discontinuities, wars, commodity shocks) and government policy can significantly alter the diamond. These are influencing variables, not core determinants, but they cannot be ignored.

## Core Concept

Why do particular nations become home to internationally competitive firms in particular industries? The answer lies not in inherited endowments — natural resources, cheap labour, geographic position — but in four interlinked attributes that constitute the "diamond" of national advantage.

**Factor conditions.** The nation's position in factors of production — labour, capital, natural resources, infrastructure, knowledge resources — necessary to compete in a given industry. The critical distinction is between basic factors (natural resources, climate, geographic location, unskilled labour) and advanced factors (digital infrastructure, university research programmes, specialised training institutions, sophisticated capital markets). Basic factors are inherited; advanced factors are created through sustained investment. Advanced, specialised factors are the most significant for competitive advantage because they are harder to replicate and they directly support innovation and productivity. Paradoxically, selective disadvantage in basic factors can be an advantage — it forces firms to innovate and upgrade, while abundance of basic factors breeds complacency. Japan's lack of natural resources compelled its industries to develop extraordinary efficiency; nations with abundant oil often suffer from the resource curse.

**Demand conditions.** The nature of home demand for an industry's product or service. The most important dimension is not the size of domestic demand but its character — specifically, whether local buyers are sophisticated and demanding. Sophisticated domestic customers pressure firms to meet high standards, to innovate, and to anticipate international trends. The composition of home demand also matters: if local demand segments represent large or growing portions of international demand, firms gain early insight into emerging needs. Italian consumers' discriminating tastes in shoes and fashion pushed Italian manufacturers to levels of quality and design sophistication that translated directly into international competitive advantage.

**Related and supporting industries.** The presence or absence in the nation of supplier industries and related industries that are internationally competitive. Competitive local suppliers create advantages in downstream industries through efficient, early, and sometimes preferential access to the most cost-effective inputs. More important, competitive local suppliers and related industries provide the basis for ongoing information exchange, technical interchange, and innovation. The presence of competitive related industries — industries that share technologies, skills, distribution channels, or customers — provides similar benefits. Italian ceramic tile machinery manufacturers and Italian ceramic tile producers reinforced each other's competitive advantage through continuous interaction and mutual upgrading.

**Firm strategy, structure, and rivalry.** The conditions governing how firms are created, organised, and managed, and the nature of domestic rivalry. This facet encompasses the goals, strategies, and structures of firms, which vary systematically across nations, and — most importantly — the intensity of domestic competition. Vigorous domestic rivalry is perhaps the single most powerful driver of competitive advantage. It pressures firms to reduce costs, improve quality, innovate, and invest in advanced factors. Nations with strong domestic rivalry in an industry almost invariably develop international competitive advantage. Domestic rivalry creates pressure for improvement; it creates knowledge spillovers as employees move between firms; it forces firms to move beyond basic advantages. The Italian ceramic tile industry had dozens of fiercely competing domestic firms; this rivalry drove continuous innovation and upgrading that made Italy the world leader.

**Government** is not a fifth force but an influencing variable. Government policy can strengthen or weaken each of the four determinants. At its best, government acts as catalyst and challenger — investing in specialised factor creation, maintaining vigorous domestic competition, setting demanding product standards, and stimulating early demand for advanced products. At its worst, government distorts the diamond through protectionism, subsidies to incumbents, and suppression of domestic rivalry.

**Chance** events — major technological discontinuities, wars, significant shifts in world financial markets, surges in global demand, political decisions by foreign governments — can reshape the diamond by creating openings for some nations and closing them for others. Chance events are important because they create discontinuities that allow shifts in competitive position. But chance alone does not determine outcomes; the response to chance events depends on the condition of the diamond.

## How to Apply

1. **Define the industry specifically.** The diamond operates at the level of specific industries and industry segments, not at the level of the economy. Analyse the competitive advantage of the Western Cape wine industry, not "the Western Cape economy."

2. **Assess each facet systematically.** For each of the four determinants, identify the current condition: Are factor conditions specialised and created, or basic and inherited? Are local customers demanding and sophisticated? Is there a strong ecosystem of related and supporting industries? Is domestic rivalry vigorous?

3. **Map the interactions.** The facets reinforce each other. Demanding customers push firms to innovate (demand → rivalry); vigorous rivalry creates demand for specialised factors (rivalry → factors); competitive suppliers upgrade the entire cluster (related industries → firm competitiveness). Identify where the feedback loops are strong and where they are broken.

4. **Identify the weakest facet.** The weakest facet is typically the binding constraint on competitive advantage. A region with strong factor conditions but weak domestic rivalry will underperform its potential because the pressure to innovate and upgrade is insufficient.

5. **Design interventions targeted at the diamond.** Effective policy strengthens specific facets: investing in specialised education and research (factors), setting demanding regulatory standards (demand), supporting cluster development (related industries), and maintaining competition policy (rivalry). Avoid interventions that weaken the diamond — subsidies that reduce competitive pressure, protection that shields firms from rivalry, or directed investment that substitutes government judgement for market signals.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African province wants to understand why its agri-processing sector underperforms relative to comparable sectors in Chile and New Zealand.
**Application:** Diamond analysis reveals: Factor conditions — the province has basic agricultural factors (land, climate, water) but lacks specialised factors (advanced food technology research, specialised logistics infrastructure, industry-specific training programmes). Demand conditions — the domestic market is price-sensitive rather than quality-demanding, providing little pressure to upgrade. Related and supporting industries — specialised packaging, cold chain logistics, and food technology service providers are weak or absent. Firm strategy and rivalry — the sector is dominated by a few large firms with limited rivalry, reducing the competitive pressure to innovate.
**Result:** The weakest facets are specialised factors and related/supporting industries. Policy should focus on building specialised research and training capacity, investing in cold chain infrastructure, and encouraging entry and competition rather than consolidating the sector further. The province should study how Chile and New Zealand systematically strengthened these facets over two decades.

**Situation:** A national government argues that its country's competitive advantage lies in abundant mineral resources and low labour costs.
**Application:** These are basic, inherited factor conditions — the weakest possible foundation for sustained competitive advantage. The diamond framework predicts that reliance on basic factors leads to low-productivity competition that cannot sustain rising living standards. The resource curse is well-documented: abundant natural resources attract rent-seeking behaviour, reduce the pressure to invest in advanced factors, appreciate the exchange rate (hurting other export sectors), and create volatile fiscal revenue that undermines long-term planning.
**Result:** The government should treat mineral endowments as a funding source for creating advanced, specialised factors — not as the competitive advantage itself. The strategic challenge is to use resource rents to build the education systems, research institutions, infrastructure, and institutional quality that create genuine competitive advantage in higher-productivity industries.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Treat the diamond as a static diagnosis.
**Why:** The diamond is a dynamic system. Today's strong facets can weaken if not continuously upgraded, and weak facets can be strengthened through sustained investment. The diagnosis should drive action, not merely describe the current state.

**Don't:** Apply the diamond at the level of the entire economy.
**Why:** Competitive advantage is industry-specific. A nation may have a strong diamond in wine but a weak one in software. Aggregating to the national level obscures the industry-specific patterns that drive competitive outcomes and policy design.

**Don't:** Use the diamond to argue for protectionism.
**Why:** Protection weakens the rivalry facet of the diamond — the single most important driver of competitive upgrading. Shielding firms from competition reduces the pressure to innovate, invest in advanced factors, and meet demanding customer standards. The diamond explicitly predicts that vigorous domestic rivalry, not protection from it, creates international competitive advantage.
