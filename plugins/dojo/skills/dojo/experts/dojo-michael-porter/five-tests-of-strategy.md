# Five Tests of a Good Strategy

## When to Use

- When reviewing a strategic plan, economic development strategy, or organisational strategy document and needing a structured evaluation framework.
- When an organisation asks "do we have a strategy?" — the five tests provide the diagnostic.
- When advising leadership on whether their stated strategy is genuine or merely aspirational.
- When teaching the concept of strategy and needing a practical, memorable framework for assessment.
- When diagnosing strategic drift — an organisation that once had a strategy but has gradually abandoned its trade-offs and lost its distinctiveness.

## Fails When

- **You treat it as a mechanical checklist.** Ticking boxes is not the point. Each test requires substantive judgement about whether the element is genuinely present and strong, not merely asserted.
- **You assume that passing all five tests guarantees success.** A good strategy can still fail through poor execution, external shocks, or unforeseen competitive responses. The tests evaluate strategic quality, not certainty of outcome.
- **You apply it to organisations that are legitimately in an exploratory phase.** Early-stage ventures searching for product-market fit may not yet have committed to all five elements. The tests apply to organisations that should have a strategy — not to those that are still searching for one.

## Core Concept

A good strategy is not a vision statement, a list of goals, or a set of improvement initiatives. It is an integrated set of choices that defines a unique competitive position. Porter identifies five tests that a strategy must pass to be considered genuine. If any one of the five is missing, the organisation does not have a complete strategy — it has, at best, a partial framework that will erode under competitive pressure.

**Test 1: A unique value proposition.** The strategy must define a value proposition that is different from competitors'. This means specifying which customers the organisation will serve, which needs it will meet, and at what relative price — and how this combination differs from what rivals offer. A value proposition that could equally describe three competitors is not unique and therefore not strategic. The value proposition answers: what distinct value do we provide, to whom, and at what relative price?

**Test 2: A different, tailored value chain.** The value proposition must be delivered through a value chain — a set of activities — that is different from competitors'. If the organisation offers a different value proposition but performs the same activities in the same way as rivals, the proposition is unsustainable because any operational advantage will be competed away. The activities must be tailored to the specific value proposition. IKEA's value chain is fundamentally different from traditional furniture retailers': self-service, flat-pack design, in-house design, suburban warehouse stores, customer assembly. The value chain is the mechanism through which the value proposition is delivered.

**Test 3: Clear trade-offs.** The strategy must make explicit choices about what the organisation will not do. Trade-offs arise because certain positions are incompatible — activities that deliver one value proposition are inconsistent with activities that deliver another. A full-service airline cannot simultaneously operate with Southwest's cost structure. A luxury brand cannot simultaneously pursue mass-market pricing. Without trade-offs, the strategy has no protection — competitors can straddle or copy without penalty. The question is: what have you deliberately chosen not to do?

**Test 4: Activities that fit together and reinforce each other.** The individual activities must form a coherent system in which each activity strengthens the others. Three types of fit matter: simple consistency (all activities are aligned with the overall direction), reinforcement (activities strengthen each other's effect), and optimisation of effort (coordination among activities eliminates redundancy). Activity systems with strong fit are exponentially harder to replicate than individual activities because a competitor must match the entire system, not just one piece of it.

**Test 5: Strategic continuity with continual improvement.** A good strategy is maintained over time, with continuous improvement in how the strategy is realised but without constant reinvention of the fundamental positioning. Strategic continuity allows the organisation to develop deep competence in its chosen activities, to build reputation and relationships with its target customers, and to deepen the fit among its activities. Frequent repositioning signals strategic confusion, prevents the development of fit, and erodes organisational coherence. Continuity does not mean rigidity — the organisation should continuously improve its chosen position and adapt to external changes — but the core positioning should be stable.

The five tests are not independent — they form an integrated logic. The unique value proposition defines what the organisation offers. The tailored value chain delivers it. Trade-offs protect it. Fit among activities sustains it. Continuity deepens it. Weakness in any element undermines the whole.

## How to Apply

1. **Test 1 — Value proposition.** Can the organisation state, in one or two sentences, what distinct value it provides, to which customers, and how this differs from competitors? If the value proposition could describe any competitor in the sector, it fails this test.

2. **Test 2 — Tailored value chain.** Are the organisation's key activities configured differently from competitors'? List the five most important activities and ask: does each activity directly support the value proposition? Are they done differently from how competitors do them?

3. **Test 3 — Trade-offs.** Can the organisation name three to five things it has deliberately chosen not to do? If leadership cannot articulate what they've given up, they have not made trade-offs, and the strategy has no protection.

4. **Test 4 — Fit.** Draw the activity system map: list the core activities and show how they reinforce each other. If the activities are independent — a collection of "strategic initiatives" with no connections — fit is absent.

5. **Test 5 — Continuity.** Has the core strategic positioning been stable for at least three to five years? If the organisation repositions annually in response to competitive moves or quarterly earnings pressure, it has not developed the depth of fit that creates sustainable advantage.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial economic development agency presents its strategy: "We will make this province the most competitive in the country by investing in infrastructure, skills, SMME development, and innovation."
**Application:** Test 1 — the value proposition is not unique; every province makes the same claim. Test 2 — no tailored activity system; the four priorities are generic. Test 3 — no trade-offs; nothing has been chosen not to do. Test 4 — the four priorities are listed independently with no explanation of how they reinforce each other. Test 5 — irrelevant because there is no strategy to sustain. The plan fails all five tests. It is an aspiration, not a strategy.
**Result:** The agency needs to make genuine choices: which specific clusters will receive concentrated attention, what distinct competitive advantages will be built, what will be deliberately deprioritised, and how the chosen investments will reinforce each other.

**Situation:** A specialised consulting firm positions itself as the only firm offering integrated development economics and monitoring and evaluation services to government clients, with proprietary analytical frameworks and a small team of senior-level consultants who are directly involved in every engagement.
**Application:** Test 1 — the value proposition is distinct: integrated development economics and M&E, with senior involvement and proprietary methods, targeting government clients. This is different from large generalist consulting firms. Test 2 — the value chain is tailored: senior-led engagement teams (not junior-heavy pyramids), proprietary frameworks (not generic methodologies), government-sector specialisation (not broad market coverage). Test 3 — trade-offs are clear: smaller scale, narrower market, no commodity consulting, no junior-led delivery. Test 4 — the activities reinforce each other: senior involvement enables proprietary methodologies, which justify premium positioning, which supports small team size. Test 5 — if the positioning has been maintained over time, continuity is present.
**Result:** This firm passes the five tests. The risk is strategic drift: growth pressure may tempt the firm to hire junior staff, pursue non-government clients, or adopt generic methodologies — each of which would erode the distinctive positioning.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Accept vague trade-offs like "we don't do bad work."
**Why:** A trade-off must be a genuine sacrifice — giving up something that a competitor pursues. "We don't do bad work" is not a trade-off; it is a quality standard that every competitor claims. Real trade-offs: "We don't serve clients below R500,000 in annual revenue." "We don't offer standard off-the-shelf solutions." "We don't compete on price."

**Don't:** Confuse operational improvement with strategic continuity.
**Why:** Strategic continuity means maintaining the core positioning over time. It does not mean freezing operations. The strategy should be stable; the execution should improve continuously. Confusing the two leads to either strategic rigidity (refusing to improve execution) or strategic instability (changing positioning whenever operations change).

**Don't:** Apply the five tests only once.
**Why:** Strategies erode under competitive pressure. Trade-offs are abandoned to pursue growth. Fit loosens as new initiatives are added without checking for coherence. Periodic application of the five tests — annually, at minimum — catches strategic drift before it becomes terminal.
