# Cluster Theory

## When to Use

- When designing economic development strategy for a region, province, or city — clusters are the natural unit of analysis and intervention.
- When diagnosing why firms in a particular location underperform — the cluster ecosystem may be the binding constraint.
- When evaluating cluster initiatives, special economic zones, or industrial park proposals.
- When a government is choosing between firm-level subsidies and business environment investments — cluster theory provides the rationale for the latter.
- When mapping a regional economy to understand its genuine specialisations and competitive advantages.
- When a region asks how to diversify its economy or upgrade from low-value to higher-value activities.

## Fails When

- **You define clusters too broadly.** A "manufacturing cluster" or "services cluster" is not a cluster — it is a sector. Clusters are defined by the specific economic linkages between firms, suppliers, related industries, and institutions. If the participants don't interact meaningfully, it is not a cluster.
- **You treat cluster development as top-down industrial policy.** Government cannot create clusters by decree. Clusters emerge from genuine economic linkages and market forces. Government can strengthen existing clusters, remove obstacles, and invest in supporting institutions, but it cannot designate a cluster into existence.
- **You assume all clusters are healthy.** Clusters can become rigid, resistant to change, and locked into obsolete technologies. The Detroit automobile cluster in the 2000s was a cluster — and it was failing. Cluster analysis must include the question of whether the cluster is upgrading or stagnating.
- **You ignore the difference between cluster mapping and cluster strategy.** Identifying clusters is the first step, not the strategy. The strategy requires diagnosing the specific strengths and weaknesses of each cluster and designing targeted interventions.

## Core Concept

Clusters are geographic concentrations of interconnected companies, specialised suppliers, service providers, firms in related industries, and associated institutions — universities, standards agencies, trade associations, vocational training providers — in a particular field that compete but also cooperate. They are a striking feature of virtually every national, regional, and metropolitan economy, especially in advanced nations.

Clusters affect competitiveness through three broad channels. First, clusters **increase the productivity** of constituent firms. Being part of a cluster allows firms to operate more productively in sourcing inputs: specialised suppliers are nearby, reducing transaction costs and logistics delays. Specialised labour pools reduce search and training costs. Knowledge spillovers — the informal exchange of technical information, market intelligence, and operational know-how — accelerate learning. Complementary products and services from related firms enhance the value proposition to buyers. The cluster provides access to specialised institutions, public goods, and infrastructure that individual firms could not justify independently.

Second, clusters **drive the direction and pace of innovation**. Proximity to sophisticated buyers, intense rivalry among local competitors, and the presence of specialised research institutions create an environment where innovation is both demanded and supported. Firms within clusters can perceive buyer needs and technology opportunities earlier than isolated competitors. The cost of experimenting and innovating is lower because specialised inputs, testing facilities, and knowledgeable partners are close at hand. Competitive pressure from local rivals intensifies the urgency to innovate.

Third, clusters **stimulate new business formation**. Clusters lower the barriers to entry for new firms: specialised inputs are available, customer relationships are visible, gaps in the value chain are apparent, and skilled employees may choose to start their own firms using knowledge gained from cluster incumbents. The cluster provides a concentration of informed and experienced individuals who can identify opportunities and mobilise resources to exploit them. Many of the inputs a new firm needs are available locally, reducing the risk and capital requirements of entry.

Cluster boundaries do not follow standard industrial classifications or political jurisdictions. They are defined by the linkages and complementarities across industries and institutions that are most important to competition. The California wine cluster, for example, includes grape growers, winemakers, equipment suppliers, barrel makers, bottle manufacturers, cork suppliers, label designers, irrigation technology firms, wine publications, research institutions (UC Davis), tourism organisations, and wine-country hospitality businesses. Understanding the full cluster — not just the primary industry — is essential for diagnosis and policy.

The implications for economic development are profound. Traditional approaches — subsidising individual firms, offering tax incentives to attract mobile investment, building generic industrial parks — are inferior to cluster-based approaches. Cluster development invests in the business environment that makes all firms in the cluster more productive: specialised education and training, applied research, efficient regulation, targeted infrastructure, and institutions that facilitate collaboration and information exchange. The returns to cluster-level investment are shared across many firms and are far more sustainable than firm-level subsidies.

Government's proper role in cluster development is not to pick which clusters should develop but to reinforce the development of clusters that are already emerging. Government should remove obstacles to cluster growth, invest in public goods that support the cluster, and convene cluster participants to identify shared challenges and collective actions. Government should not direct investment, protect cluster participants from competition, or substitute its judgement for market signals about which clusters have genuine competitive potential.

## How to Apply

1. **Map the region's clusters.** Identify the geographic concentrations of interconnected firms, suppliers, related industries, and institutions. Use employment data, export data, firm surveys, and input-output analysis to identify the linkages. Be specific — the goal is to identify the actual economic relationships, not to name broad sectors.

2. **Assess each cluster's competitive position.** For each significant cluster, apply the diamond model: What are the factor conditions? The demand conditions? The related and supporting industries? The intensity of domestic rivalry? Where is the cluster strong, and where is it weak?

3. **Identify the binding constraints.** What specific gaps in the cluster ecosystem are limiting productivity, innovation, or new business formation? Missing specialised suppliers? Inadequate skills pipeline? Weak research institutions? Insufficient rivalry? These constraints, not firm-level weaknesses, are the priority targets for intervention.

4. **Design cluster-specific interventions.** Invest in the specialised public goods that address the binding constraints: training programmes aligned to cluster needs, applied research facilities, targeted infrastructure, regulatory streamlining for cluster-specific processes, and institutions that facilitate information exchange and collective action.

5. **Convene cluster participants.** Bring together firms, suppliers, institutions, and government agencies to identify shared challenges, coordinate investments, and build collaborative capacity. Cluster initiatives work best when they are led by the private sector with government support, not when they are government-directed.

6. **Monitor cluster health over time.** Track productivity growth, innovation output, new firm formation, export performance, and employment quality within each cluster. Clusters that are upgrading show improvement across these dimensions; clusters that are stagnating show flat or declining performance despite stable size.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African metropolitan municipality is designing its economic development strategy. It has identified "manufacturing" as a priority sector and plans to build an industrial park with subsidised rentals.
**Application:** "Manufacturing" is not a cluster — it is a residual category encompassing dozens of unrelated industries. The municipality needs to identify its specific clusters: perhaps automotive components (linked to national OEM assembly), food processing (linked to regional agriculture), or specialised engineering services (linked to mining and energy). An industrial park with subsidised rentals is a firm-level subsidy, not a cluster development strategy. It attracts cost-sensitive firms that may have no linkages to each other or to the local economy.
**Result:** The municipality should map its actual clusters, diagnose the specific binding constraints in each (skills gaps, supplier weaknesses, infrastructure bottlenecks, regulatory obstacles), and invest in the cluster-level public goods that address those constraints. The industrial park may be useful if it is designed around a specific cluster's co-location needs, but it should not be the strategy itself.

**Situation:** A provincial government wants to develop a green hydrogen cluster around its port and renewable energy assets.
**Application:** The government has identified basic factor conditions (port, renewable energy resources) but has not yet diagnosed the full diamond. A cluster requires more than basic factors: it requires specialised suppliers (electrolyser components, specialised engineering), demanding buyers (industrial offtakers committed to green hydrogen procurement), related industries (renewable energy developers, chemical processing, specialised logistics), and competitive rivalry (multiple firms competing and driving innovation). If these elements are absent, the government has a project, not a cluster. The intervention should focus on creating the conditions for a cluster to emerge: specialised training programmes, applied research partnerships, regulatory frameworks that enable rather than obstruct, and incentives for multiple entrants rather than a single anchor tenant.
**Result:** The government should pursue a cluster development strategy that builds the ecosystem, not a single-project strategy that creates one facility. Multiple competing firms, a specialised skills pipeline, applied research capacity, and a network of supporting service providers are what distinguish a cluster from an isolated investment.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Declare a cluster by government fiat.
**Why:** Clusters emerge from genuine economic linkages — firms that actually buy from each other, share skilled workers, use common technologies, and serve related markets. A government declaration that "we have a biotech cluster" when there are three unrelated biotech firms with no shared suppliers, no specialised labour pool, and no supporting institutions is wishful thinking, not cluster development.

**Don't:** Focus only on the anchor firms and ignore the ecosystem.
**Why:** The competitive advantage of a cluster resides in the ecosystem — the specialised suppliers, skilled labour pool, research institutions, and information networks — not in any single firm. Investing heavily in an anchor firm while neglecting the ecosystem produces a fragile structure that depends on one company's strategic decisions rather than a self-reinforcing system.

**Don't:** Assume that cluster development means preventing competition.
**Why:** Rivalry within the cluster is one of its most important competitive assets. Attempts to coordinate firms, reduce "wasteful" competition, or consolidate the cluster around a few large players weaken the competitive pressure that drives innovation and productivity improvement. More rivalry, not less, makes clusters stronger.
