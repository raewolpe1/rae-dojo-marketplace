# Creating Shared Value

## When to Use

- When advising firms on how to connect their business strategy to social impact in ways that generate genuine competitive advantage rather than peripheral goodwill.
- When evaluating corporate social investment or CSR programmes — asking whether they create strategic value or merely charitable expenditure.
- When designing economic development programmes that rely on private sector participation — CSV provides the framework for why firms should participate.
- When a client treats social and environmental issues as costs or constraints to be managed rather than as business opportunities to be exploited.
- When connecting cluster development to community development — the third route to CSV.

## Fails When

- **You use CSV to justify any corporate activity as socially beneficial.** The framework requires that social value be genuine and measurable, not merely asserted. A mining company that claims CSV because it employs local workers while degrading the local environment is not creating shared value.
- **You ignore power asymmetries.** CSV assumes that business and community interests can be aligned. In practice, powerful firms may capture most of the value while communities receive marginal benefits. The framework must be applied with attention to who actually benefits.
- **You treat CSV as a substitute for regulation or public goods.** CSV is a business strategy, not a replacement for government's role in providing public goods, enforcing environmental standards, or protecting workers' rights. Some social problems require collective action through regulation, not voluntary corporate initiative.
- **You assume CSV eliminates all tensions between business and society.** Genuine tensions exist — between short-term profitability and long-term community health, between shareholder returns and broader stakeholder interests. CSV reduces some tensions but does not eliminate them all.

## Core Concept

The capitalist system is under siege. Business is increasingly viewed as a cause of social, environmental, and economic problems, and trust in business has declined. Companies have responded with corporate social responsibility programmes — but CSR, as typically practised, is peripheral to the business. It treats social issues as costs, constraints, or charitable obligations rather than as strategic opportunities.

Creating Shared Value offers a fundamentally different approach. CSV is defined as policies and operating practices that enhance the competitiveness of a company while simultaneously advancing the economic and social conditions in the communities in which it operates. It is not social responsibility, philanthropy, or sustainability — it is a new way to achieve economic success. The central premise is that the competitiveness of a company and the health of the communities around it are mutually dependent. Recognising and exploiting these connections between societal and economic progress has the power to unleash the next wave of global growth and to redefine capitalism.

There are three distinct routes to creating shared value, each of which reinforces the others.

**Reconceiving products and markets.** Society has enormous unmet needs — in health, housing, nutrition, financial services, environmental impact. These are not charity cases; they are markets. Companies that develop products and services that address societal needs create revenue while meeting those needs. Microfinance institutions discovered profitable markets in populations that traditional banks ignored. Companies developing nutritious, affordable food products for low-income consumers are reconceiving products and markets. The key is to identify societal needs that intersect with the company's capabilities and to develop commercially viable products or services that address those needs.

**Redefining productivity in the value chain.** Societal problems frequently create economic costs in a company's value chain. Environmental damage creates costs through energy waste, packaging waste, and logistics inefficiency. Poor employee health creates costs through absenteeism and reduced productivity. Inadequate supplier capabilities create quality and supply-chain costs. Companies that address these societal issues in their value chain reduce costs and improve quality — creating economic value through societal value. Energy efficiency reduces both environmental impact and energy costs. Investment in supplier capability in developing countries improves supply chain reliability while raising incomes in supplier communities. The insight is that what appears to be a social issue is often an economic efficiency opportunity.

**Enabling local cluster development.** No company is self-contained. Every firm operates within a cluster of related firms, suppliers, institutions, and infrastructure. The health of the local cluster directly affects the firm's productivity and innovation capacity. Companies that invest in strengthening their local clusters — through supplier development, workforce training, infrastructure improvement, and institutional support — improve their own competitive position while creating value for the broader community. This is the most distinctly Porterian route to CSV because it directly connects the cluster framework to social impact.

The critical distinction from CSR is that CSV is not about redistribution — it is about expanding the total pool of economic and social value. CSR programmes often operate at the margin of the business, funded by profits earned elsewhere. CSV is integral to the profit-generating activities of the business itself. The shared value opportunity is often largest where the societal problem most directly intersects with the company's core business.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the societal needs that intersect with your business.** Map the social and environmental issues in your communities, supply chain, and customer base. Which of these issues, if addressed, would create genuine business value — through new markets, reduced costs, improved supply chain, or strengthened cluster?

2. **Assess each of the three routes.** For each identified issue, ask: Can we reconceive products or markets to address this need profitably? Can we redesign value chain activities to reduce both social costs and business costs? Can we invest in local cluster development in ways that improve our own competitive position?

3. **Develop measurable value propositions.** CSV must be measurable — both the social value created and the economic value captured. Vague claims of "community benefit" are not CSV. Specify the social outcome (jobs created, health outcomes improved, environmental impact reduced) and the business outcome (revenue generated, costs reduced, productivity improved).

4. **Embed CSV in strategy, not in the CSR department.** CSV is a strategic function, not a philanthropic function. It should be led by business unit leaders accountable for both social and economic results, not by a CSR team operating at the margin of the business.

5. **Invest in local cluster development.** The third route is often the most overlooked and the most powerful. Identify the gaps in your local cluster — missing suppliers, inadequate skills, weak institutions — and invest in closing them. These investments improve your own productivity while creating jobs and economic opportunity in the community.

## Examples

**Situation:** A large agricultural processor in a South African province sources from hundreds of small-scale farmers. Yields are low, quality is inconsistent, and the processor faces both supply-chain risk and community criticism about low farmgate prices.
**Application:** This is a textbook CSV opportunity through the second and third routes. Redefining value chain productivity: invest in farmer training, provide access to improved inputs and agronomic advice, and introduce quality grading systems tied to price premiums. This raises farmer incomes (social value) while improving input quality and supply reliability (economic value). Enabling cluster development: invest in shared cold storage, quality testing facilities, and transport coordination that serve the entire agricultural cluster, reducing post-harvest losses for all participants while improving the processor's supply chain.
**Result:** The processor reduces supply-chain risk and improves input quality. Farmers earn higher incomes from higher yields and quality premiums. The local agricultural cluster strengthens through shared infrastructure and upgraded capabilities. Economic value and social value are created simultaneously — not through philanthropy but through a redesigned value chain.

**Situation:** A development finance institution asks whether its corporate social investment (CSI) programme — sponsoring school feeding and sports development — constitutes shared value.
**Application:** School feeding and sports development are valuable social investments, but they are CSR, not CSV. They are not connected to the institution's core business, do not improve its competitive position, and do not generate economic value for the institution beyond reputational benefit. CSV would involve the institution using its core competence — financial structuring and development finance — to address societal needs: designing affordable housing finance products for underserved markets (reconceiving products), restructuring its own procurement to support SMME suppliers (value chain productivity), or investing in financial literacy and enterprise development institutions in its key operating regions (cluster development).
**Result:** The CSI programme should continue on its own merits, but it should not be confused with CSV. Genuine shared value requires connecting social impact to the institution's core business and competitive strategy.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Relabel existing CSR as CSV without changing anything.
**Why:** CSV requires that the social activity be integral to the business strategy and generate genuine competitive advantage. Renaming the CSR budget as "shared value" without connecting the activities to core business operations is cosmetic, not strategic.

**Don't:** Assume CSV works for every social problem.
**Why:** Some social problems do not have profitable business solutions. Public health, basic education, environmental remediation of legacy damage — these often require public funding and regulation, not business models. CSV is powerful where societal needs intersect with business capabilities, but it is not a universal solution.

**Don't:** Measure only the business value and neglect the social value.
**Why:** If the social value is not genuine and measurable, the initiative is not CSV — it is simply a business investment dressed in social language. Both dimensions must be tracked and reported.
