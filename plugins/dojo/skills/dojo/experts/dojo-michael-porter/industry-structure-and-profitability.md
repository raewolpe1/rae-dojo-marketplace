# Industry Structure and Profitability

## When to Use

- When assessing whether a sector is worth entering, investing in, or providing development support to — the answer depends on structure, not on growth or technology.
- When a client asks why their sector delivers persistently poor returns despite growing demand.
- When a government agency asks which industries to prioritise and uses growth rate as the primary criterion.
- When explaining the enormous variation in profitability across industries — from consistently above-average returns in some sectors to consistently below-average in others.

## Fails When

- **You treat industry structure as immutable.** Structure can be reshaped through strategic action — mergers that reduce rivalry, innovation that creates entry barriers, vertical integration that shifts supplier power. The analysis is a snapshot, not a sentence.
- **You conclude that firms in unattractive industries should exit.** Even in structurally unattractive industries, well-positioned firms can earn above-average returns through superior positioning. The analysis sets bounds, not destiny.
- **You confuse industry analysis with firm analysis.** Industry structure determines the average profitability of the industry. Individual firm profitability depends on both industry structure and the firm's competitive position within the industry. Both matter.

## Core Concept

The average return on invested capital varies enormously from industry to industry. Some industries — such as soft drinks, pharmaceuticals, and enterprise software — have earned returns well above the cost of capital for decades. Others — such as airlines, hotels, and commodity chemicals — have earned returns at or below the cost of capital over the same period, despite growing markets and high levels of management talent.

This variation is not random, nor is it explained by industry growth, technology intensity, or regulation. It is explained by industry structure — the configuration of the five competitive forces that collectively determine how the economic value created by an industry is divided among its participants, its customers, its suppliers, potential entrants, makers of substitutes, and the firms themselves.

An industry with high barriers to entry, differentiated products, limited buyer power, few substitutes, and moderate rivalry will earn above-average returns over the long run. An industry with low entry barriers, commodity products, powerful buyers, close substitutes, and intense price competition will earn below-average returns — regardless of how fast it grows.

The critical implication is that industry selection is the first strategic decision. Before asking "how should we compete?" the strategist must ask "in which industry should we compete?" An excellent strategy in a structurally unattractive industry is constrained by the structure. A mediocre strategy in a structurally attractive industry may still generate adequate returns. The best position is a strong competitive strategy in a structurally attractive industry.

The second implication is that growth rate is a poor indicator of industry attractiveness. Fast-growing industries attract entry, which intensifies rivalry, which erodes profitability. The airline industry grew enormously for decades while destroying more shareholder value than almost any other industry. The attraction of growth must be weighed against the structural forces that determine whether growth translates into profitability.

The third implication is that structural analysis should precede strategy formulation. Understanding the forces allows the strategist to find positions where the forces are weakest, to anticipate shifts in industry structure, and to take actions that reshape the forces in the firm's favour.

## How to Apply

1. **Measure industry profitability.** Assess the average return on invested capital for the industry over a five-to-ten-year period. Short-term snapshots are misleading; the relevant question is long-run structural profitability.

2. **Diagnose the structural causes.** Using the Five Forces framework, identify which forces are strong and which are weak. The strongest forces are the structural explanation for the industry's profitability level.

3. **Separate industry effects from firm effects.** Compare the firm's profitability to the industry average. Superior performance relative to the industry average reflects competitive positioning. Below-average performance reflects weak positioning. The industry average itself reflects structure.

4. **Assess structural trends.** Is the industry becoming more or less attractive? Which forces are strengthening and which are weakening? Structural change creates both threats and opportunities.

5. **Consider structural implications for resource allocation.** When choosing where to invest, weight industry attractiveness alongside competitive position. The best investments combine attractive industry structure with strong competitive positioning.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial government wants to prioritise the retail sector for economic development support because it is the largest employer and growing rapidly.
**Application:** Structural analysis reveals that the retail sector, while large and growing, is structurally unattractive: low barriers to entry (new retailers constantly enter), intense rivalry (price competition among many competitors), powerful suppliers in some segments (branded goods manufacturers), relatively powerful buyers (price-sensitive consumers with easy switching), and significant substitution threats (e-commerce). The sector's size and growth mask its structural inability to generate above-average returns for most participants.
**Result:** Employment size and growth rate are poor criteria for prioritisation. The province should assess structural attractiveness alongside size, directing development support to sectors where structure allows firms to capture the value they create — and where the province has genuine diamond-based competitive advantages.

**Situation:** An investor evaluates a South African fintech start-up competing in the payments sector, citing the sector's rapid growth as evidence of attractiveness.
**Application:** The payments sector is growing rapidly, but structural analysis reveals intensifying rivalry (dozens of new entrants), low switching costs for merchants and consumers, growing buyer power as large retailers negotiate lower fees, and regulatory changes that reduce barriers to entry. The growth is attracting entry faster than it is creating profit pools. The structurally attractive position within the sector is platform businesses with network effects and high switching costs — but most new entrants lack these structural advantages.
**Result:** Growth alone does not make the sector attractive. The investment decision should rest on whether this specific firm can achieve a structurally advantaged position within the industry — through network effects, proprietary technology, or other entry barriers — not on the sector's overall growth rate.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Assume high-technology industries are structurally attractive.
**Why:** Technology intensity is not a structural characteristic. The personal computer industry employed cutting-edge technology and destroyed enormous amounts of investor capital. Structure — not technology — determines profitability.

**Don't:** Ignore the possibility of reshaping structure.
**Why:** Industry structure is not permanently fixed. Strategic actions — innovation that creates switching costs, consolidation that reduces rivalry, differentiation that weakens buyer power — can reshape the forces. The analysis should guide strategic action, not produce fatalism.
