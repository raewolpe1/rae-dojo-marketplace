# The Role of Government in Competitiveness

## When to Use

- When evaluating government economic development policy — asking whether government is playing the right role or overstepping.
- When a government proposes subsidies, tax incentives, or protection as the primary instruments of economic development.
- When designing government interventions in cluster development — defining what government should and should not do.
- When the debate is framed as "government intervention vs free markets" and needs to be reframed around the quality and type of intervention.
- When advising government officials on how to improve their economy's competitiveness without distorting markets.

## Fails When

- **You treat the framework as anti-government.** Porter does not argue against government involvement in the economy. He argues for a specific *type* of involvement — catalyst, challenger, business environment improver — and against a different type — director, protector, subsidiser, winner-picker. The distinction is crucial.
- **You ignore the East Asian developmental state experience.** South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, and others used more directive industrial policy than Porter prescribes, with considerable success. The framework applies most clearly to economies approaching the innovation-driven stage; earlier stages may justify more directive intervention, though even here the principles of competition and upgrading apply.
- **You assume the facilitative role is easy.** Improving the business environment — upgrading infrastructure, reforming regulation, building specialised institutions, strengthening the rule of law — is politically difficult, technically demanding, and requires sustained commitment over decades. It is the right role, but it is not the easy role.
- **You apply it without acknowledging state capacity constraints.** In countries with weak public institutions, even the facilitative role may be beyond current government capability. Building state capacity is a prerequisite for effective government in any role.

## Core Concept

Government's proper role in competitiveness is one of the most contested questions in economic policy. Porter's framework provides a specific answer: government should act as catalyst and challenger — encouraging, stimulating, and pushing companies to raise their aspirations and move to higher levels of competitive performance. Government should not act as director, protector, or subsidiser — substituting its judgement for market signals about which firms or industries should succeed.

The framework specifies what government should do across several dimensions.

**Invest in specialised factor creation.** Government should invest in the advanced, specialised factors that underpin competitive advantage: specialised education and training programmes aligned to cluster needs, applied research institutions, specialised infrastructure (testing laboratories, quality certification facilities, logistics platforms), and the institutional infrastructure that supports factor upgrading. These investments create public goods that benefit all firms in a cluster, improve the productivity frontier, and cannot typically be provided by individual firms acting alone.

**Maintain vigorous domestic competition.** Vigorous domestic rivalry is the single most powerful driver of competitive upgrading. Government should enforce competition policy, maintain open markets, resist pressure to consolidate industries or protect incumbents, and ensure that new entrants can challenge established firms. Government should resist the temptation — common in small economies — to create "national champions" by reducing competition. National champions created through protection rather than competitive success are fragile in international markets.

**Set demanding regulatory standards.** Stringent product safety, environmental, and quality standards push firms to innovate and upgrade. When standards anticipate or exceed international norms, they create first-mover advantages for domestic firms as global standards rise. Weak standards protect incumbent practices and delay the upgrading that drives competitiveness.

**Stimulate early and sophisticated demand.** Government procurement can create early demand for innovative products and services, providing domestic firms with lead-user experience that translates into competitive advantage. Government should buy on quality and innovation, not merely on lowest cost. Defence procurement drove the early development of numerous technology industries; health system procurement can drive pharmaceutical and medical device innovation.

**Remove obstacles to competition and cluster development.** Government should identify and eliminate regulatory barriers, infrastructure bottlenecks, administrative burdens, and institutional failures that suppress productivity and deter investment. This is the "business environment improvement" agenda — the most important and least glamorous element of government's role.

The framework also specifies what government should NOT do.

**Do not subsidise individual firms.** Subsidies distort competitive signals, attract footloose firms that leave when subsidies expire, protect inefficient incumbents, and divert public resources from business environment investments with broader returns. Tax incentives, grants, and preferential financing for specific firms are variants of the same error.

**Do not protect industries from competition.** Tariff protection, import quotas, and non-tariff barriers shield domestic firms from the competitive pressure that drives upgrading. Protection produces short-term comfort and long-term fragility. The most competitive industries in the world were built through rivalry, not protection.

**Do not pick winners.** Government lacks the information, incentives, and market discipline to identify which firms, technologies, or business models will succeed. Attempting to direct investment toward government-chosen winners wastes resources and creates political capture — incumbent firms lobby to maintain subsidies rather than investing in competitive improvement.

**Do not manage exchange rates for competitiveness.** Exchange rate manipulation provides temporary export cost advantages while raising import costs, reducing purchasing power, and delaying the structural upgrading that creates genuine competitiveness. Devaluation is a substitute for productivity improvement, not a complement to it.

The tension between this framework and the developmental state experience should be acknowledged. East Asian economies — particularly South Korea, Taiwan, and Singapore — used more directive industrial policy than Porter prescribes, including targeted subsidies, directed credit, and selective protection. The success of these policies depended on specific conditions: competent and insulated bureaucracies, strict performance requirements attached to support, clear sunset clauses, and the maintenance of competitive pressure through export orientation. The lesson is not that all government direction works, but that it can work under specific institutional conditions that most countries do not possess. For most countries, the facilitative model is both more realistic and less prone to capture.

## How to Apply

1. **Audit the current role.** Is government acting as catalyst and challenger, or as director and protector? Map the major economic development interventions and classify each as business environment improvement (facilitative) or firm/industry direction (interventionist).

2. **Redirect subsidies toward business environment investment.** For each subsidy, tax incentive, or firm-level support programme, ask: would the same resources be better invested in specialised education, infrastructure, research institutions, or regulatory reform that benefits all firms in the cluster? In most cases, the answer is yes.

3. **Strengthen competition policy.** Assess whether domestic competition is vigorous. Are entry barriers low? Are incumbents challenged by new entrants? Is government protecting established firms through regulation, procurement preferences, or subsidised financing? Vigorous rivalry is the most powerful driver of upgrading.

4. **Invest in specialised factors aligned to clusters.** Identify the specific skills, research capabilities, and infrastructure that the region's clusters require. Design education and training programmes, research partnerships, and infrastructure investments that address these specific needs — not generic "human capital development" or "infrastructure upgrade" programmes.

5. **Set demanding standards.** Assess whether regulatory standards — product quality, environmental performance, safety, labour conditions — are pushing firms to upgrade or protecting current practices. Standards should anticipate international trends and create incentives for innovation.

6. **Convene and facilitate, not direct.** Government's most effective role in cluster development is convening cluster participants — firms, suppliers, researchers, educators — to identify shared challenges and coordinate collective action. Government facilitates; the private sector leads.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African provincial government has allocated a significant budget to attracting foreign direct investment through a package of tax holidays, subsidised industrial land, and free infrastructure connections for qualifying investors.
**Application:** This is the subsidy model — firm-level incentives designed to attract mobile investment. The Porter framework asks: what happens when the incentives expire? If the investors came for the subsidies rather than for the quality of the business environment, they will leave. The same budget invested in specialised skills training for the province's existing clusters, upgraded logistics infrastructure, streamlined business licensing, and applied research partnerships would improve the business environment permanently, benefiting all firms — domestic and foreign — and attracting investment on the basis of genuine productivity advantages rather than temporary bribes.
**Result:** Redirect the budget from firm-level incentives to business environment investments. FDI that comes because the business environment is productive stays because the business environment remains productive. FDI that comes because of subsidies stays only until the subsidies expire or a competitor offers more.

**Situation:** A national government proposes creating a "national champion" in renewable energy by consolidating several smaller firms into one large state-supported entity with preferential procurement contracts.
**Application:** Creating a national champion through consolidation and preferential treatment reduces domestic rivalry — the most powerful driver of competitive upgrading. The champion, shielded from competition, will innovate less, invest less in capabilities, and lobby for continued protection rather than pursuing genuine competitive advantage. The alternative: maintain multiple competing firms, invest in the specialised factors they all need (renewable energy engineering programmes, grid integration research, testing and certification facilities), set demanding domestic renewable energy standards that push all firms to innovate, and let competitive rivalry determine which firms succeed internationally.
**Result:** Maintain rivalry and invest in the cluster ecosystem. The competitive firms that emerge from vigorous domestic rivalry will be far more capable international competitors than a government-created champion protected from competition.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Frame the choice as "government intervention vs free markets."
**Why:** The question is not whether government should be involved but how. Government has a vital role — investing in public goods, maintaining competition, setting standards, removing obstacles. The distinction is between facilitative involvement (improving the business environment) and directive involvement (picking winners, subsidising firms, protecting industries). Both are government intervention; only one is effective.

**Don't:** Assume that reducing government involvement always improves competitiveness.
**Why:** In many developing countries, the binding constraint is too little effective government involvement — inadequate infrastructure, weak institutions, absent public services — not too much. The prescription is not less government but better government, focused on the right role.

**Don't:** Dismiss industrial policy entirely based on this framework.
**Why:** The East Asian developmental states demonstrate that targeted industrial policy can work under specific conditions — institutional quality, performance requirements, export discipline, sunset clauses. The framework argues against the type of industrial policy most governments practise (open-ended subsidies without performance requirements) rather than against all forms of strategic government support. The key conditions are: is support tied to competitive outcomes? Is it time-limited? Does it maintain competitive pressure?
