# The Five Competitive Forces

## When to Use

- When assessing whether an industry or sector is structurally attractive — before making entry, investment, or resource allocation decisions.
- When diagnosing why an industry delivers persistently low returns despite apparent demand or growth.
- When a client or government agency asks which sectors to prioritise for economic development support.
- When evaluating the competitive dynamics facing firms in a particular sector — not just who the direct rivals are, but the full set of forces shaping profitability.
- When someone confuses a fast-growing industry with an attractive one — growth is not the same as structural attractiveness.
- When designing regulation or policy that will affect industry dynamics — understanding what the regulation changes about the forces.

## Fails When

- **The industry is genuinely nascent.** In very new industries where the product is still being defined, buyer segments are unclear, and entry barriers have not formed, the five forces are present but unstable. The analysis is still worth doing, but the results are provisional. Revisit as the industry matures.
- **The analysis becomes a checklist exercise.** Listing each force as "high/medium/low" without understanding the underlying drivers and the interactions between forces produces a superficial assessment. The value is in the diagnosis of *why* each force has its current strength and *how* it might shift.
- **You treat the forces as permanent.** Industry structure evolves. New technologies create substitutes. Regulatory changes alter entry barriers. Vertical integration shifts supplier power. The analysis is a snapshot that must be updated as the structural drivers change.
- **You confuse industry analysis with firm analysis.** The five forces diagnose the profit potential of the *industry*. A firm's profitability depends on both industry structure and the firm's own competitive position within that structure. The forces set the ceiling; the firm's strategy determines where it falls below that ceiling.

## Core Concept

The profitability of an industry is not determined by whether it produces high-technology products, whether it is growing rapidly, or whether it employs skilled workers. Profitability is determined by industry structure — the collective strength of five competitive forces that govern how the economic value created by an industry is divided among the participants.

The five forces are: (1) the threat of new entrants, (2) the bargaining power of suppliers, (3) the bargaining power of buyers, (4) the threat of substitute products or services, and (5) rivalry among existing competitors. The extended rivalry that results from all five forces defines an industry's structure and shapes the nature of competitive interaction.

**Threat of new entrants.** New entrants bring new capacity and a desire for market share, putting pressure on prices, costs, and the rate of investment needed to compete. The severity of the threat depends on the height of entry barriers — economies of scale, brand identity, capital requirements, switching costs, access to distribution channels, cost advantages independent of scale, and government policy. When barriers are high and expected retaliation from incumbents is strong, the threat of entry is low and incumbent profitability is protected.

**Bargaining power of suppliers.** Powerful suppliers capture more of the value for themselves by charging higher prices, limiting quality or services, or shifting costs to industry participants. Suppliers are powerful when they are more concentrated than the industry they sell to, when they do not depend heavily on the industry for revenue, when switching costs are high, when they offer differentiated products, when there is no substitute for what they supply, or when they can credibly threaten forward integration.

**Bargaining power of buyers.** Powerful customers can force down prices, demand better quality or more service, and play competitors against each other — all at the expense of industry profitability. Buyers are powerful when they are concentrated or purchase in large volumes, when the products they purchase are standardised, when they face few switching costs, when they earn low profits (creating pressure to reduce purchasing costs), or when they can credibly threaten backward integration.

**Threat of substitutes.** A substitute performs the same or a similar function as an industry's product by a different means. Substitutes are always present, but they are easy to overlook because they may look very different from the industry's product. The threat of substitutes is high when the substitute offers an attractive price-performance trade-off relative to the industry's product, and when buyer switching costs are low. Substitutes limit an industry's profit potential by placing a ceiling on prices.

**Rivalry among existing competitors.** Rivalry takes many forms: price discounting, new product introductions, advertising campaigns, service improvements. The intensity of rivalry is greatest when competitors are numerous or roughly equal in size and power, when industry growth is slow, when exit barriers are high, when rivals are highly committed to the business, and when firms cannot read each other's signals well. High rivalry limits industry profitability directly.

The strongest force or forces determine the profitability of the industry and become the most important to strategy formulation. The strategist's job is to understand these forces, position the firm where the forces are weakest, exploit changes in the forces, and reshape the forces in the firm's favour.

> *In essence, the job of the strategist is to understand and cope with competition. Often, however, managers define competition too narrowly, as if it occurred only among today's direct competitors.*

## How to Apply

1. **Define the industry.** Get the boundaries right. An industry is defined by the products or services that are close substitutes for each other. Too broad a definition (e.g., "financial services") obscures structural differences between segments. Too narrow a definition misses important competitive forces.

2. **Assess each force systematically.** For each of the five forces, identify the specific structural drivers and assess their current strength. Do not simply label forces as high/medium/low — explain *why* each force has its current strength and which specific drivers are responsible.

3. **Identify the strongest force or forces.** The strongest forces are the most important to strategy because they constrain profitability the most. A firm's strategy must address the strongest forces — either by finding a position that mitigates them or by reshaping them.

4. **Assess recent and likely future changes in each force.** Industry structure is not static. Identify which drivers are changing and how the changes will affect the strength of each force. This is where the analysis becomes forward-looking and strategic.

5. **Determine the implications for strategic positioning.** Given the structural forces, where can the firm position itself to earn superior returns? Which forces can be mitigated through positioning? Which can be reshaped through strategic action? This connects industry analysis to competitive strategy.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African provincial government is considering prioritising the tourism accommodation sector for economic development support, arguing that the sector is growing rapidly and employs many people.
**Application:** Five Forces analysis reveals structural problems: intense rivalry among small, undifferentiated establishments competing primarily on price; powerful intermediaries (online booking platforms) with enormous bargaining power that extract commissions and control customer relationships; low barriers to entry meaning any growth in demand is quickly met by new capacity; high threat of substitution from alternative accommodation models. The sector's rapid growth masks its structural unattractiveness — growth attracts entry, which intensifies rivalry, which suppresses margins.
**Result:** The province should not assume that sector growth equals sector attractiveness. Development support should focus on upgrading the structural position — building differentiation, developing direct booking capabilities, creating cluster-level marketing assets — rather than simply expanding capacity into a structurally unfavourable industry.

**Situation:** An economic development agency is evaluating the attractiveness of the agri-processing sector in a rural region.
**Application:** Five Forces analysis shows moderate barriers to entry (capital requirements, food safety regulation, cold chain logistics), strong bargaining power of large retail buyers (concentrated supermarket chains dictating terms), limited supplier power (many small-scale farmers competing for processing contracts), moderate substitution threats (imported processed foods), and moderate rivalry (few regional processors, growing demand). The binding constraint is buyer power — the concentrated retail sector captures most of the value chain surplus.
**Result:** Development strategy should focus on building countervailing power against buyers — through cooperative marketing arrangements, developing export channels to reduce dependence on domestic retailers, and investing in brand development that creates end-consumer pull rather than relying on retailer shelf space.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** List each force as "high/medium/low" without explaining the underlying drivers.
**Why:** The value of the framework is in the diagnosis of *why* each force has its current strength. A mechanical rating provides no insight into how the forces might change or what strategic actions might reshape them. The drivers, not the ratings, are what matter.

**Don't:** Confuse industry growth with industry attractiveness.
**Why:** Fast-growing industries attract entry, which intensifies rivalry, which can destroy profitability for all participants. The airline industry grew for decades while destroying enormous amounts of investor capital. Growth is a structural feature to be analysed, not an indicator of attractiveness.

**Don't:** Apply the framework only to existing direct competitors and ignore the other four forces.
**Why:** Managers habitually define competition as rivalry among existing firms. But the other four forces — entrants, substitutes, supplier power, and buyer power — are often more important to profitability. An industry with only two rivals can still be structurally unattractive if buyer power is overwhelming or substitution threats are severe.
