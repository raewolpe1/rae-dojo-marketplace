# Unexpected Capabilities and Value Chain Position

> **`[CHECK]` throughout.** Reconstructed from publication summaries. Verify before attribution.

## When to Use
- When a capability inventory or sector list is being built.
- When asking where the province's real capabilities are.
- When global value chain position is at issue.

## Fails When
- **The question is the general theory of capability.** Route to Sutton (revealed capability) or Hausmann (collective knowhow).

## Core Concept

**The country's real capabilities are not where the strategy documents look.**

**The standing example: mining equipment and specialist services.** South Africa developed a **technologically sophisticated and globally competitive** mining equipment and specialist services sector. `[CHECK]` It appears on almost no innovation policy sector list. It was not produced by a green paper — and Kaplan organised the first green paper on innovation policy in this country, so he had standing to say what a green paper produces.

**What produced it:** a demanding, difficult, deep-level mining industry next door for a century, asking for things that did not exist and paying for them. **A demanding customer, over a long horizon.** Note that this is Lundvall's user–producer mechanism observed in the South African record, and it converges with Sutton's finding that capability comes from somewhere specific and historical rather than from growth.

**Why sector lists miss it.** Sector lists are built out of **what people can imagine**. The capabilities a country actually has are built out of **its history**, which is a different thing and usually more interesting. Imagination selects the fashionable and the frontier; history deposits capability in odd corners, next to whatever was demanding.

**The GVC work.** Kaplan wrote on the dynamics of **global value chain engagement and industrial development in South Africa**, through case studies of **automotive** and **textiles/apparel**. `[CHECK]` Two chains, opposite trajectories — which is the point: GVC engagement is not uniformly good or bad, and the outcome depends on the terms of insertion and on what the chain permits a supplier to learn.

**The diagnostic this yields for a province:**

1. **Ask what has been demanding something here, for a long time.** That is where the capability is.
2. **Look outside the sector list.** By construction the list contains what was imagined.
3. **Trace the history.** Capability has a specific origin and someone can tell you what it was.
4. **Ask what the value chain permits.** Insertion into a chain that allows only assembly teaches nothing; insertion that demands specification teaches a great deal.

**The convergence worth noting.** Three experts arrive at the same instruction from different directions: **Kaplan** (the capability is where the demanding customer was), **Sutton** (go and record who makes what, and ask where the capability came from), **Hausmann** (read the export basket — what a place makes reveals what it knows). **None of them would build a sector list from consultation.**

## How to Apply

1. **Ask what has been demanding, here, for decades.** Follow it to the capability.
2. **Search outside the list.** The list is imagination; capability is history.
3. **Trace each capability's origin.** Somebody knows. It is rarely a policy.
4. **Assess GVC insertion by what it teaches**, not by whether it happened.
5. **Use Sutton's method** to find them, and Hausmann's to see what is adjacent.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial sector list contains agri-processing, tourism, tech, and the green economy.

**Application:** That list was imagined — it is what a room of stakeholders would produce anywhere in the world with a coastline and a wine industry. The Kaplan question is different: **what has been demanding something difficult in this province, for a long time, and paying for it?** Candidates worth investigating rather than assuming: the deciduous fruit export chain (a century of demanding European buyers with exacting specifications); the wine industry (same logic); ship repair and the fishing fleet; the deep history of the clothing sector and what survived it. **Any of those may have deposited world-class capability in a supporting industry that appears on no list** — the mining equipment pattern, locally. Find out before designing anything. This is Sutton's Enterprise Map, applied here.

**Result:** A capability inventory derived from the province's history rather than from its aspirations.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Build the sector list from what people can imagine.
**Why:** Capability comes from history. Imagination selects the frontier and the fashionable.

**Don't:** Assume GVC insertion transfers capability.
**Why:** Automotive and textiles/apparel went differently. It depends on the terms and what the chain lets you learn.

**Don't:** Expect a policy document to have produced the real capabilities.
**Why:** A demanding customer over a century produced them. He organised the first green paper and said so.
