# Evidence Base — The Start-Up Economy (Kaplan, TIPS 2024)

> **Provenance — READ.** This file is sourced **directly from the primary document**, which is now in hand: **Kaplan, D. (March 2024), "Growing the start-up economy in South Africa: Accelerating local and foreign investment", TIPS Policy Brief 1/2024.** It is the outcome of a **November 2023 TIPS workshop** of the same name that Kaplan organised, and draws on the presentations and discussion there. **Unlike the rest of this dojo, the contents below are not reconstructions.** Where a figure comes from a source Kaplan cites rather than from his own analysis, the source is named. Two figures in the brief appear garbled in extraction and are flagged.

## When to Use
- Whenever start-up, venture capital or investment attraction policy is at issue in South Africa.
- **The Trailblazers segment.** This is the file.
- When someone claims the start-up sector serves only the wealthy.

## Fails When
- **The question is the general theory of entrepreneurial finance in emerging markets.** Route to Schoar.

## THE EVIDENCE

### Why the start-up sector is unusual in South Africa
- **Source:** Kaplan (2024), Introduction.
- **Finding:** South Africa is an open economy with **strong universities and technological know-how** and a democratic society — "the key ingredients that underpin creativity and experimentation." One manifestation has been a dynamic start-up sector. Crucially: **"Start-ups are largely independent of the constraints that significantly curtail growth in other areas of the South African economy — electricity and transport. They are not significantly impacted by crime and corruption."** As a result the sector grew significantly over the past decade. **"However, the growth of start-ups in South Africa is still far from its potential."**
- **Caveat:** This is a striking and load-bearing claim: the sector sidesteps the constraints that dominate every other economic chapter you will write. It is asserted rather than demonstrated in the brief. But note what follows from it — **if load-shedding and logistics are not the constraint here, the usual provincial answers do not apply.**

### Where the sector is — the Western Cape finding
- **Source:** Kaplan (2024), citing **SAVCA, 2023 Venture Capital Industry Survey (2022 calendar year)**.
- **Finding:** **"South African VC investments are highly concentrated in the Western Cape and Gauteng. Indeed, there is very little VC activity outside of these two regions. In 2022, the Western Cape had the highest share by value (53.7%) and by number of deals (54.3%)."**

| Business location of all deals still invested, 2022 | Number of deals % | Value of deals % |
|---|---|---|
| **Western Cape** | **54.3** | **53.7** |
| Gauteng | 34.8 | 33.6 |
| KwaZulu-Natal | 2.3 | 1.5 |
| Rest of South Africa | 3.8 | 4.4 |
| Non-South African | 4.8 | 6.8 |

- **Caveat:** SAVCA data, 2022, deals **still invested** — a stock, not a flow, and self-reported by SAVCA members. It is nonetheless the cleanest available statement that **the province Rae is writing for is South Africa's leading VC destination.** Date it and attribute it to SAVCA via Kaplan.

### The slowdown
- **Source:** Kaplan (2024), citing SAVCA (2023) and Disrupt Africa (2022:11).
- **Finding:** Over the past decade the value and number of VC investments increased steadily, rising most rapidly after 2015. **But the rate of growth slowed in 2020; value declined 5.8% in 2021 and a further 4.4% in 2022.** The number of deals continued to rise but more slowly after 2019. And: **"The peak year for launches of currently active ventures was 2016, with start-ups being founded at a generally slower rate each year since 2018."**
- **Caveat — DATA FLAG.** The brief's 2012–2022 table gives deal *numbers* as 8, 19, 67, 69, 116, 147, 134, 162, 167, 186, 195 — coherent. The *value* row ends "1387, 1306, 117" for 2020–22, which cannot be right given the stated 4.4% decline from 1306. **Treat the 2022 value cell as an extraction error and do not quote it.** The percentage declines (5.8%, 4.4%) are stated in the text and are safe.

### The African reversal
- **Source:** Kaplan (2024), citing **AVCA (2022)** and NACI (2023:31–34).
- **Finding:** **"Once the leading country in Africa for investment in start-ups, South Africa has been overtaken by Nigeria"**, while Egypt and Kenya grow faster. In **2014–2020, over one-fifth (21%)** of African VC deals were in South Africa; **in 2021 SA's share fell to 17%**. In 2021 **Nigeria was top for number of deals (23%)**; Egypt 15%, Kenya 13%. **Nigeria was also top for deal value (22%); SA's share was 15%.** In H1 2022, **VC deal value in Egypt, Kenya, Nigeria and Ghana all significantly exceeded South Africa.** And the sharpest line: **"Over the past five years, Nigeria has produced five technology unicorns. South Africa has produced none."**
- **Caveat — DATA FLAG.** The brief's sentence on continental growth reads "from US$1.1 billion in 2020 to US$5.2 billion in the period 2014-2020", which is internally inconsistent; the deal-count comparison (319 in 2020 to 650 in 2021) implies the intended year is **2021**. Quote the deal counts and the shares, not the garbled value sentence.

### The exit — capital flight in a form nobody counts
- **Source:** Kaplan (2024).
- **Finding:** **"A significant number of high potential South African start-ups have left South Africa altogether — Gyft, Yola, Yoco and Clickatell, among others."** And: **"Rather than attracting foreign investment into start-ups, South Africa is experiencing capital flight as high potential start-ups relocate with consequent loss of output, jobs, and revenue."** The scale: **"In 2021, high-growth South African start-ups that are now located abroad raised over R175 billion. Only foreign investors can provide such sums. Moreover, only foreign investors can provide global market access."**
- **Caveat:** R175 billion is raised *by firms that were South African*, abroad. It is a counterfactual loss, not a measured outflow. Note the irony worth checking: **Clickatell appears as a listed partner of the 2026 SA Startup Act Movement report** — a firm Kaplan names as having left.

### The misunderstanding — what the sector actually is
- **Source:** Kaplan (2024), citing **Disrupt Africa (2022:15, :18)**.
- **Finding:** **"There is widespread misunderstanding of the start-up sector — what it does and who benefits. The popular perception is of a very few technology savvy people becoming rich through producing high technology products that meet the needs of the already wealthy... But, overall, and more particularly with South African start-ups, the picture is very different. Indeed, the start-up economy addresses all the major challenges that South Africa currently faces."**
  - **Growth:** since 2015, well over **US$1 billion** invested in start-ups. 2015: 45 start-ups raised US$50m. 2021: 89 start-ups raised over **US$330m**.
  - **Diversification:** "South Africa is far too dependent on raw material exports. Start-ups are key to diversifying the economy."
  - **Employment:** "as they expand, most start-ups provide employment to far more people who are less skilled — **490 South African start-ups... employed more than 11,000 individuals, with the average number of employees at 23 per start-up. Seventy-five percent of these employees are black.**"
  - **Transformation:** start-ups are a **"nursery"** where young people acquire first business exposure and develop deep managerial and technology skills. **29% of start-ups are led by a Black founder. Only one in seven counts a female among its founding team.**
  - **Competition:** start-ups introduce novelty and are "the seeds of new entrants."
  - **Poverty:** **"A large share of South African start-ups is directed at providing services for the poor: access to finance and insurance, employment, low-cost health and housing, affordable education, and small-scale agriculture."**
  - **Fiscal:** revenue via capital gains, employee income tax and VAT — **"the further development of the start-up economy does not require any significant government expenditure."**
- **Caveat:** The employment, founder and demographic figures are **Disrupt Africa's**, not Kaplan's, and rest on a tracked sample (490 firms), not a census. Attribute to Disrupt Africa (2022) via Kaplan. **The 75%-black-employees and 29%-Black-founder figures are the most quotable things in this brief for a transformation argument — get them from Disrupt Africa directly before they enter a DEDAT document.**

### The binding constraint
- **Source:** Kaplan (2024), "Enhancing investment."
- **Finding:** "Start-ups are embedded in an ecosystem. Inter alia an effective start-up ecosystem requires a supportive business environment, a supply of well-trained people, linkages to universities and other research centres, co-working spaces, incubators and accelerators. **While there is considerable room for improvement in all these aspects, the most significant challenge currently facing high potential start-ups in South Africa is the limited supply of venture funding. This is the binding constraint.**"
- **Caveat — THE MOST IMPORTANT ONE IN THIS FILE.** Note the scoping: **"high potential start-ups."** He is not claiming VC is the binding constraint on enterprise generally. See `evidence-base-the-2026-startup-picture.md`, where a 2026 report establishes that **VC reaches only 1–2% of new firms in the United States** — so a 98–99% non-VC rate is not a South African pathology, it is the world rate. **Kaplan's claim survives that only because of the scoping.** Reproduce the scoping or you misrepresent him.
- **Second caveat:** "binding constraint" is Rodrik's term of art (Hausmann–Rodrik–Velasco growth diagnostics), used here without the diagnostic. Schoar would ask whether the constraint is capital or entrepreneurial talent, and this brief does not test the alternative.

### Local capital — the pension fund paradox
- **Source:** Kaplan (2024), citing Sukha (2023) and Manning (2023), both TIPS workshop presentations.
- **Finding:** SA has a **large and sophisticated financial sector**, asset-to-GDP well above most emerging markets. **Domestic credit to the private sector at 129% of GDP** — against **Nigeria 12%, Egypt 27%, Kenya 33%**. **Pension funds hold more than R4.6 trillion, one of the highest assets-to-GDP ratios in the world.** Yet: **the local VC market was only 0.15% of the US$643 billion global VC market in 2021.** Pension funds have been allowed up to **15%** of assets in private equity alternatives for years; **allocations to private markets in general are below 2%, and almost none of that is VC.** By contrast **European pension funds contribute one-fifth of VC funds raised; US pension funds two-thirds.** Notably: **"In contrast with other African countries South African start-ups are largely locally funded"** — elsewhere in Africa the bulk of start-up funding is foreign.
- **The demonstration case:** the **Consolidated Retirement Fund for Local Government (CRF)** — the first SA retirement fund to commit to local VC. It seeded and anchored **three blended finance funds backed by National Treasury's Jobs Fund**, and a blended finance fund launched by the **SA SME Fund** — the first commitment by a retirement fund in the local VC space. **"CRF has outperformed the market on all indicators, providing significant returns to pensioners"**, while growing employment and contributing to a just energy transition.
- **The instrument:** **Fund of Funds** — diversified exposure across sectors, stages and managers. Some provide **concessionary/subordinated capital on a first-loss basis**, giving downside protection without capping upside. The **SA SME Fund** blends commercial and institutional capital with first-loss investors (corporates, government, US AID); its VC Fund of Funds has a first-loss provision under which commercial/institutional investors **rank senior** to first-loss investors on return of capital. **"This fund has secured capital from South African institutional investors for the first time."**
- **Caveat:** CRF is **one fund**, reported via a workshop presentation (Sukha 2023), and outperformance over a short window in an asset class with long J-curves is weak evidence. It is a demonstration of possibility, not a return distribution. Kaplan presents it as the former.

### The foreign capital blockage — the mechanics
- **Source:** Kaplan (2024), "Enhancing foreign investment."
- **Finding:** **Global VCs are very reluctant to invest directly in a South African company** — regulations are more restrictive and uncertain than in competitor environments, and **"in many instances, large global VCs located abroad are prohibited altogether from investing in a company whose HQ is in South Africa. Their mandates only allow for them to invest in the US, Europe, the United Kingdom, and a few other countries."** So SA companies must set up an **offshore holding company (OHC)** which acquires the SA start-up's shares share-for-share, creating a **loop structure**; the OHC holds the IP directly and foreign investors capitalise the OHC.
  - **Three specific frictions:**
    1. **Exchange control.** Loop structures have been permitted **since January 2021**, but **prior approval is required to raise offshore capital, and obtaining it is "very cumbersome, costly and time-consuming."** "Time is of the essence for start-ups and rather than face these delays, **many South African start-ups choose rather to relocate their entire operation abroad.**"
    2. **IP transfer.** Start-ups must apply to transfer IP to the OHC; **"current IP legislation imposes onerous and costly restrictions"**; costs and delays are prohibitive.
    3. **The CGT trap.** Forming the OHC triggers a tax event: SA resident shareholders are treated as having disposed of their shares. **Section 42 of the Income Tax Act defers CGT until actual sale — but only for transactions between South African resident companies.** So a share *swap* with a non-resident OHC creates a CGT liability **with no cash from which to pay it**: "Typically, the South African resident shareholders are cash-poor founders or early-stage investors. Moreover, the South African start-up is typically loss making, and has only 'paper value'... faced with a high tax bill, based on the aspirational value of a high-risk investment, and have no cash to pay the CGT."
  - **Kaplan's fix:** **"The application of the Tax Deferment Provisions, Section 42 of the Income Tax Act, should be extended to include reorganisations involving a non-resident company."** CGT triggered only on actual sale. "This would allow for funds to be invested in growth and consequently a higher sales price for the shares, and thus return more to the South African fiscus than would otherwise be the case."
- **Caveat:** This is the most concrete and checkable material in the dojo — and it is **legislative and regulatory, i.e. entirely national.** See "How to Apply."

### The conclusion
- **Source:** Kaplan (2024), citing Lewis (2023, *Business Day*, 21 November).
- **Finding:** **"A number of African countries have recently passed start-up Acts to create a start-up friendly environment — Tunisia, Kenya, Nigeria and Morocco."** As a result they see greater investment, particularly foreign, and faster start-up growth. **"The more rapid growth of start-ups elsewhere in Africa is the clearest indication that South Africa is performing below its potential. Given its far more developed financial sector, and hence the far greater potential availability of local funds for investment, South Africa has the potential to out-perform other African countries."** And the closing move, which is Kaplan entire: **"The changes required are costless and, as this paper has outlined, are clear and easily implementable."** Quoting Lewis: **"All that is required is a small change in mindset and a few regulatory tweaks."**
- **Caveat:** "Costless" is doing real work and is defensible on its own terms — these are regulatory changes, not expenditure. But *costless* is not *frictionless*: Section 42 sits with National Treasury and SARS, exchange control with the Reserve Bank, IP legislation with the DTIC. **The absence of a fiscal cost is not the absence of a political one.**

## How to Apply

1. **Scope the claim.** VC is the binding constraint **for high-potential start-ups**. Not for enterprise. Reproduce the qualifier every time.
2. **Use the Western Cape figure and date it.** 54.3% of deals, 53.7% of value, 2022, SAVCA via Kaplan.
3. **Recognise what a province holds here: nothing.** Exchange control (SARB), Section 42 (Treasury/SARS), IP legislation (DTIC). **Every fix Kaplan identifies is national.** This is the sharpest illustration in the roster of `what-a-provincial-department-can-do.md` — the man who was DEDAT's chief economist, writing about the sector most concentrated in the Western Cape, identifies a fix list the province cannot touch. **The provincial verb is advocate, and the indicator is influence, not delivery.**
4. **Take the misunderstanding passage for the Trailblazers narrative.** 75% of start-up employees black; 29% Black founders; a large share serving poor customers. It is the best available answer to "why should a transformation strategy care about start-ups?"
5. **Do not import "binding constraint" uncritically.** It is Rodrik's term used without Rodrik's diagnostic. Schoar's alternative — that the constraint is talent, not capital — is untested here.
6. **Get Disrupt Africa (2022) directly** before its figures enter a deliverable.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial strategy proposes a Trailblazers pillar with a provincial venture fund and start-up support programme.

**Application:** Kaplan's brief is the most authoritative local treatment and it points away from a provincial fund. His diagnosis: the binding constraint on high-potential start-ups is venture funding supply, and the causes are **regulatory** — exchange control approval delays that drive firms to relocate entirely, IP transfer restrictions, and a CGT trap that taxes a share swap with no cash to pay it. **A provincial fund addresses none of those, and at provincial scale it would be a rounding error against a R13.5bn AUM industry that is already 54% located in the Western Cape.** What the province can do: **advocate**, precisely and technically — Section 42 extension to non-resident reorganisations is a named, costless, drafted-in-one-paragraph ask; convene, since Kaplan's own method was to convene the people who knew and publish the residue; and use the misunderstanding passage to make the transformation case for a sector its own strategy under-claims.

**Result:** A Trailblazers pillar with a national advocacy ask that is specific enough to be actionable, rather than a fund that is too small to matter.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Quote "VC is the binding constraint" without "for high-potential start-ups."
**Why:** Unscoped, it is contradicted by the global 1–2% VC penetration rate. Scoped, it stands.

**Don't:** Promise provincial delivery on any fix in this brief.
**Why:** Exchange control, Section 42 and IP legislation are national. The province can advocate. Say so.

**Don't:** Quote the 2022 VC value figure or the continental five-fold sentence.
**Why:** Both are garbled in extraction. The percentage declines and the deal counts are safe.
