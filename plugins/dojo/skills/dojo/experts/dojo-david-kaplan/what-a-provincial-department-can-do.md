# What a Provincial Department Can Actually Do

## Fails When

## Core Concept

**The fact that grounds this file: David Kaplan was Chief Economist (part-time) of the Western Cape Department of Economic Development and Tourism from 2004 to 2010.** Six years. Before that, three years as the **first Chief Economist of the DTI (2000–2003)**. He knew both levels from the inside, and the difference between them.

**The honest scoping question: what does a provincial department actually hold?**

It does **not** hold: innovation policy (DSI). The science councils. The universities. Immigration. Trade policy. Labour law. Most of the regulation that binds firms. The fiscal instruments that matter. In Stam's language these are **framework conditions** and they are national.

It **does** hold — and this is the shorter, more useful list:

**1. Convening power.** The ability to get people in a room who would not otherwise meet, and to make that meeting mean something. For Hausmann's chicken-and-egg problem this is the instrument: **coordination is what a provincial department is genuinely well placed to supply**, and it costs little.

**2. Its own procurement.** The province buys things. A demanding, specifying, feedback-giving buyer is — per Lundvall — an innovation instrument, and it is one of the few the province owns outright.

**3. A modest budget.** Modest is the operative word. It funds a selective instrument for a few hundred firms, not a universal one for fifteen thousand.

**4. Knowing what is happening.** The department can be the institution that actually knows the provincial economy — its firms, its capabilities, its constraints. This is what a chief economist is *for*, and it is the cheapest thing on the list and the most neglected. Sutton's Enterprise Map is the instrument; nobody has built one here.

**The strategic implication.** A strategy built from those four things is short, unglamorous, and deliverable. A strategy built from the national policy menu is long, impressive, and mostly not the province's to promise. **Most provincial strategies are the second kind**, and the gap shows up in the implementation report five years later.

**The framework-conditions honesty.** Where the province cannot act, it can **advocate** — and advocacy commitments should carry advocacy indicators, not delivery indicators. This is the Stam framework/systemic split arriving at the same place from a different direction. **Do not promise delivery on something you can only lobby about.**

## How to Apply

1. **Sort every commitment: does the province hold this instrument?** Most of the impressive ones, no.
2. **Build from the four.** Convening, procurement, budget, knowing.
3. **Use convening for coordination problems.** It is the province's comparative advantage and it is cheap.
4. **Treat procurement as the innovation instrument** it is.
5. **Fund the knowing.** An Enterprise Map of the Western Cape is within a provincial budget and nobody has one.
6. **Separate advocacy from delivery**, with different verbs and different indicators.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial strategy commits to reforming the regulatory environment, growing the science base, and attracting skilled migrants.

**Application:** The province holds none of the three. Regulation is national and municipal; the science base is DSI's; immigration is Home Affairs'. As written these are delivery commitments the department cannot deliver, and they will appear as failures in the implementation report. Rewrite as **advocacy** commitments with influence indicators — and then spend the recovered space on what the department does hold. Convening the coordination problems in two priority value chains. Using provincial procurement as a demanding buyer. Funding the knowing: a firm-level capability map nobody else will build. **That strategy is shorter and it is the department's to keep.**

**Result:** Commitments the institution can honour, and advocacy honestly labelled.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Promise delivery on framework conditions.
**Why:** They are national. The commitment will fail and the failure was foreseeable.

**Don't:** Design a universal instrument on a modest budget.
**Why:** The budget funds selective. Universal reach and a provincial budget are incompatible.

**Don't:** Neglect the knowing.
**Why:** It is the cheapest thing the department can do, it is what a chief economist is for, and nobody has done it.
