# Resource-Based Industrialisation — One Thing Leads to Another

> **`[CHECK]` throughout.** Reconstructed from the book's title and summary material, not from reading it. **Obtain the book before relying on any claim here.**

## When to Use
- When a commodity, agricultural or resource sector is being treated strategically.
- When resource dependence is framed as a curse.
- When asking whether a primary sector can lead anywhere.

## Fails When
- **The question is macro resource curse, Dutch disease, or exchange rate management.** Different literature.

## Core Concept

**The book:** Kaplan, D., with **Mike Morris** and **Raphie Kaplinsky**, ***One Thing Leads to Another: Promoting Industrialisation by Making the Most of the Commodity Boom in Sub-Saharan Africa***.

**The title is the argument.**

**The standard view** treats commodity dependence as a curse: the resource sector crowds out manufacturing, distorts the exchange rate, invites rent-seeking, and leaves nothing behind when the boom ends. There is real evidence for this and it should not be dismissed.

**The counter-possibility:** the commodity is a **starting point** if the **linkages** around it are built.

**What a resource industry actually demands.** Equipment. Specialist services. Engineering. Logistics. Chemicals. Software. Skilled people. Maintenance. **Every one of those is a potential industry, situated right next to the resource, with a guaranteed and demanding local customer.** That is an unusually favourable position to build an industry from — far more favourable than the greenfield sector on the priority list.

**The proof case is South African.** The **mining equipment and specialist services sector** is technologically sophisticated and globally competitive, `[CHECK]` and it exists because there was a demanding, deep-level mining industry next to it for a century. **The linkage strategy is not hypothetical here. It happened.**

**The window is finite and it is set by the boom.** The argument in the book is about **making the most of the commodity boom** — building the linkages **while the demand is there**. When the boom ends, the demanding customer goes, and the industry that was going to grow next to it does not.

**The condition, which follows from the rest of Kaplan's work: linkages do not build themselves, and they do not build under a predatory state.** The industry that grows next to the resource requires a state that can **coordinate** and a private sector willing to invest against a long horizon. Where the state is **extracting** rather than coordinating, the resource is exported and nothing leads to anything. `[CHECK]` The book's optimism is conditional and the condition is the state.

**The transfer to a provincial agricultural economy.** The Western Cape's resource is agricultural, and the logic is identical: what does a demanding, exacting, century-old fruit and wine export industry require? Cold chain, packaging, plant material, irrigation technology, logistics, testing, compliance services, equipment. **Ask which of those are made here, which are imported, and which could be made here.** That is the linkage question, and it is a better-founded sector list than the imagined one.

## How to Apply

1. **List what the resource industry demands.** Equipment, services, engineering, logistics, inputs. Each is a candidate industry.
2. **Ask which are imported.** Those are the linkage opportunities.
3. **Check the window.** Is the demand there now? Linkages build while the customer is demanding.
4. **Name the coordinator.** Linkages do not self-assemble.
5. **Apply it to agriculture.** Same logic, different resource.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial strategy treats agriculture as a mature primary sector and looks elsewhere for industrial growth.

**Application:** That is the resource-curse framing applied to the province's most demanding customer. Run the linkage question instead: a century-old deciduous fruit and wine export industry, selling into the most exacting markets in the world, requires cold chain equipment, controlled-atmosphere technology, packaging, plant material, irrigation systems, testing and certification services, specialised logistics, and processing equipment. **Which of those are made here?** Each import is a linkage opportunity with a guaranteed, demanding, local customer — which is the most favourable condition for building an industry that exists, and considerably more favourable than any greenfield sector on the list. This is the mining equipment pattern, and the mining equipment sector is the proof that it works in this country.

**Result:** An industrial strategy derived from the province's most demanding existing customer.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the resource as a curse and look elsewhere.
**Why:** It is a demanding customer with a guaranteed local presence. That is an asset.

**Don't:** Assume the linkages build themselves.
**Why:** They need a coordinator, and they do not build under a state that extracts.

**Don't:** Miss the window.
**Why:** The linkages build while the demand is there. When the boom ends, so does the opportunity.
