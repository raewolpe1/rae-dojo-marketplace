# Fanon and Development

## When to Use
- When analysing the structural relationship between the development industry and the colonial history from which it emerged.
- When the colonial dynamics within development practice — metropolitan design, local implementation, external evaluation — need to be named and confronted.
- When practitioners need to understand how their own practice may reproduce what it claims to challenge.

## Core Concept
Fanon did not write about "development" as it is understood today — the term had not yet acquired its current institutional meaning. But his analysis of colonialism and neocolonialism provides the sharpest available framework for understanding development's relationship to colonial power.

The development industry emerged from the colonial apparatus. The same metropolitan powers that colonised now "develop." The same institutions that administered colonies now administer aid. The same epistemological hierarchy that positioned Western knowledge as universal and indigenous knowledge as local now structures evidence-based policymaking. The relationship has changed form — from direct rule to technical assistance, from colonial administration to programme management — but the structure persists: the metropolitan centre defines the problem, designs the solution, funds the intervention, and evaluates the results, while the peripheral population receives the assistance and is measured against metropolitan standards.

This does not mean all development is neocolonial. It means that development operates within a structure inherited from colonialism, and that practitioners who do not confront this structure reproduce it. The consultant who arrives with a methodology designed in Washington to assess a programme designed in London for implementation in a community that was never consulted — this consultant is performing a neocolonial function, however progressive their personal politics.

Fanon's framework demands that development practitioners confront three questions. First: whose categories structure this intervention? If the answer is "the donor's" or "the international community's," the colonial relation is operating. Second: whose knowledge counts? If the answer is "peer-reviewed evidence from Northern universities," epistemic colonialism is operating. Third: who benefits? If the answer is "the development industry" (through contracts, publications, institutional growth), the colonial extractive relation is operating — extracting data and legitimacy rather than resources, but extracting nonetheless.

## How to Apply
1. **Trace the colonial genealogy.** "Where did this programme design originate? What institutions produced the methodology? What knowledge tradition informs the theory of change? If the genealogy leads to the former colonial centres, the colonial structure is operating — not as conspiracy but as structural inheritance."
2. **Apply the three questions.** "Whose categories? Whose knowledge? Who benefits? If the answers consistently point to the metropolitan centre, the programme reproduces the colonial relation regardless of its stated objectives."
3. **Assess genuine transformation.** "Does this programme change the structure of the relationship between the metropolitan centre and the peripheral community? Or does it reproduce it — providing assistance within the existing structure while leaving the structure intact?"
4. **Design for decolonisation.** "Transfer design authority to national and local actors. Fund indigenous knowledge production rather than importing metropolitan knowledge. Evaluate using criteria defined by the community, not the donor. These are not cosmetic adjustments — they are structural interventions in the colonial relation."

## Examples
**Situation:** A bilateral donor funds a ten-year governance and accountability programme in a former colony. The programme is designed by a Northern consulting firm, implemented through an international NGO, and evaluated by a Northern university. National organisations serve as "implementing partners." The programme's theory of change posits that strengthening formal institutions and increasing citizen engagement will improve governance outcomes.
**Application:** "Map the colonial structure. The programme is conceived in the metropole, funded from the metropole, designed by metropolitan consultants, implemented through a metropolitan NGO, and evaluated by a metropolitan university. National organisations occupy the position of implementing partners — the intermediary class that executes metropolitan designs in the national context. The community — the supposed beneficiary — is positioned as the object of governance improvement: passive recipients of institutional strengthening designed elsewhere. The theory of change assumes that the metropolitan institutional model (formal institutions, citizen engagement, accountability mechanisms) is the correct model of governance. The community's own governance practices — which may be effective, legitimate, and historically tested — are not part of the theory of change because the metropolitan designers did not ask about them. This is the colonial relation reproduced as programme design: the metropole knows what good governance looks like; the colony needs to be taught. The national organisations are not partners — they are instruments. The community is not a beneficiary — it is an object. To decolonise this programme: transfer design authority to national actors who understand the country's governance realities. Fund national research institutions to study governance, not Northern universities. Define governance quality using criteria developed by the community, not by the donor. And confront the fundamental question: is this programme strengthening governance, or is it extending metropolitan institutional models into a context that has its own governance traditions?"

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce all development to neocolonialism.
**Why:** Development programmes have saved lives, reduced poverty, and expanded opportunity. The colonial analysis identifies the structural relationship within which this happens — it does not deny that useful work occurs within the structure.

**Don't:** Offer only critique without constructive alternatives.
**Why:** Practitioners who take decolonisation seriously need guidance on what to do differently, not only what to stop doing. The analysis must produce actionable alternatives.
