# Black Skin, White Masks

## When to Use
- When the colonised — or those positioned as colonised in contemporary structures — are experiencing themselves through the coloniser's categories: judging their own worth by the coloniser's standards, devaluing their own knowledge, culture, and being.
- When diagnosing the psychological mechanisms through which colonial racism operates on the consciousness of those it dominates.
- When the desire for recognition by the metropolitan centre — the donor, the international community, the former colonial power — is driving professional practice.

## Core Concept
*Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) is Fanon's phenomenological and psychoanalytic analysis of the psychological effects of colonialism on the colonised. Its central argument is that colonialism produces a specific form of psychic damage: the colonised person is forced to experience themselves through the coloniser's gaze, adopting the coloniser's categories of value and finding themselves wanting.

The colonised person confronts an impossible situation. The colonial world is structured by a racial hierarchy that places whiteness at the top and blackness at the bottom. The colonised person internalises this hierarchy — desiring whiteness, devaluing blackness, experiencing their own skin as a prison. The "white mask" is the attempt to be recognised by the coloniser on the coloniser's terms: to speak the coloniser's language perfectly, to adopt the coloniser's culture, to demonstrate one's capacity to be "civilised." But the mask never fits — the coloniser's gaze always returns the colonised person to their skin, to their race, to their position in the colonial hierarchy.

The mechanism is the gaze. The colonised person is "fixed" by the coloniser's look — reduced to a body, a colour, a category. Fanon describes the experience of being "looked at" by whiteness: the sudden awareness that one is Black, that one is an object rather than a subject, that one's humanity is conditional on the coloniser's recognition. This is not merely a psychological experience — it is an ontological condition. Colonialism determines who is fully human and who is not.

For development practice, this analysis illuminates the neocolonial dynamics that persist within the development industry. The national professional who adopts the donor's methodology, the government official who speaks the donor's language, the community leader who frames their community's needs in the donor's categories — all may be wearing white masks. This is not hypocrisy — it is the rational response to a field structured by colonial power, in which recognition, resources, and authority flow from the metropolitan centre.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the mask.** "Where are national professionals, government officials, or community leaders adopting the categories of the international development apparatus in order to be recognised? Is this genuine adoption or strategic performance? Either way, what does it reveal about who holds the power to define legitimate practice?"
2. **Trace the desire for recognition.** "Whose recognition is being sought? The donor's? The international community's? The former colonial power's? The desire for metropolitan recognition is a diagnostic of colonial power — it reveals who holds the authority to validate."
3. **Name the impossible situation.** "The national professional who adopts the donor's framework is rationally navigating a field structured by colonial power. The professional who refuses the donor's framework is marginalised. Both responses are produced by the same colonial structure. Do not blame the individual for navigating a structure they did not create."
4. **Attend to the psychic costs.** "What is the psychological cost of wearing the mask? The national evaluator who writes in the donor's language, uses the donor's methodology, and produces findings in the donor's categories may be professionally successful. But at what cost to their own intellectual autonomy, their own ways of knowing, their own relationship to the communities they evaluate?"

## Examples
**Situation:** A national evaluation association in an African country has adopted international evaluation standards wholesale — the methodology guidelines, the ethical protocols, the competency frameworks — all designed by Northern professional associations. Members of the association express pride in meeting "international standards." A junior member privately wonders whether the association's own evaluative traditions and practices are being erased.
**Application:** "The association's adoption of international standards is the white mask at the institutional level. The 'international standards' are the standards of the former colonial centres — North American and European evaluation societies that codified their own practices and universalised them as 'international.' The African association's adoption of these standards is rational — it provides legitimacy, recognition, and access to international networks and funding. But attend to what is erased. The evaluative practices that existed before the international standards arrived — community-based accountability mechanisms, oral assessment traditions, elder-mediated judgment — are not recognised by the 'international' framework because the framework was not designed to see them. The association has achieved recognition by adopting the coloniser's standards. The junior member's discomfort names what the recognition costs: the suppression of indigenous evaluative knowledge in exchange for metropolitan validation. This is not a failure of the association — it is the structural logic of a field in which the power to define 'good evaluation' resides in the former colonial centres. The remedy is not to reject international standards wholesale but to refuse the premise that standards must be adopted rather than co-created, that legitimacy flows only from the centre, and that the association's own traditions are supplementary rather than foundational."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Blame the colonised for wearing the mask.
**Why:** The mask is a rational response to a structure of domination. The national professional who adopts the donor's framework does so because the field rewards it. The problem is the field's structure, not the individual's strategy.

**Don't:** Treat this analysis as historically confined.
**Why:** Formal colonialism has ended, but the psychic structures it produced persist — in development practice, in professional standards, in the desire for metropolitan recognition. Fanon's analysis is contemporary, not archival.
