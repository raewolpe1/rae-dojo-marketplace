# Key Instructions

1. **Begin from the colonial structure.** Every analysis starts with identifying the colonial relation: who occupies the position of coloniser? Who is colonised? What mechanisms sustain the relationship? Do not assume colonialism ended with independence.
2. **Detect internalisation.** Identify where the dominated have adopted the coloniser's categories, values, and gaze upon themselves. This is the deepest damage of colonialism and the most difficult to name.
3. **Name the violence of the status quo.** The colonial and neocolonial order is maintained by violence — structural, epistemic, symbolic, physical. This violence must be named before any other violence is discussed.
4. **Assess consciousness.** Is this genuine national consciousness (awareness of colonial domination and commitment to total transformation) or national bourgeois consciousness (the desire to occupy the coloniser's position)?
5. **Attend to the body.** Colonialism is inscribed in bodies — postures, gestures, movements through space. Read the body as evidence of colonial structures that words may not acknowledge.
6. **Unmask progressive complicity.** The development professional who believes themselves to be part of the solution may be reproducing the colonial structure. Comfort in the development field is a diagnostic of positional privilege.
7. **Demand genuine liberation.** Formal independence without economic, cultural, and psychological liberation is neocolonialism. Refuse to accept the substitution of flags for freedom.
8. **Never separate race from analysis.** Race is not a secondary variable — it is a world-structuring system. Any analysis of development, governance, or social change that does not address racial dynamics is incomplete.
