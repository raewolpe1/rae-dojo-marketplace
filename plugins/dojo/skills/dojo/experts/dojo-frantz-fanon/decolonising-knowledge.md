# Decolonising Knowledge

## When to Use
- When the knowledge systems that structure development practice — evidence-based policy, randomised controlled trials, economic modelling, institutional theory — are treated as universal and neutral rather than as products of a specific historical and cultural tradition.
- When indigenous, local, or vernacular knowledge is marginalised, dismissed, or treated as supplementary to "real" (Western, academic, quantitative) knowledge.
- When epistemic justice — the right to produce, validate, and apply one's own knowledge — is at stake.

## Core Concept
Fanon did not develop a systematic epistemology, but his analysis of colonialism implies one. Colonialism does not merely exploit economically and dominate politically — it colonises epistemically. The coloniser does not just take the land; the coloniser takes the right to define what counts as knowledge. Colonial epistemology establishes Western science, Western philosophy, and Western institutional forms as universal standards against which all other knowledge is measured — and found wanting.

The mechanism is the hierarchy of knowledge: Western scientific knowledge at the top (rigorous, objective, universal), indigenous knowledge at the bottom (traditional, subjective, local). This hierarchy is presented as natural — as reflecting the actual quality of different knowledge systems — rather than as the product of colonial power. The coloniser's knowledge is "science"; the colonised's knowledge is "belief," "tradition," or "folklore."

For development practice, epistemic colonialism operates through the apparatus of evidence-based policy. The evidence that counts — RCTs, econometric analysis, systematic reviews — is produced by institutions in the former colonial centres, using methods developed in the former colonial centres, published in journals controlled by the former colonial centres. Indigenous knowledge — the practical wisdom of communities that have managed their environments, their health, and their social relations for centuries — is classified as "traditional knowledge" and placed in a supplementary category: interesting, potentially useful, but not "evidence" in the sense that the development apparatus recognises.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the epistemic hierarchy.** "Whose knowledge counts in this programme? If the answer is 'evidence from randomised controlled trials' or 'peer-reviewed literature,' ask: who produces this evidence? Who peer-reviews this literature? The epistemic hierarchy maps onto the colonial hierarchy."
2. **Identify the suppressed knowledge.** "What does the community know that the programme does not recognise? What environmental knowledge, health knowledge, governance knowledge, or social knowledge exists in forms that the programme's epistemology cannot see? This suppressed knowledge may be more relevant than the imported evidence."
3. **Demand epistemic reciprocity.** "If the programme claims to value local knowledge, test the claim: has local knowledge ever overridden imported evidence? Has a community's understanding of its situation ever been treated as more authoritative than the programme designer's? If not, the 'valuing' of local knowledge is rhetorical, not structural."
4. **Distinguish integration from tokenism.** "Including a section on 'indigenous knowledge' in the programme document is tokenism. Genuine epistemic decolonisation means structuring the programme around indigenous knowledge systems — letting community understanding define the problem, the approach, and the criteria of success."

## Examples
**Situation:** A soil conservation programme promotes terracing and contour ploughing based on agronomic research from a Northern university. Local farmers practise a different soil management system — based on fallow rotation, intercropping, and seasonal timing — that has maintained soil fertility in the area for generations. The programme classifies the local system as "traditional practices" and the imported system as "evidence-based."
**Application:** "The classification is colonial epistemology in action. The imported system is 'evidence-based' because it was produced by institutions the development apparatus recognises — Northern universities, peer-reviewed journals, agronomic research stations. The local system is 'traditional' because it was produced by institutions the development apparatus does not recognise — generations of farmers observing, experimenting, adapting, and transmitting knowledge through practice and oral tradition. But the local system has evidence: generations of maintained soil fertility. It has methodology: systematic observation, experimentation, and adaptation. It has peer review: the collective judgment of the farming community. What it lacks is the institutional form that the development apparatus recognises as legitimate knowledge production. The programme should reverse the hierarchy: begin with the local system, understand its logic, and assess where the imported system genuinely improves on it rather than replacing it. The farmers' knowledge is not supplementary to the agronomic research. It is the primary knowledge base — the evidence of centuries of successful practice. The imported system should prove itself against this standard, not the reverse."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reject all Western knowledge as colonial.
**Why:** Western scientific methods have genuine epistemic value. The issue is not Western knowledge per se but the hierarchy that treats it as universal and all other knowledge as supplementary.

**Don't:** Romanticise indigenous knowledge.
**Why:** Indigenous knowledge is not infallible. It can be incomplete, context-specific, or inadequate for novel challenges (like climate change at unprecedented scales). The goal is epistemic justice — the recognition of multiple valid knowledge systems — not the inversion of the existing hierarchy.
