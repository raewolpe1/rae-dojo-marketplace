# Fanon Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When the practitioner wants to move from understanding colonial dynamics to confronting them in their own work.
- When translating Fanon's analysis into daily professional practices and structural interventions.
- When the development professional needs to confront their own position within the colonial structure.

## Core Concept
Applying Fanon to practice is uncomfortable by design. Fanon does not offer tools for incremental improvement — he demands confrontation with structures that the practitioner benefits from. The white consultant who applies Fanon must confront the fact that their expertise, their mobility, their authority, and their salary are products of the same colonial structure that Fanon analysed. The national professional who applies Fanon must confront the ways in which their own career has been shaped by the colonial hierarchy — the metropolitan education, the English fluency, the institutional capital that positions them above the community they serve.

Five practices emerge from Fanon's work:

First, the colonial audit. Before any professional engagement, ask: what colonial structure is operating here? Who occupies the coloniser's position? Who is colonised? What mechanisms sustain the relationship? This is not an academic exercise — it is a diagnostic of the power relations within which the professional engagement occurs.

Second, the complicity examination. What is your position in the colonial structure? What forms of colonial capital do you hold — metropolitan education, donor networks, English fluency, institutional affiliation? How does your position benefit from the structures that Fanon diagnosed? This is not confession — it is strategic analysis of one's own location.

Third, the inversion practice. In any professional situation, reverse the colonial relation experimentally. What if the community designed the programme? What if national professionals led the evaluation? What if the donor reported to the community rather than the community reporting to the donor? The inversion reveals the colonial assumptions embedded in the current arrangement.

Fourth, the language discipline. Notice which language dominates. Whose concepts structure the analysis? Whose vocabulary defines the problem? When you use terms like "capacity building," "beneficiary," or "evidence-based," recognise these as the vocabulary of a specific institutional tradition — not as neutral professional language.

Fifth, the body check. In any professional encounter, read the bodies. Whose body is at ease? Whose is tense? Who occupies space freely and who contracts? The bodies tell the truth about power relations that the words may disguise.

## How to Apply
1. **Begin with the colonial audit.** "Before the programme design begins, before the evaluation is commissioned, before the strategy is developed — map the colonial structure. This takes one hour and transforms every subsequent decision."
2. **Practise the inversion.** "Once per quarter, take a programme design or evaluation plan and systematically reverse the colonial relation: community as designer, national professionals as leads, metropolitan professionals as support. Compare the inverted design with the actual design. The differences reveal the colonial assumptions."
3. **Maintain a complicity journal.** "Weekly, note one instance in which your professional practice reproduced the colonial structure. Not as self-flagellation but as diagnostic: how does the structure operate through you? What would you need to change — not just in yourself but in the structure — to disrupt it?"
4. **Read the bodies.** "In every meeting, attend to the corporeal evidence. If the bodies confirm the colonial hierarchy — white professionals at ease, national professionals measured, community members contracted — the meeting's structure is colonial regardless of its content."

## Examples
**Situation:** A development consultant has read Fanon and is convinced of the analysis but does not know what to do differently. She continues to accept consultancy assignments, apply metropolitan methodologies, and write reports for donors — while feeling increasingly uncomfortable about the colonial structure she now recognises.
**Application:** "Discomfort is the beginning, not the end. Fanon does not demand that you leave the field — he demands that you confront the structure within which you operate. Four actions, in order of difficulty. First, the colonial audit: in your next assignment, spend the first day mapping the colonial structure of the engagement. Write it down. Share it with the client. The act of making the structure visible is itself disruptive. Second, the knowledge inversion: in your next evaluation, begin not with your methodology but with the community's own assessment of the programme. Let their categories define the evaluation questions. If the donor's terms of reference conflict with the community's assessment, note the conflict in the report — this is evidence of the colonial structure, not a methodological problem to be resolved. Third, the structural intervention: when you write the next proposal, propose that national consultants lead and you support. Propose that the report be written first in the national language. Propose that the evaluation criteria be defined by the community. These proposals will probably be rejected — but the act of proposing them makes the colonial assumptions visible. Fourth, the long game: build relationships with national institutions that can replace you. Transfer the methodology, the networks, the institutional knowledge. The goal is to make your own position unnecessary — which is the structural intervention Fanon demands."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat discomfort as sufficient.
**Why:** Feeling bad about your position in the colonial structure does not change the structure. Fanon demands structural transformation, not emotional response.

**Don't:** Reduce decolonial practice to a checklist.
**Why:** Decolonisation is not a procedure — it is a continuous confrontation with structures of domination. A checklist domesticates what should remain unsettling.
