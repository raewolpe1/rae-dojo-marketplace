# Language and Colonialism

## When to Use
- When the language in which development operates — English, French, Portuguese — is the colonial language, and this fact structures who can participate, whose knowledge counts, and whose voice is heard.
- When analysing how the requirement to operate in the colonial language excludes, marginalises, or alienates those the programme is supposed to serve.
- When the psychic costs of linguistic colonialism — thinking in a language that was imposed — need to be named.

## Core Concept
In the opening chapter of *Black Skin, White Masks*, Fanon argues that to speak a language is to assume a world. The colonised person who speaks the coloniser's language does not merely communicate — she enters the coloniser's conceptual universe, adopts the coloniser's categories, and experiences herself through the coloniser's linguistic framework. The Antillean who speaks perfect French "becomes white" in a specific sense: she is recognised by the colonial system as having achieved a level of civilisation that the coloniser's language represents. But this recognition is conditional and always partial — her skin remains, and the coloniser's recognition is always tinged with surprise.

Language, for Fanon, is not a neutral medium of communication. It is a system of power that determines who can think, who can speak legitimately, who can be heard, and whose knowledge counts. The colonial language is the language of power, administration, education, and modernity. Indigenous languages are relegated to the domains of the private, the traditional, the oral, and the pre-modern. The hierarchy of languages reproduces the hierarchy of races: the colonial language is to indigenous languages as the coloniser is to the colonised.

For development practice, the politics of language is everywhere and almost always unnamed. International development operates in English (or French, or Portuguese). Programme documents, evaluation reports, theories of change, and results frameworks are written in the colonial language. Meetings, workshops, and consultations are conducted in the colonial language — with translation provided as a concession rather than as a structural acknowledgment of linguistic diversity. The professionals who succeed in the development field are those who command the colonial language. Those who do not — the majority of the populations development claims to serve — are linguistically excluded from the apparatus that governs their lives.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the linguistic hierarchy.** "This programme operates in English. The communities it serves speak local languages. The hierarchy is clear: English is the language of power, planning, and reporting; local languages are the languages of 'the field,' 'beneficiaries,' and 'local context.' This hierarchy is colonial."
2. **Assess linguistic access.** "Who can participate fully in this process? Only those who command the colonial language. Everyone else participates through the mediation of translation — which is itself an act of power, because the translator determines what is said and how."
3. **Attend to what is lost in translation.** "Indigenous concepts, categories, and ways of knowing may have no equivalent in the colonial language. When the community's understanding is translated into the programme's language, something essential may be lost — not through bad translation but through the structural inadequacy of the colonial language to capture indigenous knowledge."
4. **Design for linguistic justice.** "Produce programme documents in local languages — not as translations of the English original but as documents conceived and written in the local language. Conduct evaluation in local languages with local evaluators. Recognise that linguistic justice is not a communication issue but a power issue."

## Examples
**Situation:** A regional climate adaptation programme produces all documents in English. National staff translate key documents into local languages for community use. The English versions are authoritative; the local language versions are derivative. Community feedback is collected in local languages, translated into English, and summarised in English-language reports. The communities never see the reports.
**Application:** "The programme's linguistic structure reproduces the colonial hierarchy. English is sovereign: it is the language of the original document, the authoritative version, the reporting language, the language in which decisions are made. Local languages are subordinate: they carry the English content downward to communities, and they carry community feedback upward — but in both directions, the translation is controlled by the programme, and the English version is definitive. The communities' climate knowledge — accumulated over generations, expressed in local languages, embedded in local concepts — enters the programme only after being translated into English categories. What cannot be translated is lost. The community's understanding of seasonal patterns, ecological relationships, and adaptation strategies may be more sophisticated than the programme's — but it is expressed in a language the programme does not speak, in categories the programme does not recognise, and in a register the programme does not value. Linguistic justice would require: programme documents conceived in local languages, not translated from English; evaluation conducted in local languages by evaluators who think in those languages; and the recognition that the English version is the translation — the local language version is the original."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss all use of colonial languages as complicity.
**Why:** In multilingual post-colonial states, the colonial language often serves as a practical lingua franca. The issue is not the use of English per se but the hierarchy that makes English sovereign and other languages subordinate.

**Don't:** Romanticise indigenous languages.
**Why:** Indigenous languages have their own internal politics — gendered registers, class-based variations, and power dynamics. Linguistic decolonisation is not simply the substitution of one language for another but the restructuring of who has the power to define what counts as legitimate speech.
