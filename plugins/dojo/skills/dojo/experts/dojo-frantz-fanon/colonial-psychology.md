# Colonial Psychology

## When to Use
- When psychological symptoms — passivity, aggression, self-destructive behaviour, dependency — are being attributed to individual pathology rather than to the structural violence of the colonial or neocolonial situation.
- When analysing how systems of domination produce specific psychological effects in those they dominate.
- When Fanon's psychiatric training illuminates the connection between political structures and psychic life.

## Core Concept
Fanon was a psychiatrist before he was a revolutionary, and his clinical practice at Blida-Joinville Hospital in Algeria gave him direct evidence that the psychiatric symptoms of his patients — both colonised Algerians and French colonisers — were produced by the colonial situation itself. The colonised patient's depression, anxiety, and self-destructive behaviour were not individual pathologies but rational responses to an irrational system. The coloniser's cruelty, numbness, and dehumanisation were not individual moral failures but the psychological effects of occupying the dominant position in a violent system.

This insight — that individual psychology is produced by political structures — is Fanon's most distinctive contribution. Colonialism does not merely exploit the colonised economically or dominate them politically. It damages them psychologically — producing a fractured consciousness, a devalued self-image, and a set of behavioural adaptations (passivity, mimicry, displaced aggression) that are intelligible only when the colonial structure that produces them is understood.

The clinical implications are profound: you cannot treat the individual without addressing the structure. The colonised patient's depression is not cured by medication or therapy — it is cured by liberation. This does not mean individual suffering is irrelevant. It means that individual suffering has a political aetiology, and effective treatment must address both the symptom and its structural cause.

For development practice, colonial psychology illuminates why communities that have been subjected to long-term structural domination — through colonialism, apartheid, or ongoing neocolonial arrangements — exhibit patterns of behaviour that development programmes often misidentify as cultural traits, capacity deficits, or attitudinal problems. Passivity in the face of institutional processes is not a cultural trait — it is a learned response to a system that punishes initiative. Dependency on external assistance is not a character flaw — it is a rational adaptation to a system in which survival depends on the patron's goodwill.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose the structure, not the individual.** "Before attributing passivity, dependency, or self-destructive behaviour to individual or cultural pathology, ask: what political and economic structure produced these behaviours? The behaviour is a symptom — the structure is the disease."
2. **Trace the adaptive logic.** "Every behaviour that appears dysfunctional from the outside may be perfectly rational from within the dominated position. Deference, passivity, and mimicry are survival strategies in a system that punishes assertion."
3. **Refuse to pathologise the colonised.** "When community members do not 'participate' in programme activities, do not diagnose 'low motivation.' Diagnose the power structure that makes participation irrational, risky, or meaningless for those in subordinate positions."
4. **Connect treatment to transformation.** "Individual interventions — training, mentoring, psychosocial support — address symptoms. Structural interventions — redistribution of power, institutional reform, decolonisation of knowledge — address causes. Both are necessary; neither is sufficient alone."

## Examples
**Situation:** A community development programme in a former settler colony notes that community members are reluctant to make decisions in programme meetings, deferring consistently to external facilitators and programme staff. The programme diagnoses "low self-confidence" and designs a self-esteem workshop.
**Application:** "The community's deference is not low self-confidence. It is the habitus of the colonised — a set of dispositions formed through generations of a system in which initiative by the colonised was punished and deference was rewarded. The settler colonial system taught this community, through sustained violence and dispossession, that decisions are made by those who hold power, and that the appropriate response of the powerless is compliance. The programme's meeting — with its external facilitators, its imported agenda, its unfamiliar procedures — reproduces the colonial structure: outsiders arriving with a plan and asking for the community's 'participation.' The community's deference is not a deficit to be remedied by a self-esteem workshop. It is a rational reading of the power dynamics: the programme controls the resources, the facilitators control the agenda, and the community has learned that its role in such encounters is to agree. The remedy is not psychological intervention but structural redistribution: transfer decision-making authority to the community, place resources under community control, and — most importantly — acknowledge that the community's deference is a response to the programme's own power, not a pre-existing condition to be treated."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Prescribe individual therapy for collective domination.
**Why:** The colonised patient's depression has a political aetiology. Individual therapy without structural change treats the symptom while perpetuating the cause.

**Don't:** Pathologise the colonised.
**Why:** The behaviours produced by colonial domination — passivity, mimicry, displaced aggression — are rational adaptations to an irrational system. Diagnosing them as individual pathology is a form of symbolic violence that locates the problem in the dominated rather than in the system of domination.
