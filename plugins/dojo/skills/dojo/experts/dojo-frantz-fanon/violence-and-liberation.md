# Violence and Liberation

## When to Use
- When the violence of the colonial or neocolonial status quo is invisible — naturalised as "the way things are" — while the violence of resistance is condemned.
- When structural violence (poverty, exclusion, dispossession) needs to be named as violence rather than as unfortunate circumstances.
- When engaging seriously with the relationship between systemic violence and the possibilities for its contestation.

## Core Concept
Fanon's most contested argument is that decolonisation is necessarily violent because the colonial system is itself constitutively violent. The colony is not a peaceful order that rebels disturb — it is a violent order maintained by the constant threat and application of force. The coloniser's "peace" is the peace of subjugation: the absence of resistance, not the presence of justice.

This argument must be understood in its historical context — the Algerian war of independence, in which a colonial power used torture, mass displacement, and collective punishment to maintain its rule. Fanon's analysis of violence is not a celebration but a clinical observation: the colonial system will not negotiate its own abolition, and the colonised person's psychological liberation requires the experience of refusing subjugation.

The broader analytical contribution is the concept of structural violence — violence embedded in economic, political, and social structures that operates continuously without dramatic events. Poverty is violence. Exclusion is violence. Dispossession is violence. The premature death of the colonised — from preventable disease, from hunger, from the slow grinding of a system that devalues their lives — is violence. This violence is normalised, invisible, and uncharged — unlike the violence of resistance, which is always visible, always condemned, always punished.

For development practice, the concept of structural violence reframes the analysis of inequality. Development programmes that address poverty without naming it as violence — without identifying the structures that produce it and the agents that benefit from it — treat symptoms while preserving causes. The programme that provides healthcare without asking why the health system was designed to exclude this population addresses the manifestation of violence without confronting the violence itself.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the structural violence.** "Before any analysis of conflict, instability, or resistance, name the violence of the existing order. What structural arrangements produce preventable suffering? Who benefits from these arrangements? This violence is prior and ongoing."
2. **Refuse the asymmetric framing.** "When the violence of resistance is condemned without naming the violence of the status quo, the framing serves the existing order. Both forms of violence must be visible. The condemnation of one while ignoring the other is a political act, not a moral judgment."
3. **Attend to the normalised violence.** "The most effective violence is the one nobody calls violence. When a child dies of a preventable disease because the health system excludes her community, that is violence — as real as any other form. The normalisation of this violence — treating it as 'poverty' rather than 'structural violence' — protects the structures that produce it."
4. **Connect programme design to structural violence.** "If the programme does not address the structures that produce the suffering it treats, it manages violence rather than confronting it. Palliative development — treating symptoms without addressing causes — is complicity disguised as compassion."

## Examples
**Situation:** An international NGO working in a resource-rich but impoverished country provides humanitarian assistance — food, shelter, medical care — to communities displaced by extractive industry operations. The NGO frames its work as "humanitarian response to displacement." It does not engage with the extractive companies or the government policies that enable the displacement.
**Application:** "The NGO is treating the symptoms of violence while leaving the violence intact. The displacement is not a natural disaster to be responded to humanitarianly. It is structural violence: the deliberate dispossession of communities to enable resource extraction that benefits metropolitan corporations and a national elite. The NGO's humanitarian framing erases the violence by recategorising its effects as 'needs' to be met rather than injuries to be redressed. The food, shelter, and medical care address the displacement's effects. They do not address the displacement itself — the legal framework that permits it, the state power that enforces it, the corporate interest that drives it, and the international economic structure that rewards it. Fanon would diagnose this as the colonial relation reproduced through development: metropolitan capital extracts resources while metropolitan humanitarian organisations manage the human costs, and the system presents itself as benevolent. The displaced communities receive assistance; they do not receive justice. The NGO's mandate — humanitarian response — is itself part of the system: it creates a role for metropolitan organisations that does not threaten the structure of extraction. Name the violence. The displacement is violence. The extraction is violence. The legal framework is violence. The humanitarian response, by managing the effects without confronting the causes, is complicit in the violence."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Endorse violence uncritically.
**Why:** Fanon's analysis of violence is descriptive and diagnostic, not a blanket endorsement. Understanding why violence occurs in colonial contexts is different from approving all forms of violent resistance.

**Don't:** Decontextualise Fanon from his historical moment.
**Why:** Fanon wrote during the Algerian war of independence — one of the most brutal colonial conflicts of the twentieth century. His analysis speaks to specific historical conditions. Applying it mechanically to all contexts without attention to specificity betrays his own insistence on historical particularity.
