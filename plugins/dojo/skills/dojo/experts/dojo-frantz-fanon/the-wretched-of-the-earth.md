# The Wretched of the Earth

## When to Use
- When analysing the gap between formal independence and genuine decolonisation — why political transfer without structural transformation reproduces colonial arrangements.
- When confronting the scale of transformation that genuine decolonisation requires: not reform but the creation of fundamentally new social relations.
- When diagnosing the specific failures of post-independence states through Fanon's framework.

## Core Concept
*The Wretched of the Earth* (1961) is Fanon's most comprehensive and most contested work. Written while he was dying of leukaemia, during the Algerian war of independence, it provides both an analysis of colonial domination and a theory of liberation. Its central argument is that decolonisation is "the replacing of a certain 'species' of men by another 'species' of men" — a total transformation of social relations that cannot be achieved through negotiation or reform but only through the violent contestation of a system that is itself constitutively violent.

The book analyses decolonisation through several dimensions. The colonial structure: the colony is a Manichaean world divided into the zone of the coloniser (the city, with its lights, its order, its humanity) and the zone of the colonised (the village, the medina, with its poverty, its density, its dehumanisation). This spatial division is a total system — economic, political, psychological, and corporeal. National consciousness: the awareness of colonial domination that produces the demand for independence. Fanon distinguishes genuine national consciousness (which demands total social transformation) from the consciousness of the national bourgeoisie (which demands only the transfer of power to a national elite). The pitfalls of national consciousness: Fanon's devastating analysis of how post-independence states reproduce colonial structures under national flags — the chapter that has proven most prophetic. Culture and liberation: the role of cultural recovery in the process of decolonisation.

For development practice, *The Wretched of the Earth* provides the framework for understanding why development in post-colonial states so often reproduces the colonial structure it claims to transcend. The development industry — designed by former colonial powers, funded from metropolitan capitals, staffed by metropolitan professionals, operating through metropolitan categories — is a continuation of the colonial relation by other means. This does not mean development is impossible — it means that development which does not confront its colonial genealogy is not development at all.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess the completeness of decolonisation.** "Has decolonisation in this context been genuine — a transformation of social relations — or merely formal — a transfer of administrative authority? If the structures of the colonial economy, the colonial education system, and the colonial institutional framework remain intact, decolonisation is incomplete."
2. **Identify the Manichaean divide.** "Does the colonial spatial division persist? Are there zones of privilege and zones of deprivation? Does the development compound, the expatriate quarter, or the gated community reproduce the coloniser's city within the postcolonial state?"
3. **Diagnose the national bourgeoisie.** "Who inherited the state at independence? Have they used state power for social transformation or for personal enrichment? Have they created new economic structures or merely occupied the positions the coloniser vacated?"
4. **Demand total transformation.** "Reform within colonial structures is not decolonisation. Decolonisation requires the creation of new institutional forms, new economic arrangements, new cultural expressions, new ways of being. If the programme preserves the colonial structure while changing its personnel, it has not decolonised."

## Examples
**Situation:** Thirty years after independence, a country's economic structure remains essentially colonial: primary commodity exports to former colonial markets, import dependence on manufactured goods, an extractive elite that controls resource revenues, and a development sector funded and directed from the former metropole.
**Application:** "The flag changed. The structure did not. This is what Fanon predicted: independence captured by a national bourgeoisie that replaced the colonial administration without transforming the colonial economy. The extractive elite occupies the positions the coloniser vacated — controlling resource revenues, maintaining metropolitan economic relationships, governing through the administrative structures the colonial power built. The development sector completes the picture: metropolitan donors fund programmes designed in metropolitan capitals, implemented through metropolitan frameworks, evaluated by metropolitan criteria. The development industry is the colonial relation continued — not through direct political control but through the control of knowledge, resources, and categories. Genuine decolonisation would require: restructuring the economy from extractive export dependence to productive diversification; building institutions that reflect national rather than colonial logics; developing knowledge systems that are indigenous rather than imported; and redistributing the wealth that currently flows from the country to the metropole through the channels of 'development.' Fanon saw this clearly: 'The national middle class which takes over power at the end of the colonial regime is an underdeveloped middle class.' Its underdevelopment is not a deficiency to be remedied by capacity building — it is a structural position produced by the colonial economy. The remedy is not training but transformation."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce the book to a justification of violence.
**Why:** Violence occupies one chapter. The rest of the book analyses consciousness, culture, national failure, and the conditions for genuine social transformation. Reducing Fanon to violence is itself a form of colonial dismissal.

**Don't:** Treat decolonisation as a completed historical event.
**Why:** Formal colonialism ended. But the structures of colonial domination — economic, institutional, epistemological, psychological — persist and are actively maintained. Decolonisation remains an incomplete and ongoing process.
