# Fanon and Race

## When to Use
- When racial dynamics — who speaks, who is heard, who is credited, who is positioned as expert — are operating in professional settings but are not being named.
- When race needs to be analysed as a structural system, not reduced to individual attitudes or unconscious bias.
- When the development industry's own racial structure — white leadership, Black beneficiaries — needs to be confronted.

## Core Concept
For Fanon, race is not a secondary variable — it is a world-structuring system. Racism is not prejudice (individual attitudes) or discrimination (unequal treatment). It is an ontological system that determines who is fully human and who is not. The colonial world is a racial world: the coloniser's humanity is affirmed at the expense of the colonised, and the boundary between them is inscribed on the body.

Fanon's contribution is to show that race operates simultaneously as a political, economic, psychological, and embodied system. Politically, it determines who governs and who is governed. Economically, it determines who owns and who labours. Psychologically, it determines whose self-image is affirmed and whose is distorted. Corporeally, it determines whose body moves freely and whose body is constrained, surveilled, and controlled.

Whiteness, in this analysis, is not a skin colour but a structural position — the unmarked norm against which all others are measured. The white professional is simply "professional"; the Black professional is always "a Black professional." Whiteness does not need to declare itself because it operates as the default — the invisible standard of competence, authority, and legitimacy.

For development practice, the racial structure is glaring and largely unnamed. The development industry is racially stratified: white leadership in headquarters, Black and brown populations as "beneficiaries" in the field. White consultants are deployed as experts to advise national professionals who may have more relevant knowledge. The racial hierarchy of the colonial period — white expertise over Black capacity — is reproduced in the structure of technical assistance, consultancy, and knowledge production.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the racial structure.** "Who leads this organisation? Who are the consultants? Who are the beneficiaries? If the pattern is white leadership and expertise, brown implementation, and Black beneficiation, the colonial racial structure is being reproduced."
2. **Identify whiteness as a position.** "Where does whiteness operate as the unmarked norm? In which spaces is the white professional simply 'the expert' while the national professional must prove their competence? The asymmetry reveals the racial structure."
3. **Refuse the reduction to individual prejudice.** "The issue is not whether individual professionals hold racist attitudes. The issue is whether the structure — of organisations, of funding flows, of knowledge production, of expertise — reproduces a racial hierarchy. Structural racism does not require individual racists."
4. **Connect race to colonial continuity.** "The racial structure of the development industry is not accidental. It is the continuation of the colonial racial hierarchy by other means. The same countries that colonised now 'develop'; the same populations that were colonised now receive 'assistance.' The racial hierarchy persists because the power structure that produced it persists."

## Examples
**Situation:** An international development organisation conducts an internal equity review. It finds that senior leadership is 85% white (from North America and Europe), while 75% of field staff are nationals of the countries where the organisation operates. The organisation's response is to commit to "diversifying leadership" and "addressing unconscious bias."
**Application:** "The equity review has identified the symptom. The response addresses the wrong cause. 'Diversifying leadership' and 'addressing unconscious bias' treat the racial structure as a product of individual attitudes and recruitment practices. But the racial hierarchy of the organisation is structural — it reproduces the colonial division between white expertise and Black labour. White professionals occupy leadership because the organisation's structure positions them there: they are recruited from metropolitan universities, they possess the forms of capital (Northern education, English fluency, donor networks) that the organisation values, and they are perceived as 'leaders' because whiteness carries the symbolic capital of authority. National staff remain in field positions because the organisation's structure positions them there: they possess local knowledge (which the organisation undervalues), they lack the metropolitan credentials the organisation rewards, and they are perceived as 'implementers' because the racial hierarchy assigns them that role. 'Unconscious bias training' cannot address a structural problem. What is needed is structural change: relocating authority to national staff, restructuring recruitment to value different forms of capital, redistributing budgets from headquarters to country offices, and confronting the colonial genealogy that produced the organisation's racial hierarchy in the first place."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce race to individual prejudice.
**Why:** Structural racism operates through institutions, systems, and distributions of capital — not through individual attitudes. An organisation can have no racist individuals and still reproduce a racist structure.

**Don't:** Use racial analysis to shame.
**Why:** The purpose is illumination, not condemnation. Understanding how racial structures operate is the precondition for transforming them. Shaming individuals for their position in a structure they did not create produces defensiveness, not change.
