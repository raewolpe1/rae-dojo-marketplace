# Fanon and the Body

## When to Use
- When the body provides evidence of colonial or neocolonial power that words do not capture — who enters the room with confidence, who defers physically, who occupies space and who contracts.
- When the physical dimensions of domination — spatial segregation, bodily constraint, the experience of being physically marked by race — need to be named.
- When liberatory practice must address not just consciousness but the body's colonised dispositions.

## Core Concept
Fanon's phenomenology of the body is among his most powerful contributions. The colonised body is not merely a physical organism — it is a site of domination. Colonialism inscribes itself on the body: in posture (the deference of the colonised, the expansiveness of the coloniser), in movement (who walks freely, who steps aside), in spatial occupation (who takes up space, who contracts), and in the experience of being-in-the-world (the white body as subject, the Black body as object).

Fanon describes the experience of being Black in a white world as a corporeal experience: the sudden, violent awareness of one's own body as seen by the other. The body becomes a prison — the skin, the hair, the physiognomy become markers that fix the colonised person in the colonial hierarchy regardless of their individual qualities. The colonised person develops what Fanon calls a "third-person consciousness" — experiencing their own body as it is seen by the coloniser, from the outside.

For development practice, the body is evidence. In any professional setting — a meeting, a workshop, a field visit — the bodies in the room tell a story about power that the agenda, the minutes, and the report will not record. Who sits at the head of the table? Who stands? Who makes eye contact with the chair and who averts their gaze? Who enters the room with the assumption that they belong, and who enters tentatively, as if requesting permission? These corporeal dynamics are not secondary to the "real" content of the meeting — they are the primary evidence of the power relations that shape every other dimension of the encounter.

## How to Apply
1. **Read the bodies.** "In any professional encounter, attend to the bodies. Who occupies space expansively? Who contracts? Who moves freely? Who waits for permission? The bodies tell the truth about power relations that the words may disguise."
2. **Attend to spatial dynamics.** "How is space organised? Does the programme compound reproduce the colonial geography — high walls, guarded gates, air-conditioned offices separate from the community? The physical space embodies the power relation."
3. **Name the corporeal costs.** "What does it cost the national professional to enter the donor's office? The community member to enter the government building? The woman to enter the meeting of men? These entries require bodily performances — adjustments of posture, gesture, and self-presentation — that reveal the power relation."
4. **Design for bodily dignity.** "How does the programme's physical arrangement affect the dignity of those it serves? The queue for cash transfers, the medical examination in the mobile clinic, the registration process for the aid programme — each imposes a bodily experience that may be dignified or degrading."

## Examples
**Situation:** During a programme review meeting in a government conference room, the international consultants sit at the main table, speaking freely and at length. The government officials occupy chairs along the wall, speaking only when addressed. The community representatives stand near the door, silent.
**Application:** "Read the bodies. The spatial arrangement is the colonial hierarchy made physical. The international consultants occupy the centre — the position of authority, of expertise, of the right to speak. Their bodies are expansive: leaning back, gesturing, making eye contact with each other. The government officials occupy the periphery — the position of the intermediary, the national administrator who mediates between the colonial centre and the colonised population. Their bodies are measured: upright, attentive, speaking when invited. The community representatives occupy the margin — the position of the colonised, the object of the programme, the body that is present but silent. Their bodies are contracted: standing, close to the exit, ready to leave or ready to defer. This arrangement was not designed — it emerged from the habitus of each group, from the embodied dispositions that determine how each group occupies space in relation to the others. But it is not natural. It is the colonial hierarchy inscribed in bodies. A genuine programme review would redistribute the bodies: community representatives at the centre, speaking first and at length, in their own language. The international consultants at the periphery, listening. The spatial rearrangement is not symbolic — it is the physical condition for a different kind of encounter."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce the body to a metaphor.
**Why:** Fanon's body is not a metaphor. It is the actual, physical, corporeal experience of domination — the skin that is seen, the posture that defers, the space that contracts. The analysis must attend to real bodies, not to the body as a theoretical concept.

**Don't:** Aestheticise bodily suffering.
**Why:** The colonised body's suffering — the violence inscribed on it, the dignity denied to it — is not material for theoretical contemplation. It is a reality to be confronted and transformed.
