# Fanon and Culture

## When to Use
- When cultural recovery — the revitalisation of indigenous languages, practices, knowledge systems, and art forms — is part of a liberation project.
- When the tradition-versus-modernity debate conceals a colonial power relation: "tradition" as the coloniser's category for what should be abandoned, "modernity" as the coloniser's category for what should be adopted.
- When culture is being treated as heritage (static, preserved, displayed) rather than as a living resource for social transformation.

## Core Concept
Fanon analyses culture through the lens of colonial domination and liberation. Colonialism does not merely suppress indigenous culture — it devalues it. The coloniser establishes a hierarchy of cultures (civilised/primitive, modern/traditional, universal/local) that positions the coloniser's culture as the norm and the colonised's culture as the deviation. The colonised person is told — through education, through media, through institutional practice — that their own culture is inferior, backward, and incompatible with progress.

The response to cultural colonialism takes three stages, which Fanon describes in "On National Culture." First, the assimilation stage: the colonised intellectual adopts the coloniser's culture, demonstrates mastery of the coloniser's language and art forms, and seeks recognition within the coloniser's cultural system. Second, the romantic return: the colonised intellectual, recognising assimilation as self-betrayal, returns to indigenous culture — but in an idealised, essentialist form, celebrating pre-colonial culture as pure and authentic. Third, the combat stage: the intellectual moves beyond both assimilation and romantic return to create new cultural forms that draw on indigenous resources while responding to contemporary conditions. This is the fighting culture — not heritage to be preserved but a living resource for liberation.

For development practice, the cultural dimension is often ignored or instrumentalised. Programmes treat culture as either an obstacle to development (traditional practices that must be overcome) or as a decorative element (cultural performances at programme events). Neither approach takes culture seriously as a site of political struggle — as the domain in which communities define who they are, what they value, and how they want to live.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the cultural hierarchy.** "What culture is positioned as modern, progressive, and developmental? What culture is positioned as traditional, backward, and obstructive? This hierarchy is colonial — it positions the coloniser's culture as the standard and the colonised's culture as the deviation."
2. **Assess the stage.** "Is the community or institution in the assimilation stage (adopting metropolitan culture), the romantic return (celebrating idealised tradition), or the combat stage (creating new cultural forms from indigenous resources)? The stage determines what support is appropriate."
3. **Treat culture as political.** "Cultural recovery is not heritage preservation — it is a political act. When a community revitalises its language, practises its governance traditions, or applies its knowledge systems to contemporary problems, it is contesting the colonial hierarchy. Support this as political action, not as cultural tourism."
4. **Avoid instrumentalising culture.** "Do not include 'cultural activities' in the programme as decoration. Either take culture seriously — as a dimension of liberation that shapes the programme's design and implementation — or do not invoke it at all."

## Examples
**Situation:** A community development programme includes a "cultural component" that funds traditional dance performances, craft production for tourist markets, and the documentation of oral histories. The programme describes this as "preserving cultural heritage." Community elders express discomfort, saying that their culture is not a museum exhibit.
**Application:** "The programme has frozen culture. It has taken the living practices of a community — dance, craft, oral narrative — and converted them into heritage: static artefacts to be preserved, displayed, and marketed. This is the coloniser's treatment of the colonised's culture: interesting, valuable in a folkloric sense, worthy of preservation, but fundamentally past — a relic of the pre-modern that must be documented before it disappears. The elders are right: their culture is not a museum exhibit. It is a living resource — a system of knowledge, governance, social organisation, and meaning-making that continues to function in the present. The programme should ask: what cultural resources does this community possess that are relevant to contemporary challenges? How does the community's governance tradition inform current institutional design? How does its environmental knowledge inform current climate adaptation? How does its conflict resolution practice inform current peacebuilding? These are not heritage questions — they are development questions. The cultural resources are not to be preserved in a museum but deployed in the present. This is Fanon's combat stage: the use of indigenous cultural resources not as nostalgia but as the raw material for new forms of social life."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Romanticise pre-colonial culture.
**Why:** Pre-colonial cultures were complex, stratified, and internally contested. Treating them as pure origins is the mirror image of the coloniser's dismissal — both deny the culture its actual complexity.

**Don't:** Substitute cultural politics for material transformation.
**Why:** Cultural recovery without economic redistribution, institutional reform, and political transformation is Fanon's combat culture without combat — a performance of liberation without the substance.
