# The Colonial Gaze

## When to Use
- When the act of observation itself — monitoring, evaluation, research, classification — reproduces a colonial power relation in which the observer defines the observed.
- When communities experience being studied, measured, and reported on as a form of domination rather than accountability.
- When the development professional's gaze — what they see, how they see it, what they record — needs to be examined as a form of power.

## Core Concept
Fanon's account of the colonial gaze draws on and radicalises phenomenological theories of the look. For Fanon, the coloniser's gaze does not merely observe the colonised — it constitutes them. The colonised person is "fixed" by the look: reduced to a body, a colour, a category. The gaze does not see the colonised person as they are — it sees them as the colonial system requires them to be: primitive, dangerous, childlike, in need of civilisation.

The power of the gaze is the power to define. The coloniser sees the colonised, describes them, classifies them, and records them — in reports, in censuses, in anthropological studies, in development assessments. The colonised do not describe themselves — they are described. They do not define their needs — their needs are defined. They do not tell their story — their story is told. The asymmetry is total: the coloniser is the subject who sees; the colonised is the object who is seen.

For development practice, the colonial gaze operates through the apparatus of monitoring, evaluation, and research. The development professional arrives in a community, observes it, measures it, classifies it, and reports on it — to a distant audience (the donor, the headquarters, the academic journal) that has the power to decide what the community is and what it needs. The community is the object of this gaze — observed, measured, and represented without controlling the observation, the measurement, or the representation.

## How to Apply
1. **Examine who is looking at whom.** "In this evaluation, who is observing and who is observed? Who has the power to define what counts as evidence, what questions are asked, and how findings are represented? The direction of the gaze reveals the direction of power."
2. **Check for representational control.** "Does the community control how it is represented — in the evaluation report, the programme document, the research publication? Or is the community represented by external observers using external categories? Representational control is a dimension of power."
3. **Attend to the experience of being observed.** "How does the community experience the evaluation? As accountability (their right to know how resources are used) or as surveillance (being watched, measured, and judged by outsiders)? If the latter, the colonial gaze is operating."
4. **Redistribute the gaze.** "Can the evaluation be redesigned so that the community is the subject rather than the object? Community-led evaluation, participatory monitoring, and self-assessment redistribute the gaze — making the community the observer and the programme the observed."

## Examples
**Situation:** An international research team conducts a large-scale household survey in a rural area. The survey collects detailed information on household income, assets, health, education, and governance participation. The data is analysed at a Northern university and published in an academic journal. The community never sees the results.
**Application:** "The research team's survey is the colonial gaze formalised as methodology. The community is the object of observation — measured, coded, analysed, and represented in a journal article that the community will never read, in a language the community does not speak, for an audience that has no relationship to the community. The community's complex, particular, morally significant reality is reduced to a dataset — variable values in a statistical analysis that will inform the careers of the researchers and the policies of the donors but not the lives of the community. The community did not choose to be studied, did not design the questions, did not control the analysis, and will not benefit from the publication. They provided information; they received nothing. This is the extractive logic of the colonial gaze: the metropolitan centre observes the periphery, extracts knowledge, processes it in the metropole, and publishes it for metropolitan consumption. The community is the raw material. The remedy is not to stop research but to redistribute the gaze: community involvement in research design, community access to results, community control over representation, and genuine reciprocity — not the fiction of 'informed consent' but the reality of shared ownership."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reject all observation as colonial.
**Why:** Monitoring, evaluation, and research serve legitimate purposes — accountability, learning, evidence for policy. The issue is not observation itself but the power relation within which it occurs.

**Don't:** Use the concept loosely.
**Why:** The colonial gaze names a specific power relation: the asymmetry between the subject who defines and the object who is defined. Not every act of observation is colonial. The concept has analytical power only when the specific asymmetry is identified.
