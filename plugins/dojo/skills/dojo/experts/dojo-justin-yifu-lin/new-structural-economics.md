# New Structural Economics

## When to Use
- When the framework itself needs explaining.
- When structural transformation is being discussed.
- When industrial upgrading is being treated as a cause rather than a consequence of development.

## Fails When
- **The question is general growth strategy.** Route to Rodrik — growth diagnostics is the better tool and Lin adds nothing.

## Core Concept

**Three propositions and a consequence.**

**1. Endowment structure determines optimal industrial structure.** A country at any moment has a given relative abundance of **labour, capital, natural resources and infrastructure**. That structure makes some industries viable and others not. **Relative** abundance is what matters — not whether a factor exists, but its price relative to the others. This is a constraint, not a preference, and it operates whether or not policy acknowledges it.

**2. Development is endowment upgrading first; industrial upgrading follows.** You do not become capital-abundant by building capital-intensive industries. You accumulate capital by being competitive in industries your current endowment supports, and the optimal industrial structure moves as the endowment does. **Industrial upgrading is an output of development, not an input to it.** Reversing that order is the characteristic error.

**3. The state's role is to facilitate the transition** the endowment makes possible — not to force one it does not.

**The consequence: NSE is a constraint on industrial policy, not a licence for it.** This is what is most often misread. Lin is grouped with the industrial policy advocates, and he is one — but his signature claim is that **most industrial policy fails because it defies comparative advantage, and most industrial policy is therefore wrong.** He is the conservative in that camp.

**Why "structural" and why "new".** *Structural* because the framework insists that the structure of the economy — factor endowments, industrial composition, infrastructure — is the primary object, against a neoclassical tradition that abstracted from it. *New* because the old structuralism (Prebisch, Singer, the ECLA tradition) reached the opposite policy conclusion from the same starting point: it saw structure as a trap to be escaped by force, and prescribed import-substituting industrialisation, which is CAD, which failed. **NSE shares old structuralism's diagnosis and rejects its prescription.**

**The position relative to the Washington Consensus.** Equally hostile, for the opposite reason: it ignored structure entirely, treating all economies as the same object requiring the same reforms. NSE's complaint is that both traditions got structure wrong — one by trying to override it, the other by not seeing it.

**What it delivers operationally.** A **filter** (the viability test), a **target class** (latent comparative advantage), a **method** (GIFF benchmarking), and a **role definition** (the facilitating state). Whether it delivers more than Rodrik and Chang already do is the question `lin-vs-rodrik-and-chang.md` answers, and the answer is: only the filter and the method.

## How to Apply

1. **Establish the endowment structure.** Relative abundance of labour, capital, resources, infrastructure. This is the starting point of every NSE argument.
2. **Derive the viable industrial structure from it**, rather than selecting industries and asking how to make them work.
3. **Treat industrial upgrading as a consequence.** If the strategy's causal arrow runs from industry to development, it is inverted.
4. **Locate NSE against its two rivals** when explaining it: old structuralism (right diagnosis, wrong prescription) and the Washington Consensus (no diagnosis).
5. **Do not use it as a general growth framework.** It is a sector-selection framework. Rodrik covers growth.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy's logic runs: target advanced industries → the economy becomes advanced → incomes rise.

**Application:** The arrow is inverted. In NSE the sequence runs: compete in what the endowment supports → generate surplus without support → accumulate capital → the endowment upgrades → the next tier of industries becomes viable → repeat. Industrial upgrading is the *output*. A strategy that begins by selecting advanced industries has assumed the endowment it intends to produce, and the mechanism by which the assumption is enforced is protection — which starts the CAD failure sequence. Rewrite the causal chain and the sector list changes with it.

**Result:** A strategy whose sector list follows from its endowment rather than from its ambition.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Read NSE as a case for more intervention.
**Why:** It is a case for constrained intervention. The constraint is the contribution.

**Don't:** Confuse it with old structuralism.
**Why:** Same diagnosis, opposite prescription. Old structuralism prescribed CAD and it failed.

**Don't:** Use it as a general growth framework.
**Why:** It is a sector-selection tool. Rodrik's diagnostics are the better general instrument.
