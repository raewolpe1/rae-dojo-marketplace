# The Facilitating State

## When to Use
- When defining what the state should actually do.
- When protection, subsidy, or an open-ended incentive is proposed.
- When an SEZ or industrial park is on the table.

## Fails When
- **The question is institutional design** — selectivity, sunset clauses, discipline mechanisms, insulation from capture. **Route to Rodrik.** That is his contribution, not Lin's, and Lin adds nothing to it.

## Core Concept

**The state facilitates what the endowment makes viable. It does not pick and protect what it wishes were viable.**

**Two errors, and NSE rejects both.**

**The picking-and-protecting state** selects industries by ambition, protects them from competition, subsidises them when protection fails, directs credit when subsidy fails, and ends up with a permanently uncompetitive industry, a repressed financial system, and a captured policy process. This is the CAD sequence.

**The absent state** assumes markets produce the optimal industrial structure unaided. They do not, because coordination failures and information externalities are real. Viable industries fail to emerge and nobody notices.

**What the facilitating state actually does — the complete list, and note how short it is:**

1. **Infrastructure.** Power, transport, water, connectivity. The classic public good.
2. **Coordination.** Where an industry needs a supplier base, a skills pipeline, or a shared facility that no single firm will build and all need — convene and coordinate.
3. **Compensate the information externality.** The pioneer discovers whether the industry works and the information is free to imitators. A **limited, time-bound** incentive corrects the under-entry. **This is the only subsidy in the framework.**
4. **Incubate**, where no domestic firm exists in an identified latent industry.
5. **Attract the right FDI** — specifically from comparator countries being pushed out of the industry by rising wages.
6. **Build the second-best instrument** where the first-best is unavailable: an industrial park or SEZ that fixes one square kilometre of the business environment.

**And that is the list.** Notice what is not on it: protection, permanent subsidy, directed credit, ownership. Notice also that **every item has an end date or is a permanent public good.** Nothing in the list is an open-ended transfer to a firm.

**The test that governs everything:** **if the support does not end, the industry was not viable.** A facilitating intervention is finite by construction — infrastructure is built, coordination is solved, a pioneer incentive expires. An intervention with no end date is not facilitation; it is protection, and it has started the CAD sequence.

**On SEZs and industrial parks — the second-best argument.** Lin recommends them without embarrassment, and the reasoning is worth stating because it applies directly to a provincial actor. **If you cannot fix the national business environment, fix one square kilometre of it.** This is not ideal. The first-best is a good business environment everywhere. But the first-best is not on offer to a province, and refusing the second-best on the grounds that it is not first-best leaves you with the status quo, which is third-best.

**Where Lin stops and Rodrik starts.** Lin tells you *what* the state should do — facilitate the viable, never protect the non-viable. **He does not tell you how to build a state that can do it without being captured.** Selectivity, performance conditions, sunset clauses, embeddedness without capture — that is Rodrik's contribution and it is the harder half. Use both, and do not pretend Lin covers institutional design.

## How to Apply

1. **Check every intervention against the six-item list.** If it is not on it, ask why.
2. **Apply the end-date test to everything.** No end date, no facilitation.
3. **Take the second-best seriously.** A province cannot fix the national business environment. It may be able to fix a square kilometre.
4. **Never grant protection as a bridge.** It is a destination.
5. **Route the institutional design question to Rodrik.** Lin says what; Rodrik says how without capture.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy proposes preferential procurement, extended tax incentives, and tariff protection for a priority sector.

**Application:** Test each against the list and the end date. **Preferential procurement:** an open-ended transfer to firms in a chosen sector — not on the list, and it creates exactly the soft budget constraint that removes the incentive to become efficient. **Extended tax incentives:** if time-bound and compensating a pioneer's information externality, this is item 3 and legitimate; if open-ended and available to all entrants, it is a rent. **Tariff protection:** not on the list at all, and it is step 3 of the CAD failure sequence. **What would be legitimate instead:** the infrastructure the sector needs; coordination of the supplier base or skills pipeline; a time-bound pioneer incentive for genuine first movers; and — if the business environment cannot be fixed provincially — a park that fixes it locally.

**Result:** An instrument set that facilitates rather than protects, and every item with an end date.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Grant open-ended incentives.
**Why:** The end date is the definition of facilitation. Without one it is protection.

**Don't:** Reject SEZs as second-best.
**Why:** They are second-best. The first-best is unavailable to a province and the alternative is third-best.

**Don't:** Look to Lin for institutional design.
**Why:** He says what the state should do. Rodrik says how to build one that can do it without capture — the harder half.
