# Latent Comparative Advantage

## When to Use
- When an industry fails the viability test and the *reason* needs establishing.
- When asking what the state should legitimately act on.
- **This is the difficult judgement in NSE.** It is where the framework is most useful and most abusable.

## Fails When
- **The industry is clearly viable and emerging.** Then nothing is latent; get out of the way.

## Core Concept

**Latent comparative advantage: industries a country could be competitive in given its endowment structure, but is not, because something is preventing emergence rather than preventing viability.**

**The four emergence failures — and they are the state's legitimate business:**

1. **Coordination failure.** The industry needs infrastructure, a supplier base, or a skills pipeline that no single firm will build alone and all firms need. Nobody moves first. The state coordinates.
2. **Information externality.** The **pioneer** firm that enters a new industry discovers whether it works — and that information becomes free to every imitator. The pioneer bears the full cost of discovery and captures a fraction of its value. Under-entry follows, predictably. **A limited, time-bound incentive to pioneers is the correct answer**, and it is finite by construction.
3. **Transaction costs.** Logistics, customs, contract enforcement, payment systems — costs that make a viable industry unviable *at this location* without changing its underlying economics.
4. **Infrastructure gaps.** Power, water, transport, connectivity.

**Why this is the crux of the whole framework.** An industry can fail the naive viability test for two reasons that look identical from the outside:

- **The endowment cannot support it** → CAD → do not target it, ever.
- **The endowment supports it but emergence has failed** → latent → target it, and the intervention will work.

**These look the same on the ground.** In both cases there are no firms, or firms that are losing money. The distinction is counterfactual: *if the coordination were solved and the infrastructure existed, would a normally managed firm survive without ongoing support?* If yes, latent. If no, CAD.

**How the framework gets abused, in both directions.** A conservative reading rejects genuinely latent industries as CAD, because they currently fail. An advocate's reading relabels CAD ambitions as "latent" — after all, one can always claim that with enough coordination and infrastructure the industry would fly. **"Latent" is the word that will be used to smuggle CAD back in**, and anyone using this framework should expect that and guard against it.

**The guard.** Two tests. **First: is the support finite?** Coordination is solved once; infrastructure is built once; a pioneer incentive is time-bound by construction. **If the required support has no end date, the industry is not latent — it is non-viable.** Second: **does the industry exist in comparator economies with a similar endowment structure?** If nowhere with your endowment does this profitably, the claim that only coordination stands in the way is doing a great deal of work. That is what the GIFF benchmark is for.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask the counterfactual, not the current state.** If the emergence failure were removed, would it survive unsupported?
2. **Name the specific failure.** Coordination, information externality, transaction cost, or infrastructure. If it cannot be named, "latent" is doing the work of a wish.
3. **Apply the end-date test.** Finite support → latent. Permanent support → CAD.
4. **Benchmark it.** Does anyone with your endowment structure make this profitably? (See GIFF.)
5. **Expect the word to be abused** and guard the definition, because it is where the framework leaks.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy argues that a high-value agro-processing sub-sector is "latent" and needs support to emerge.

**Application:** Test it. **Name the failure:** is it cold chain infrastructure that no single processor will build (coordination — legitimate)? Is it that the first exporter to a new market discovers the compliance pathway and every follower gets it free (information externality — legitimate, and a time-bound pioneer incentive is correct)? Or is it that the product simply costs more to make here than elsewhere (endowment — CAD, and no support fixes it)? **Apply the end date:** the cold chain is built once. The pioneer incentive expires. If instead the sub-sector needs an ongoing per-unit subsidy, it is not latent. **Benchmark:** do economies with a comparable endowment structure and roughly double the income produce this profitably? If several do, the latency claim is strong. If none do, it is a wish.

**Result:** A latency claim that has been tested rather than asserted.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use "latent" without naming the specific emergence failure.
**Why:** Unnamed, it is a wish. Named, it is a diagnosis with an instrument.

**Don't:** Grant open-ended support to a "latent" industry.
**Why:** The end date is the test. No end date means it was never latent.

**Don't:** Assume that enough coordination makes anything viable.
**Why:** That reasoning relabels every CAD ambition as latent, which is exactly how the framework fails in practice.
