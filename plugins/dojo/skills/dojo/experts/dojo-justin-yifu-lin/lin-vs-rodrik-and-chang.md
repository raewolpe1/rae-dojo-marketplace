# Where Lin Is Redundant, and Where He Is Not

## When to Use
- **Load this on almost every question routed to this dojo.** It is the honesty file.
- When deciding whether Lin adds anything to Rodrik and Chang.
- When all three are being considered for a panel.

## Fails When
- **The question is specifically the GIFF benchmarking procedure.** That is Lin's alone; skip the disclaimer and use it.

## Core Concept

**This dojo overlaps heavily with Rodrik and Chang, both already in the collection. That is a known limitation. This file exists so the overlap is managed rather than discovered.**

### The three positions, precisely

| | **Rodrik** | **Chang** | **Lin** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **On industrial policy** | Legitimate and necessary; the challenge is *design* — selectivity, performance conditions, sunset, embeddedness without capture | Legitimate and necessary; **defying comparative advantage is how development actually happened** — the rich countries did it and then kicked away the ladder | Legitimate and necessary but **tightly constrained** — follow comparative advantage; **defying it is why most industrial policy fails** |
| **Method** | Growth diagnostics — find the binding constraint, country by country | Historical — what did the now-rich countries actually do? | Structural — what does the endowment structure support? |
| **On sector selection** | Largely agnostic; the diagnostic points at constraints, not sectors | Target strategically, accept the cost of defying | **Benchmark: similar endowment, double the income, a generation ago** |
| **Temperament** | Second-best; it depends | Combative; the orthodoxy is hypocrisy | Conservative; most of what you want to do is wrong |

### The famous disagreement

**Lin and Chang have a published exchange** on precisely this question — whether industrial policy in developing countries should conform to comparative advantage or defy it. It is the clearest statement of the axis. Chang: the ladder was climbed by defying, and telling poor countries to follow their comparative advantage is telling them to stay poor. Lin: the countries that defied mostly failed, and the ones that succeeded (Korea, Taiwan) were following a comparative advantage that was upgrading fast — the defiance is a misreading of the record. `[CHECK]` — the exchange's exact citation is unverified; it is associated with *Development Policy Review*.

**Do not resolve this.** It is a live disagreement between two serious people, and a strategy that pretends otherwise is doing advocacy.

### Where Lin is REDUNDANT

- **"Should we do industrial policy?"** → Rodrik and Chang cover it. Lin adds a third voice saying broadly the same thing with a different accent.
- **"What's wrong with the Washington Consensus?"** → Rodrik, comprehensively. Lin agrees; nothing is added.
- **"Is industrial policy legitimate?"** → All three say yes. Loading three voices to agree is noise.
- **"Design our industrial policy institutions"** → Rodrik. Selectivity, sunset, performance conditions, embeddedness without capture is his contribution, not Lin's.
- **"Why do rich countries oppose our industrial policy?"** → Chang. That is his whole argument and Lin has no equivalent.

### Where Lin is NOT redundant — the actual marginal value

Three things, and they are narrow:

**1. The endowment-structure constraint on sector selection.** Rodrik's diagnostics tell you what is *blocking* growth. Chang tells you that defiance is *permitted*. **Neither gives you a rule for deciding which sectors to target.** Lin does: does the endowment structure support this industry, and is a normally managed firm in it viable without protection? That is a usable filter and the other two do not supply one.

**2. The GIFF benchmarking procedure.** An actual operational method — identify countries with similar endowment structure and roughly double the income per capita a generation ago; look at what they made. **Rodrik and Chang have no equivalent procedure.** This is the single most transferable thing in this dojo.

**3. The conservative reading of the failure record.** Lin is the only one of the three whose central claim is that **most industrial policy is wrong**. That is a genuinely different disposition, and in a room where everyone is enthusiastic about industrial policy it is the useful voice. It also pairs unexpectedly well with McKenzie's scepticism — for entirely different reasons.

### The honest verdict

**Lin's marginal value in this collection is the sector-selection filter and the GIFF method. Nothing else.** If the question is not about which sectors to target and on what basis, use Rodrik or Chang and do not load this dojo.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask whether the question is sector selection.** If not, route to Rodrik or Chang.
2. **If it is sector selection, Lin is the only one with a rule.** Use him.
3. **Never merge the three.** They disagree, publicly and substantively, and the disagreement is the information.
4. **Where Lin and Chang conflict, present both.** Do not resolve it.
5. **Note the Lin–McKenzie convergence** where relevant: both expect most of what is proposed to fail, from opposite methodologies.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy asks whether the province should pursue industrial policy at all.

**Application:** Do not use Lin. Rodrik answers this better — industrial policy is legitimate and necessary, the challenge is design — and Chang answers the political-economy half. Lin would say yes-but-constrained, which is a third variation on a settled point. **Now change the question:** "the province has a list of eight priority sectors — are they the right ones?" **That is Lin's question and only Lin's.** Apply the viability test to each, sort into comparative-advantage-following, latent, and defying, and benchmark the list against comparator economies with a similar endowment structure at roughly double the income a generation ago.

**Result:** The right expert on the right question, and two experts left unloaded.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Load Lin, Rodrik and Chang together on a general industrial policy question.
**Why:** They will agree at length, in three voices, and the reader learns nothing.

**Don't:** Resolve the Lin–Chang disagreement.
**Why:** It is live, published, and between two serious people. Presenting a resolution is advocacy.

**Don't:** Use Lin as a generic industrial policy advocate.
**Why:** His central claim is that most industrial policy is wrong. Using him as a yes-vote inverts him.
