# Comparative Advantage Following versus Defying

## When to Use
- When an industry or sector target is proposed.
- When asked why a long-protected industry is still not competitive.
- As the diagnostic core of any Lin answer.

## Fails When
- **The question is about a single firm.** This is an industry-level test.

## Core Concept

**The viability test:** could a firm in this industry, **with normal management**, survive in an **open competitive market** in this country, **without protection and without subsidy**?

**Yes → viable → the industry follows comparative advantage (CAF).**
**No → non-viable → targeting it defies comparative advantage (CAD).**

**Why viability is not a synonym for profitability.** A protected firm can be profitable and non-viable simultaneously — profitable *because* protected. Viability asks the counterfactual: strip the protection, open the market, keep the management ordinary. What happens?

**The CAD failure sequence, which is the same everywhere it has been run:**

1. The industry is targeted because it is strategically attractive — it is what rich countries make.
2. Firms enter and cannot compete, because the endowment does not support the factor intensity the industry requires.
3. **Protection** is granted, as a bridge to competitiveness.
4. Protection does not produce efficiency, so **subsidy** follows.
5. Subsidy is insufficient, so **credit is directed** to the industry.
6. Directing credit requires **repressing the financial system**, which distorts the price of capital for every other firm in the economy.
7. The firm now faces a **soft budget constraint** — losses are absorbed — and therefore has **no reason to become efficient** and every reason to lobby.
8. Rents attract **capture**.
9. Twenty years on, the industry is not competitive, and the argument is that it needs more time.

**It does not need more time. It needed a different endowment structure.** This sequence is not an implementation failure. **It is what CAD is.** The distinction matters enormously for how a strategy reads its own history.

**The CAF virtuous sequence:**

1. Firms compete in industries the endowment supports.
2. They generate **surplus** without support.
3. Surplus accumulates as **capital**.
4. Capital accumulation **upgrades the endowment structure**.
5. The upgraded endowment makes the **next tier of industries viable**.
6. Repeat.

**This is the entire theory of growth in NSE**, and note that it makes industrial upgrading an *output* of development rather than an input to it.

**The essential qualification — do not misuse this test.** An industry can fail the viability test for two completely different reasons, and conflating them destroys the framework:

- **The endowment cannot support it** → genuinely CAD → do not target it.
- **The endowment supports it, but it has not emerged** because of coordination failure, transaction costs, infrastructure gaps, or missing information → **latent comparative advantage** → this is exactly what the state should act on.

Distinguishing these is the difficult judgement in NSE and it is where the framework can be abused in either direction — by rejecting viable industries as CAD, or by relabelling CAD ambitions as "latent".

**The honest limit.** The Korea and Taiwan cases are contested. Chang reads them as successful defiance. Lin reads them as following a comparative advantage that was upgrading very fast. **Both readings are available from the record**, and a strategy that asserts one without acknowledging the other is doing advocacy.

## How to Apply

1. **Run the viability test on every proposed sector.** Normal management, open market, no protection, no subsidy.
2. **Sort into three, not two:** CAF (support and facilitate), latent (act on the emergence failure), CAD (remove).
3. **Check the failure reason** before assigning to CAD. Endowment, or coordination?
4. **Read the protection history as evidence.** Decades of protection without competitiveness is a completed viability test.
5. **Time-bound anything you do grant.** Open-ended support is the entry point to the CAD sequence.
6. **Acknowledge the Korea/Taiwan contest** rather than settling it.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy proposes to build a provincial electronics manufacturing capability, citing East Asian precedent.

**Application:** Run the test. Could a normally managed electronics manufacturer in the Western Cape survive in an open market against East Asian competition without protection or subsidy? Almost certainly not — the endowment does not supply the supplier density, the scale, or the specialised labour, and relative abundance is what counts. So the target is CAD, and the East Asian citation is contested precisely on this point: whether Korea defied comparative advantage or followed one that was upgrading rapidly. Then the important second question: **is there a latent version?** Not electronics manufacturing, but perhaps a specific component or service where the endowment does support viability and only a coordination failure prevents entry. That is worth the search. The general target is not.

**Result:** A CAD target removed, and a latent search opened where the endowment actually reaches.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat protection as a bridge.
**Why:** It is a destination. The soft budget constraint removes the reason to cross.

**Don't:** Assign an industry to CAD without checking for coordination failure.
**Why:** Latent advantage fails the naive viability test too. The difference is everything.

**Don't:** Cite Korea as settled.
**Why:** Chang reads it as successful defiance and Lin as fast-upgrading following. The record supports both readings.
