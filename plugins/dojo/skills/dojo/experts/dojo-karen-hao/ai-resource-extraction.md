# AI Resource Extraction

## When to Use
- When AI is presented as weightless software — as pure capability without material substrate — and the physical infrastructure needs to be made visible.
- When data centre siting, energy consumption, water use, or mineral supply chains are at stake.
- When the geography of AI extraction — which communities bear the costs — needs to be analysed.

## Core Concept
AI is not weightless. The frontier AI systems that are marketed as pure digital intelligence rest on a massive physical infrastructure: data centres that consume electricity equivalent to small cities, cooling systems that require millions of gallons of water, servers built from rare minerals mined under exploitative conditions, and land acquired in locations chosen for cheap energy and weak regulation.

Hao's reporting documents specific cases. In Chile, Google's data centre consumed water in an arid region where farmers and residents competed for the same resource — and the full extent of the consumption was obscured by confidentiality agreements between the company and local authorities. (Hao later publicly corrected an overstated estimate in *Empire of AI* — the corrected numbers remain alarming.) In Iowa, Microsoft and Meta's data centres consumed energy that drove up electricity costs for local residents. In Northern Virginia, the concentration of data centres strained the regional electrical grid. In each case, the pattern is the same: the AI company captures the compute value while the local community bears the environmental cost.

The mineral supply chain extends the extraction further. The servers that power AI run on chips manufactured from rare earth elements, cobalt, lithium, and other minerals — many sourced from mines in the DRC, Chile, and Australia under conditions that include child labour, environmental destruction, and worker exploitation. The full supply chain — from mine to chip to server to data centre to model to user — constitutes a resource extraction pipeline analogous to the extraction pipelines of historical empires.

For development practitioners, the resource dimension is essential because data centre siting decisions are increasingly being made in Global South countries that are being invited to "participate in the AI economy" by hosting infrastructure. These invitations must be evaluated with clear eyes: what resources will the community provide? At what cost? Who captures the value?

## How to Apply
1. **Materialise the AI.** "When AI is presented as capability, software, or intelligence, ask: what is the physical infrastructure? How many data centres? How much energy? How much water? Where are the minerals sourced? The materiality reveals the political economy."
2. **Map the resource geography.** "Where are the data centres located? Why were those locations chosen? The answer — cheap energy, abundant water, weak regulation, available land — reveals the resource-frontier logic of AI extraction."
3. **Apply to data centre proposals.** "When a company proposes to build a data centre in your context, demand: independent environmental impact assessment, binding resource-use limits, transparent reporting, and community consent. The company's investment projections are marketing; the community's resource costs are permanent."
4. **Trace the mineral supply chain.** "The servers are built from minerals. Where are the minerals sourced? Under what conditions? The full supply chain — mine to chip to server to model — constitutes the material basis of the AI empire."

## Examples
**Situation:** A country in Southern Africa is offered a data centre investment by a major cloud provider. The company promises 200 permanent jobs and $150 million in investment. The proposed site is near a river that supplies water to agricultural communities downstream.
**Application:** "Before celebrating the investment, conduct the resource accounting. Energy: how much electricity will the data centre consume? What is the current generation capacity and who will bear the cost of expansion or diversion? Water: how much water will the cooling systems require? What is the river's current allocation and what are the downstream communities' existing water rights? Land: what is the current use of the proposed site and what community consent process is required? Then assess the value distribution: the 200 permanent jobs are real but modest for a $150 million facility (data centres are capital-intensive, not labour-intensive). The compute value — the revenue from serving global cloud customers — flows to the company's headquarters and shareholders, not to the local economy. The community provides the resources (water, energy, land); the company captures the value (compute revenue). This is the resource-frontier logic of the AI empire: resources extracted from peripheral locations, value concentrated in the metropolitan centre. If the government proceeds, it should negotiate aggressively: binding water-use limits with penalties, energy-cost guarantees for local users, community benefit-sharing agreements, and transparent environmental reporting."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Exaggerate beyond documented evidence.
**Why:** Hao herself corrected an overstated figure in *Empire of AI*. Reportorial integrity requires using verified numbers, not inflated claims. The documented reality is alarming enough.

**Don't:** Present resource extraction as the only issue.
**Why:** Resource extraction is one dimension of the AI empire alongside labour exploitation, capital concentration, and ideological self-justification. The full analysis requires all four.
