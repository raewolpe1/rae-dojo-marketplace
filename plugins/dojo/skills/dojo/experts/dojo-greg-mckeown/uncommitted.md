# Uncommitted — The Discipline of Not Starting

## When to Use

Use this when you are considering taking on something new and feel the familiar pull toward yes. Use it when an opportunity is genuinely attractive but you are not certain it serves your essential priorities. Use it when you are about to commit to something primarily because it seems good, or because someone you respect is asking, rather than because it is the highest use of your time and energy.

## Fails When

The uncommitted discipline fails when applied rigidly as an excuse to avoid anything difficult or new. Essentialism is not a philosophy of comfort — it is a philosophy of deliberate choice. Some of the most essential things you could pursue will be demanding, uncertain, or unfamiliar. The relevant question is not "is this easy?" but "is this essential?" Refusing all new commitments produces stagnation, not focus.

## Core Concept

Most people have a commitment problem that operates in two stages. The first stage is adding commitments over time until the load becomes unmanageable. The second stage is attempting to eliminate enough of the accumulated load to restore functionality. McKeown's insight is that it is far easier and less costly to not start non-essential commitments than to stop them once they are underway.

This is because starting a commitment creates momentum. You build relationships around it. You develop identity connected to it. You make promises to others that depend on its continuation. Other people's plans begin to rely on your involvement. The longer a commitment runs, the more of these dependencies accumulate, and the more costly it becomes to exit. The window of lowest cost is before you start.

**The "hell yes or no" principle.** McKeown draws on Derek Sivers' formulation: if the answer is not a clear and enthusiastic yes, it should be no. The reasoning is that most people use an implicit threshold of "reasonably good" for what they agree to. But if you are already close to capacity — which most people are — saying yes to something merely good crowds out the possibility of doing something essential well. The threshold should be set much higher than most people set it.

**The sunk cost pre-emption.** One specific value of the uncommitted discipline is that it prevents the sunk cost trap before it is set. Once you have invested time, money, or identity in something, the bias toward continuing it is powerful. By applying rigorous scrutiny before committing, you avoid the situation of having to argue against your own prior investment.

**The 90 percent rule applied to new commitments.** McKeown's 90 percent rule states that if you cannot score something at least 90 out of 100 on your explicit criteria, the answer is no. Applied to new opportunities: before committing, write down the three or four qualities an ideal opportunity in this category would have. If the opportunity before you does not meet most of those criteria at a high level, decline — regardless of how good it appears in absolute terms.

**The pause as a practice.** Many non-essential commitments are accepted in the moment under social pressure before the person has had time to think about whether they actually want to do them. McKeown recommends building in a pause: "Let me check my schedule and get back to you." This is not evasion — it is the creation of space in which an actual decision can be made rather than a reflexive yes.

**Opportunity cost made visible.** When evaluating a new commitment, the full question is not "is this worth doing?" but "is this worth doing more than the best alternative use of the same time and energy?" Non-essentialists evaluate opportunities in isolation. Essentialists evaluate them in comparison. Making the opportunity cost explicit — "If I do this, I will not be doing [X]" — produces different and better decisions.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify your current capacity honestly.** Before evaluating any new commitment, assess your current load against your true capacity. This provides the baseline for whether anything new — even excellent things — can be taken on without compromising existing essential work.

2. **Apply the 90 percent rule.** Write down two or three explicit criteria that an ideal new commitment in this category would meet. Score the opportunity against those criteria. If it does not score 90 or above, the answer is no regardless of how attractive it appears in other ways.

3. **Name what would be displaced.** For any new commitment you are considering, identify specifically what would receive less time and attention if you accepted. This makes the trade-off concrete and tests whether the new thing is actually more valuable than what it displaces.

4. **Build in a pause.** Practice responding to new requests with "let me check and come back to you" rather than yes in the moment. Use the pause to apply steps 2 and 3 before committing.

5. **Watch for the compelling-but-not-essential category.** The hardest uncommitted discipline is with opportunities that are genuinely good — interesting, flattering, connected to things you care about — but not at the level of your essential priorities. These require the most discipline because the usual reason to say no (lack of interest) is absent. The reason to say no is simply that it is not essential enough.

6. **Communicate your decision clearly.** A decision not to commit is only complete when communicated. Vague non-responses create the same ambiguity as vague yeses. Decline clearly and early.

## Examples

**Example 1: The board invitation**
A director receives an invitation to join the board of a well-regarded nonprofit. The cause is one she supports. Several people she respects are involved. She is flattered to be asked. Applying the uncommitted discipline: she identifies her explicit criteria for board commitments — strategic relevance to her work, ability to contribute distinctively rather than generically, alignment with her three-year priorities. The nonprofit scores well on the first criterion and poorly on the other two. She declines clearly and early, expressing genuine respect for the organisation. She does not spend the next three years managing a commitment that consumed twelve days per year without serving her essential direction.

**Example 2: The conference keynote**
A researcher receives an invitation to deliver a keynote at a large conference — a real professional honour. The preparation would take approximately two weeks. He applies the opportunity cost question: what would not happen in those two weeks? The answer is a significant piece of writing that has been his genuine priority for six months and has been displaced repeatedly by attractive opportunities exactly like this one. He declines the keynote. The writing gets done.

## Anti-Patterns

**The flattery override.** Accepting commitments primarily because being asked is pleasing — a keynote, a board seat, an advisory role — without applying any explicit test of whether the commitment serves essential priorities. Flattery is information about how others perceive you; it is not a reliable guide to what you should do.

**The FOMO commitment.** Accepting something because declining feels like missing an opportunity, even when there is no clear articulation of what the opportunity is or how it serves your priorities. Opportunities to do non-essential things are not net losses when declined — they are space preserved for essential things.

**The deferred decision.** Saying "maybe later" to something that should be a clear no, in order to avoid the discomfort of declining now. Deferred decisions occupy mental space, create ambiguity for others, and typically result in a more difficult conversation later.
