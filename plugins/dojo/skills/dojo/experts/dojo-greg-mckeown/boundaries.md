# Boundaries — Proactive Protection of the Essential

## When to Use

Use this when you find yourself repeatedly saying yes to things you wish you had declined. Use it when your time is constantly interrupted or claimed by others before you can invest it in your own priorities. Use it when you feel resentful about commitments you technically agreed to but did not genuinely choose.

## Fails When

Boundary-setting fails when it is used as a defensive reflex rather than a positive design. If your only use of boundaries is to protect yourself from people you find difficult, you are missing the proactive dimension. Boundaries should define what you are protecting space for, not merely what you are protecting space from. Without that positive content, boundaries feel arbitrary to others and eventually erode.

## Core Concept

McKeown draws a distinction between two types of people in relation to requests and demands from others. The first type responds to each request individually, evaluating it on its merits and deciding case by case. The second type has pre-decided, at a more general level, what kinds of things they do and do not do — and applies that pre-decision consistently when specific requests arrive.

The first approach is exhausting and inconsistent. It places the full cognitive and social burden of each refusal on the moment of the request, when social pressure is highest and information is lowest. The second approach moves the decision upstream: you decide once, in a calm moment with full information about your priorities, what your boundaries are. Then you apply them consistently, without having to re-evaluate every individual case.

**Boundaries as standards, not rules.** McKeown resists framing boundaries as rigid prohibitions. They are better understood as expressions of a standard — a description of what you are committed to. "I don't take on work that doesn't contribute to [my essential priorities]" is a standard. It is flexible enough to accommodate genuine exceptions while providing consistent guidance for the routine case.

**The cost of boundarylessness.** When you lack clear boundaries, others fill the vacuum with their own agendas. This is not malicious — it is simply how organisations and social systems work. People request what they need. If you have not proactively allocated your time and energy, those requests will allocate it for you. McKeown notes that boundarylessness feels like generosity in the short term but produces resentment and depletion in the medium term.

**Clarity as a form of care.** One of McKeown's more counterintuitive arguments is that clear boundaries are a form of respect for others. A vague yes — "I'll try to get to that" — creates false hope and ambiguity that costs the other person time and planning. A clear no, delivered early and without drama, allows them to seek another solution promptly. Clarity is not unkindness; delayed and ambiguous responses are often the more damaging option for everyone involved.

**Boundaries and the social contract.** McKeown acknowledges that clear limits can create friction, especially in cultures that value apparent flexibility and responsiveness. The solution is not to abandon limits but to make them part of an explicit conversation about priorities. When others understand what you are protecting and why, limits read as commitment rather than selfishness.

**Pre-deciding the decision.** The most practical implementation of limits in McKeown's framework is what he calls pre-deciding: before you are in a specific situation, decide what you will do. "When someone asks me to join a new committee, I will say I can't take on new standing commitments right now." "When a client asks for a scope extension, I will ask them which current deliverable they want to defer." The pre-decision means that when the situation arises, you are applying a principle rather than negotiating with yourself under social pressure.

## How to Apply

1. **Name what you are protecting.** Before setting any limit, articulate what you are protecting it for. "I protect my mornings for deep work." "I protect my evenings for family." "I protect my strategic capacity for our three core priorities." Limits without a positive content are hard to maintain and hard to explain.

2. **Identify your most common boundary-crossing situations.** Where do your commitments most consistently exceed your intentions? Recurring meeting requests? Scope extensions? Last-minute urgent asks? Identify the pattern rather than treating each instance as unique.

3. **Pre-decide your response to each pattern.** For each common situation, write a specific response you will give. Practice it until it is natural. "I'm fully committed right now but could revisit in [timeframe]." "That's not in my lane — the right person for that is [X]." The response should be true, brief, and redirect rather than simply refuse.

4. **Apply consistently.** The value of a limit is in its consistency. Case-by-case evaluation under social pressure reliably produces worse decisions than pre-decided principles applied consistently.

5. **Communicate proactively where possible.** Where you have ongoing relationships, communicate your standards proactively rather than waiting for a request to decline. "I should tell you that I've restructured my work to protect [X] — so for things that fall into that category, I'll be saying no more consistently than I used to."

6. **Review periodically.** Circumstances change. A limit that was appropriate in one season may not be in another. Review your established limits alongside your priorities, and update when genuinely necessary — not when social pressure accumulates, but when your priorities have actually shifted.

## Examples

**Example 1: The open calendar**
A manager's calendar is consistently taken over by requests from colleagues and direct reports. She intends to do deep analytical work but never has uninterrupted time. Applying pre-decided limits: she blocks her first two hours every day as focus time, visible on her calendar with a note that she does not take meetings in that window. She communicates this proactively to her team. Initially there is friction; within three weeks, colleagues adapt their request patterns to her available times. Her deep work happens consistently for the first time in years.

**Example 2: The scope creep pattern**
A consultant repeatedly finds that projects expand beyond their contracted scope, consuming time and energy he has not budgeted. Rather than negotiating each expansion individually, he pre-decides his standard response: "I can do that — it would replace [X deliverable] or we can scope it as an addition. Which would you prefer?" This response makes the trade-off explicit without being confrontational. Over six months, scope expansions decrease significantly because clients understand they require a conversation rather than a casual ask.

## Anti-Patterns

**The apologetic limit.** Declining a request with so many qualifications, apologies, and expressions of regret that the message is unclear and the social cost nearly equals that of saying yes. A clear, brief, warm no is more respectful than a hedge.

**The selective limit.** Applying limits rigorously to people you find difficult while making exceptions for people you want to please. This defeats the purpose. Limits must apply to the situation type, not the person asking, or they provide no systematic protection.

**The permanent limit.** Setting limits as though they are permanent structures rather than living decisions. Limits should be reviewed alongside priorities. A limit that made sense in one season may not serve in another, and rigid adherence to outdated limits produces the same problem as having none at all.
