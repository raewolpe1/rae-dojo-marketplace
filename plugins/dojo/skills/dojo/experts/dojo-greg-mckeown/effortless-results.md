# Effortless Results — Residual Value from Single Investments

## When to Use

Use this when you are doing the same essential work repeatedly without the cumulative investment producing proportionately more. Use it when you want your past efforts to compound rather than simply accumulate. Use it when you are designing a system, a resource, or a piece of work and want to build in residual value — the capacity to keep generating returns beyond the original effort.

## Fails When

Effortless results are not available in every domain or for every kind of work. Some essential work inherently requires presence and direct effort each time — it cannot be systematised or automated without losing what makes it valuable. McKeown's concept applies best to knowledge work, content, systems, and relationships, not to every form of contribution. Attempting to automate or systematise work that inherently requires presence is not effortless results; it is non-presence.

## Core Concept

Effortless results is the third tier of McKeown's Effortless framework, following effortless state and effortless action. Where state concerns inner conditions and action concerns how you do essential work, results concerns whether the outputs of your work continue producing value after the work is done.

The animating question is simple: *can I do this once and benefit many times?* McKeown calls this residual results — the difference between linear results (where output is directly proportional to input each time) and compounding results (where earlier investments create leverage that reduces the cost of future returns).

**Teaching versus doing.** The clearest example of effortless results is the distinction between doing something and teaching someone else to do it. Teaching has a higher upfront cost but creates returns that compound over time — the student applies the learning across dozens of contexts you will never personally encounter. Doing it yourself produces linear returns; teaching produces residual ones. McKeown notes that people often avoid teaching because it takes longer initially, without recognising that the cumulative return on teaching is far greater than the cumulative return on doing.

**Creating once, using many times.** Another form of effortless results is creating knowledge objects — frameworks, guides, processes, templates — that can be used repeatedly without reinvestment. Every time you find yourself explaining the same thing twice, solving the same problem twice, or creating similar material again, the question is whether a one-time investment in creating a reusable resource would eliminate future reinvestment. The guide, the template, the recorded explanation — these are investments that pay residual returns.

**Automating the repeatable.** For work that is genuinely repeatable and does not require judgment, automation creates effortless results by converting recurring effort into a one-time setup investment. The automation continues to run; the effort does not recur.

**Building relationships that compound.** McKeown extends the concept beyond knowledge work to relationships. Some relationships produce linear value — each interaction requires equivalent effort and produces equivalent return. Others compound — early investments in trust, in generosity, in genuine support, produce returns that grow over time and require less maintenance per unit of value. The relationships worth investing most deeply in are those with the greatest compounding potential.

**Trusting high-integrity processes.** At the systems level, effortless results require building processes you can trust — so that the process itself does the work rather than requiring your continuous oversight. A process you trust can run; a process you don't trust requires your attention every time it runs, eliminating the effortless character of the result. High-integrity processes are therefore not just an operational concern; they are a prerequisite for effortless results.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify your recurring efforts.** What do you explain repeatedly? What problems do you solve again and again? What work do you produce variants of regularly? These are candidates for conversion into residual results.

2. **Ask the "teach it" question.** For each recurring effort, ask: could I invest in teaching this once and have others handle it from there? What would be required to teach it well enough that the teaching produced better results than my recurring direct effort?

3. **Build the reusable resource.** For knowledge that you hold and transfer verbally each time, create the written or recorded version once. The guide, the template, the recorded walkthrough. The upfront cost is higher; the ongoing cost drops toward zero.

4. **Automate what does not require judgment.** Map your recurring tasks and identify which require genuine judgment and which do not. Non-judgment tasks are candidates for automation. Implement the automation once; benefit from it repeatedly.

5. **Invest in compounding relationships.** Identify the relationships in your professional and personal life with the highest compounding potential — those where early investment produces ongoing trust and mutual support. Invest disproportionately in those relationships relative to their current value, because their future value is what matters.

6. **Build processes you can trust.** For any system you create, ask what would be required for you to trust it to run without your continuous oversight. If the answer is "nothing — I just need to let it run," you have an effortless result. If the answer is "I need to check it every time," the process is not yet complete.

## Examples

**Example 1: The repeated onboarding**
A team leader onboards a new team member every three months. Each time, she spends three days walking the new person through everything verbally. After two years of this, she builds a complete onboarding guide — written documentation, recorded walkthroughs, structured first-week programme. The guide takes her two weeks to build. Afterwards, onboarding new people requires two hours of her time rather than three days. The two-week investment pays returns across every future hire.

**Example 2: The consultant's framework**
A consultant repeatedly solves similar problems for different clients — each time from scratch, producing customised analysis. He realises the analysis has a repeatable structure that he rebuilds mentally each time. He invests in building the framework explicitly: the questions, the diagnostic steps, the output format. The framework takes two weeks to properly codify. Afterwards, his analysis quality improves (because the framework is better than his on-the-fly reconstruction), his speed doubles, and he can train associates to apply it without his involvement.

## Anti-Patterns

**Residualising what requires presence.** Automating, delegating, or systematising work that inherently requires your direct involvement and judgment — and finding that the output is degraded because you removed what made it valuable. Not everything should be residualised; the art is identifying what can be without loss of quality.

**Never investing in the reusable version.** Continuing to do the recurring work linearly because building the reusable version takes longer upfront — even when the cumulative cost of repetition has already exceeded the cost of building the reusable version once. This is a calculation failure: the compounding math consistently favours the investment, but the urgency of the immediate task crowds out the less urgent but higher-value investment.

**Building processes you don't trust.** Creating systems and then monitoring them continuously because you don't actually believe they'll work correctly without oversight. This produces the worst of both worlds: the overhead of the system plus the effort of oversight. Either build the trust (through better process design and testing) or don't build the system.
