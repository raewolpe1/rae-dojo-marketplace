# The Essentialist Mindset — Less But Better

## When to Use

Use this when you feel overwhelmed by competing demands and can't identify where to focus. Use it when you have said yes to too many things and your execution is suffering across the board. Use it when you sense your current trajectory is not leading where you actually want to go, but the path feels too entangled to change.

## Fails When

The essentialist mindset is least effective when applied only once. It is not a clean-up exercise. Applied as a one-time audit, it produces a temporary reprieve before the non-essential floods back in. It also fails in genuinely coercive environments where the person has no meaningful discretion over their commitments — the framework assumes some degree of agency.

## Core Concept

The essentialist mindset is not a productivity technique. It is a fundamental reorientation of how you relate to your time, attention, and energy.

Most people operate from a non-essentialist posture without realising it. The non-essentialist believes that everything is important, that more is always better, and that saying yes creates more opportunity than saying no. This posture feels virtuous — responsive, hardworking, helpful. But it has a concealed cost: when everything is a priority, nothing is. Effort is dispersed across many things and produces diluted results everywhere.

The essentialist believes three things, each of which the non-essentialist denies:

**First: I have a choice.** The non-essentialist surrenders choice to social pressure, habit, and FOMO — the fear of missing out. When someone asks for help, saying no feels impossible. When an interesting project appears, saying no feels like lost opportunity. The non-essentialist does not consciously decide to take on more; they simply fail to decline, and their commitments accumulate by default. The essentialist insists that every yes is a choice and every no is a choice, and that refusing to choose is itself a choice — usually a poor one.

**Second: Almost everything is noise.** The world generates an overwhelming supply of apparently good ideas, urgent requests, attractive opportunities, and reasonable demands. Most of them are not essential. Pareto's insight applies here with force: a small minority of inputs — usually around 20% — produce the vast majority of meaningful outputs. The essentialist does not try to do everything slightly better; they try to identify the vital few and give those the resources they deserve.

**Third: Trade-offs are real.** The non-essentialist wants to believe that clever enough management can eliminate trade-offs — that sufficiently good systems will allow them to do everything. The essentialist accepts that trade-offs are not a problem to be solved but a reality to be designed around. Every yes is funded by a no somewhere. The question is not whether you will trade off; you already are. The question is whether you will trade off consciously and in favour of what matters, or unconsciously in favour of what is urgent or socially demanded.

The phrase "less but better" — Dieter Rams' design principle — captures the essentialist stance precisely. The goal is not less for its own sake, as minimalism sometimes implies. The goal is to concentrate investment in what produces the most value, which requires eliminating investment in what does not.

The essentialist does not produce less. They produce more, in the things that matter, by doing fewer things. This is counterintuitive in a culture that rewards busyness and visibility. The essentialist must be willing to appear less productive by conventional measures in order to be more productive by meaningful ones.

This mindset shift has a practical prerequisite: **creating space to think**. The non-essentialist is perpetually reactive — responding to the last email, the latest request, the most recent urgency. This state of perpetual reaction leaves no room to ask whether the right things are being done at all. McKeown argues that time for exploration, reflection, and discernment is not a luxury to be earned by completing the inbox — it is the precondition for knowing what should be in the inbox.

## How to Apply

1. **Audit your current commitments.** List everything you have said yes to — projects, roles, recurring meetings, relationships, obligations. Do not filter as you write. Just inventory.

2. **Apply the essential filter.** For each item, ask: if I were not already doing this, would I actively choose to start? If the honest answer is no, the item is a candidate for elimination or reduction.

3. **Look for the vital few.** Among the things that survive the first filter, ask: which of these, if I gave them full attention and resources, would produce the most meaningful results? These are the essential few.

4. **Identify what is crowding them out.** The vital few are almost never the bottleneck. What limits them is everything else claiming the same time and attention. The essentialist work is removing that competition.

5. **Make one essential change.** Do not attempt to overhaul everything. Essentialism applied all at once tends to collapse into a temporary purge followed by rebound. Instead, identify the single most important thing to stop doing or start saying no to, and do that first.

6. **Build a decision rule.** Create a simple criterion that will help you evaluate future requests before they become commitments. McKeown's 90% rule is one option: if something scores less than 9 out of 10 on what matters most to you, the answer is no.

## Examples

**Example 1: The over-committed professional**
A senior manager has twelve direct reports, sits on four cross-functional committees, leads two special projects, and has taken on mentoring three junior employees. She is technically competent at all of it, but stretched thin — meetings are frequent, preparation is rushed, and she rarely has time to think. When asked to identify her three most essential contributions, she finds it genuinely difficult. She has been optimising for responsiveness rather than impact. Applying the essentialist mindset, she maps her commitments against her actual mandate and identifies two committee memberships that were assumed rather than chosen. Stepping back from those frees seven hours a week. She reinvests those hours in her two special projects, which are the ones with the highest leverage, and execution quality visibly improves. The essential filter did not tell her what to do — it told her what to stop.

**Example 2: The strategic inflection point**
An entrepreneur has built a services business that works. Three potential expansion directions have emerged: a product, a training programme, and a geographic expansion. All three are viable. None is obviously wrong. The non-essentialist response is to explore all three simultaneously, keeping options open. The essentialist response is to ask: which of these, if it succeeded, would most move me toward the future I actually want? Only one answer is compelling when examined honestly. The others are attractive but not essential. Choosing one and actively deciding not to pursue the others for at least twelve months concentrates resources and produces faster, cleaner progress.

## Anti-Patterns

**The everything-is-essential trap.** When asked what could be eliminated, many people find that everything on their list is genuinely important. This is usually a sign that the filter being applied is "is this important?" rather than "is this essential?" Importance is a low bar. Essentiality is a high one. If your list has no obvious cuts, the problem is the filter, not the list.

**The one-time purge.** Treating essentialism as a periodic clean-up rather than an ongoing mindset. The non-essential returns quickly unless the mindset — and the decision rules — are maintained continuously. Quarterly reviews and recurring filters are necessary infrastructure.

**Treating the mindset as a licence to disengage.** The essentialist is not someone who does less out of laziness or avoidance. They are someone who does less in order to do the vital things better. If the outcome of applying the mindset is reduced output on the essential things, the mindset has been misapplied.
