# Trade-offs — The Central Essentialist Practice

## When to Use

Use this when you are facing a choice between two or more genuinely good options and feel pressure to choose all of them. Use it when you are postponing a decision by trying to keep all options open. Use it when you sense that your inability to commit clearly to one direction is limiting your execution across all of them.

## Fails When

The trade-off framework fails when applied to genuinely false dilemmas — situations where what appears to be a binary choice can actually be avoided through reframing, sequencing, or creative problem-solving. Not every choice is a true trade-off. The essentialist applies the framework where constraints are real, not as a reason to avoid exploring alternatives.

## Core Concept

The non-essentialist treats trade-offs as problems to be solved. The essentialist treats them as reality to be designed around.

This is not a small distinction. If trade-offs are problems to be solved, the goal is to eliminate them — to be efficient enough, clever enough, or resourceful enough to avoid having to give up one good thing for another. This is the logic behind "why can't I have it all?" and "I just need better systems." It is seductive and ultimately self-defeating, because it denies the structural constraint that makes strategy necessary in the first place.

If trade-offs are reality to be designed around, the goal is to make them consciously and well. Which trade-offs do you want to make? Which things will you give up, and in favour of what? These become design questions rather than failure states.

McKeown's most useful phrase here is: **"You can do anything, but not everything."** The constraint is not on possibility — in principle, you could pursue any of the options available to you. The constraint is on simultaneity. You cannot pursue all of them at the same quality level, with full attention and investment, at the same time. Accepting this is the beginning of strategic thinking.

Trade-offs operate at multiple scales:

**At the level of time:** Every hour given to one thing is an hour not given to anything else. This is elementary, but its implication — that your schedule is a statement of values, whether or not you intend it to be — is frequently ignored.

**At the level of attention:** Attention does not divide cleanly. Shallow attention spread across many things degrades quality across all of them. Deep attention concentrated on fewer things produces qualitatively better work.

**At the level of identity:** Saying yes to a certain type of work, relationship, or commitment sends signals about who you are and what you value. Saying yes to too many types creates identity incoherence that makes it harder to attract the opportunities most aligned with your real direction.

**At the organisational level:** McKeown discusses Southwest Airlines as an example of deliberate trade-offs: the company explicitly chose to be the low-cost carrier and to give up everything that conflicted with that — no meals, no seat assignments, no connections to other carriers. The commitment to the trade-offs is what produces competitive clarity.

The key discipline in the trade-off framework is **making the choice explicit and owning it**. Most people make trade-offs implicitly — they simply run out of time for some things, and those things do not happen. The essentialist makes the choice before the deadline and with full awareness of what is being sacrificed. This requires a particular kind of intellectual honesty: acknowledging that choosing one thing means not choosing another, and that this is not a failure but a decision.

McKeown also notes that **deliberately chosen trade-offs often feel worse in advance than they do afterward**. The anticipated loss is vivid; the benefits of focus accumulate quietly over time. The essentialist has to be willing to endure this asymmetry — to accept present regret in exchange for future coherence.

## How to Apply

1. **Name the real choice.** When facing a decision between multiple options, resist the impulse to add more options or to hedge. Write out the actual alternatives as mutually exclusive choices. "I can do A, or I can do B. If I do A, I cannot also do B at full quality."

2. **Name what you would be giving up.** For each option, write specifically what you would not be doing or what would be degraded if you chose it. Vague trade-offs are easy to avoid. Specific ones are harder to ignore.

3. **Ask which loss is more acceptable.** You will lose something either way. The question is not how to avoid loss but which loss is more consistent with what you are trying to build. Frame it as: "Which regret can I live with more easily in a year's time?"

4. **Make the choice before you are forced to.** Delayed decisions make themselves by default — the most urgent option wins, regardless of whether it is most important. Making trade-offs deliberately and early produces better decisions than making them reactively.

5. **Commit fully to the chosen option.** Half-hearted commitment to a decision often attempts to preserve optionality — you keep one foot in the unchosen path. This undermines execution of the chosen path without actually preserving the alternative. Commit to the trade-off or reconsider the decision.

6. **Review the trade-off periodically.** Circumstances change. What was the right trade-off six months ago may not be optimal now. Build a habit of reviewing significant trade-offs and being willing to choose differently when conditions have genuinely shifted.

## Examples

**Example 1: The career decision**
A professional is offered two roles simultaneously: one is a stretch promotion within her current organisation, high visibility, higher stress, excellent compensation. The other is a lateral move to a smaller organisation where she would have significantly more autonomy and do work that is more aligned with her long-term interest. Both are good options. She attempts to keep both in play by delaying her response and asking for extensions. This strategy fails — one organisation requires an answer. Applying the trade-off framework: she names what she gives up in each direction. Choosing the promotion means giving up autonomy and alignment for the foreseeable future; the long-term opportunity may still exist, but it will be delayed. Choosing the lateral move means giving up compensation growth and visibility in the short term. She asks which loss she can live with more easily. The answer is clear when she stops trying to avoid the choice.

**Example 2: The product roadmap**
A product team has six features that have been requested and are each defensible. Stakeholders want all six. Engineering capacity allows three, at quality, within the quarter. The non-essentialist approach is to attempt all six at reduced scope — a classic strategy that typically produces six mediocre half-features rather than three excellent complete ones. The essentialist approach is to make the trade-off explicit: which three features, if excellent, would produce the most value? The other three are not abandoned — they are consciously deferred with a rationale. The team commits to the three with full resources.

## Anti-Patterns

**The false compromise.** Attempting to reduce the magnitude of trade-offs by doing partial versions of multiple things rather than full versions of one. This usually produces the worst of both worlds — you give up the benefits of concentrated investment in either direction while retaining the costs of splitting attention.

**Treating "I'll do it later" as a decision.** Deferring a trade-off decision is not a neutral holding position. Time spent in deferral is time spent without commitment to any option, which typically means drifting toward the default — what is easiest or most expected rather than what is most essential.

**Agonising over sunk costs.** Previous investment in an option — time, money, identity — should not control current decisions about trade-offs. The relevant question is always forward-looking: given where I am now, which choice produces the most value from here? Sunk cost thinking prevents this clarity.
