# Discernment — Choosing Among the Good

## When to Use

Use this when you have completed exploration and have a range of genuinely good options, and now need to determine which are truly essential. Use it when all the options before you are legitimate and valuable and yet you cannot pursue them all well. Use it when you are struggling to distinguish between what is good and what is best.

## Fails When

Discernment fails when it is used to postpone commitment indefinitely — when the desire to make the perfect choice prevents you from making any choice. Good discernment results in clear commitment. If your discernment process produces ongoing ambiguity rather than eventual clarity, it is functioning as avoidance rather than wisdom. At some point, the essentialist chooses.

## Core Concept

The hardest version of the essentialist choice is not choosing between good and bad. It is choosing between good and good — between options that are all defensible, all attractive in some dimension, and all supported by at least some of the people you respect.

Discernment is the capacity to distinguish, within a field of genuinely good options, which ones rise to the level of essential. It is harder than it sounds, and it requires three capabilities that are each individually learnable: the ability to apply explicit criteria rather than vague feeling, the capacity to tolerate the discomfort of eliminating good things, and the willingness to commit to a choice that cannot be perfectly validated in advance.

**The clarifying question.** McKeown's most reliable discernment tool is the question: "What is the highest and best use I can make of my time and energy right now?" Not "what is good?" but "what is the best possible use of what I have to offer?" This question has a comparative dimension — it asks not just whether something is valuable but whether it is the most valuable thing available.

**The one-sentence test.** If you cannot describe your essential direction in one clear, compelling sentence, you have not yet achieved discernment. The discipline of condensing to one sentence forces you to confront the trade-offs you have been deferring. What are you about, at core, right now? If multiple sentences are required, you may have more than one essential direction — which likely means you have not yet made the essential choice.

**The regret minimisation frame.** McKeown borrows from Jeff Bezos the practice of regret minimisation: projecting forward to the end of your life and asking which choices you would most regret. This frame cuts through the noise of present social pressure and surface attractiveness. Many things that feel important in the present do not feel regrettable in retrospect; many things that feel difficult to choose in the present — the bold direction, the authentic commitment, the genuinely essential priority — appear obvious and clearly correct from a longer time horizon.

**Distinguishing essential from merely important.** McKeown makes a distinction between important and essential. Important means valuable, worth doing, justifiable. Essential means the highest-value use of what you uniquely bring. Many things are important; very few are essential. Discernment is the practice of working your way from "this is important" to "this specifically is essential" by comparing important options against each other and asking which rises highest.

**The test of distinctive contribution.** One reliable marker of the essential is that it represents what you specifically, given your particular capabilities and position, can contribute that others cannot. Generic contributions — things anyone equally qualified could do — are often important but rarely essential. The essential tends to be distinctive: it sits at the intersection of what you do well, what the situation specifically needs, and what would not happen or not happen as well without your particular involvement.

**The emotional signal.** Genuine essentials tend to produce a quality of engagement that non-essentials do not: a sense of full presence, genuine curiosity, and energy that sustains itself rather than depleting. McKeown is careful not to oversimplify this — not everything essential feels energising at every moment, and some non-essential things produce temporary excitement. But as a general signal, the quality of engagement with something over time is meaningful data about whether it is essential for you.

## How to Apply

1. **List your current candidates.** After exploration, you should have a set of options that have passed an initial filter. Write them all down explicitly. Make the field of choice visible.

2. **Apply the distinctive contribution test.** For each candidate, ask: "Is this something I and I specifically can do, or could anyone at my level do this equally well?" Essential things tend to be at the intersection of distinctive capability and genuine need. Generic work, however important, is rarely essential.

3. **Apply the one-sentence test.** Attempt to write a single sentence that describes your essential direction and encompasses the candidates you are considering. If the sentence requires multiple clauses that point in different directions, the candidates may not be mutually coherent — and you may need to make a more fundamental choice first.

4. **Use the regret minimisation frame.** Project forward ten years and ask which of your current candidates you would most regret not having pursued. This often produces unexpected clarity by removing the weight of present social pressure.

5. **Sit with discomfort.** Genuine discernment sometimes requires holding the question open for longer than feels comfortable, without forcing a resolution. This is different from avoidance: you are actively engaged with the question, returning to it, gathering information, but not forcing a premature answer. Real discernment has a different texture from deferral.

6. **Commit and communicate.** When discernment produces clarity, the process is not complete until you have committed to the essential and communicated that commitment — to others and to yourself. Uncommitted clarity is a common failure mode: you know what is essential but have not yet acted on that knowledge.

## Examples

**Example 1: The career crossroads**
A senior analyst has two clear options: take a leadership role at her current organisation, or take an individual contributor role at a smaller company doing work that is more aligned with her core capabilities. Both are good. Applying the discernment framework: the distinctive contribution test reveals that the leadership role at her current organisation could be filled by several colleagues equally well, whereas the individual contributor role at the smaller company specifically requires her expertise. The regret minimisation test: at sixty-five, which would she more likely regret? The answer is immediate. She takes the role at the smaller company.

**Example 2: The project selection**
A research director has five potential research directions, all of which have been approved and funded at some level. He cannot pursue all five at the depth that would produce excellent work. Applying the discernment framework: he maps each against his distinctive capability — what does he specifically add to each that could not be replicated by his team without him? Two of the five genuinely require his particular expertise. He concentrates his personal involvement on those two and delegates leadership of the others to team members who are well-positioned for them.

## Anti-Patterns

**Analysis paralysis.** Using the discernment process as a substitute for decision rather than a pathway to decision. Discernment has a legitimate end: a clear choice. If the process is producing increasing data and decreasing clarity, it may be the process itself that is the problem rather than insufficient information.

**Crowdsourcing discernment.** Consulting so many people about your essential direction that the aggregate of their opinions replaces your own judgment. Other people's input is useful data in the discernment process; it is not a substitute for it. What is essential for you is partly a function of who you specifically are — which means it is not perfectly answerable by people who are not you.

**Choosing what is most approved of.** Selecting the option that garners the most social approval rather than the one that genuinely feels most essential. Approval is pleasant but is a weak proxy for alignment with your actual priorities. The most essential choice is often not the most popular one.
