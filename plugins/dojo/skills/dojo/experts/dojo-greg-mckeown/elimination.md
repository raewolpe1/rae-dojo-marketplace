# Elimination — Removing What Doesn't Belong

## When to Use

Use this when your commitments, projects, or responsibilities have accumulated beyond what you can do well. Use it when you are holding onto things out of habit, sunk cost, or social obligation rather than genuine value. Use it when you know something should stop but you have not yet found the mechanism or courage to stop it.

## Fails When

Elimination applied without discernment becomes blunt cutting rather than strategic pruning. The problem is not all activity — it is non-essential activity. If you have not first done the exploratory work to identify what is truly essential (see exploration.md and discernment.md), elimination may remove things that were actually valuable. Cut with clarity, not exhaustion.

## Core Concept

If trade-offs are the central reality of essentialist thinking, elimination is the central practice. It is not enough to know what matters most — you must actively remove what does not.

McKeown's observation is that most people operate with a mental model of addition: they improve their situation by adding more — more options, more commitments, more tools, more strategies. The essentialist model runs in the opposite direction. You improve by subtracting: by removing the things that consume time, attention, and energy without contributing to the essential few.

**The zero-based thinking prompt.** One of McKeown's most practical tools for elimination is the question: "If I wasn't already doing this, would I still choose to start it?" This bypasses the cognitive bias toward preserving the status quo. Most ongoing commitments persist not because someone would choose them today but because stopping them requires an active decision that no one has gotten around to making. Zero-based thinking makes the continuation of any activity the result of a positive choice rather than inertia.

**The reverse pilot.** Before fully eliminating something, McKeown suggests a reverse pilot: stop doing it quietly for a period and observe what happens. In many cases, the feared consequences do not materialise — the activity was consuming resources without producing results that anyone noticed or depended on. The reverse pilot makes elimination feel less permanent and therefore less psychologically threatening.

**The cost of keeping.** Every non-essential commitment you carry has a cost even when you are not actively working on it. It occupies mental bandwidth. It appears on lists. It generates small anxieties about when you will get to it. It crowds out mental space that would otherwise be available for thinking about the essential. McKeown calls this the hidden cost of non-essential commitments — they cost you whether or not you are actively working on them.

**Sunk cost and the endowment effect.** Two cognitive biases make elimination difficult. Sunk cost: the investment already made in a project or commitment feels like a reason to continue even when the forward-looking case for continuing is weak. The endowment effect: we value things more highly simply because we own or hold them. Both biases inflate the perceived cost of elimination. The essentialist counters them by focusing consistently on forward value: "What will this produce from here?" rather than "What has this cost so far?"

**Boundaries as pre-emptive elimination.** McKeown distinguishes between reactive elimination — stopping things you are already doing — and proactive elimination, which means setting boundaries that prevent non-essential commitments from accumulating in the first place. Reactive elimination is always more costly than proactive boundary-setting. It is easier not to start something than to stop it once started.

**The editing metaphor.** McKeown returns repeatedly to the metaphor of editing: great editors do not just cut words — they clarify and strengthen the essential message by removing what obscures it. The same principle applies to a life or organisation. Elimination is not loss; it is clarification. When you remove what does not belong, what remains becomes more visible and more powerful.

## How to Apply

1. **Conduct a current commitments audit.** List everything you are currently doing, responsible for, or have said yes to. Do not filter — capture everything: standing meetings, ongoing projects, informal promises, recurring tasks, memberships, relationships that require maintenance.

2. **Apply the zero-based question to each item.** For each commitment, ask: "If I weren't already doing this, would I choose to start it today given everything I know?" Items where the honest answer is no are candidates for elimination.

3. **Run a reverse pilot on candidates.** Before eliminating something, stop doing it quietly for two to four weeks. If no one notices and nothing breaks, the case for elimination is strong.

4. **Identify the emotional obstacles.** For each item you know should be eliminated, name the specific reason you have not stopped: obligation to a person, sunk cost in a project, fear of a specific consequence. Making the obstacle explicit makes it easier to address directly.

5. **Create a stopping mechanism.** Elimination requires an active communication or transition in many cases. Draft the email, schedule the conversation, or set the end date. Decisions without mechanisms do not happen.

6. **Set proactive boundaries.** Identify the categories of request most likely to produce non-essential commitments. Establish a default response (a graceful no) before the next request arrives, so elimination becomes proactive rather than reactive.

## Examples

**Example 1: The project graveyard**
A consultant has six active projects, four client relationships requiring regular contact, and three internal initiatives she volunteered for. She is doing none of them well. Applying the zero-based question to the internal initiatives reveals that she would not choose any of them today — they made sense six months ago under different circumstances. She conducts a reverse pilot on one: stops attending the associated meeting and sends no updates for a month. Nothing happens. She formally withdraws from all three initiatives in a single conversation with her manager, freeing roughly four hours per week. The quality of her client work improves within two weeks.

**Example 2: The meeting schedule**
A senior manager has fifteen recurring meetings per week. Many predate his current role. Applying the zero-based question to each: he identifies eight that he attends out of habit or political courtesy rather than contribution or value. He removes himself from four immediately by delegating. He runs a reverse pilot on two more by sending a team member in his place. He reduces his recurring meeting load by nearly half, recovering ten hours per week for deep work.

## Anti-Patterns

**Elimination by exhaustion.** Stopping things because you have no energy left rather than because they are non-essential. This is reactive and undiscriminating — it tends to stop whatever is most recent rather than whatever is least important. True elimination is deliberate and forward-looking.

**Incomplete elimination.** Removing the visible commitment while retaining the underlying obligation — agreeing to "step back" from a project but remaining on the distribution list, available for questions, and present in progress meetings. Partial removal does not produce the benefit of full removal.

**Eliminating and refilling.** Creating space through elimination and immediately filling it with new commitments before the benefits of space have been realised. The purpose of elimination is not to accommodate more commitments — it is to create room for deeper engagement with fewer.
