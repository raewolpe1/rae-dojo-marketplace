# Managerial Capital and Its Depreciation

## When to Use
- **When any capability programme is being designed.** The retention question is almost never asked.
- When management improvement is being treated as a permanent gain.
- When modelling the returns to a capability intervention over time.

## Fails When
- **The subject is physical capital.** Different depreciation, different logic.

## Core Concept

**Management is capital, and it depreciates faster than machinery.**

The structural estimation in "Management as a Technology?" recovers the parameters of managerial capital and finds that **both the depreciation rate and the adjustment costs are larger than for tangible non-managerial capital**. Depreciation lands around **13%** per year. Adjustment cost is estimated at **0.290** — about **50% higher** than the value for capital, if management is allowed the same 50% resale loss as capital.

**Why it depreciates: it is attached to people.** The interpretation offered is that **management capital is tied to the identity of plant managers**. The survey's average plant manager tenure is **6.4 years in the post and 13.0 years in the company** — implying post and company quit rates of roughly **15% to 7%**, which brackets the 13% depreciation estimate almost exactly. The depreciation rate *is* the turnover rate.

**And it was observed directly.** In the **eight-year follow-up to the India experiment, the largest reason for a deterioration in management practices in the treatment plants was the attrition of the plant manager.** The consulting worked. The gains eroded. The dominant cause was that the person who held the practices left.

**What this means for programme design, and it is not subtle.**

**A capability programme with no retention component improves individuals, not firms.** You will pay for a management upgrade, measure it at 12 months, report a success, and watch roughly an eighth of it evaporate every year thereafter — faster if the manager you improved becomes more employable, which is precisely what your programme was designed to achieve. **The better the programme, the faster the leakage.**

**Three design responses.**

1. **Embed the practices in systems, not people.** Documented procedures, data collection routines, standing meeting structures, software. Systems have lower turnover than staff.
2. **Build depth.** If two people hold the practice, one leaving costs half as much. Note that the India experiment's own second-order effect was increased **decentralisation** — more people holding more decisions. That is depth, and it is protective.
3. **Treat leakage into the labour market as an outcome, not a loss** — but say so honestly. If the improved manager takes modern practice to another provincial firm, the province gained even though the treated firm did not. That is a legitimate public rationale. It is *not* the rationale most programmes claim, and it implies different indicators.

**The under-appreciated corollary.** Higher adjustment costs mean management is **slow and expensive to build** as well as quick to lose. This is an argument for intensity and duration over reach — the same conclusion the dose evidence reaches from the other direction.

## How to Apply

1. **Model the decay.** Apply ~13% annual depreciation to any claimed management gain. The 10-year NPV of a capability programme is much smaller than the year-one effect suggests.
2. **Ask the retention question at design.** Who will hold this practice, and what is their tenure?
3. **Embed in systems.** Documentation, routines, software. Not in a person's head.
4. **Build depth deliberately.** Two holders, not one. Decentralisation is protective.
5. **If leakage is the rationale, say so** and measure provincial diffusion rather than treated-firm performance.
6. **Prefer intensity to reach.** High adjustment costs mean thin interventions build nothing.

## Examples

**Situation:** A capability programme reports a management score improvement at 12 months and projects sustained productivity gains through 2035.

**Application:** Apply the depreciation. At roughly 13% a year, tied to the identity of the people holding the practices, less than a quarter of the year-one gain survives a decade absent reinvestment — and the eight-year India follow-up found manager attrition to be the largest single driver of erosion in exactly this kind of intervention. The 2035 projection is therefore not supported by the evidence the programme rests on. Two fixes: model the decay explicitly and re-plan the reinvestment, and redesign the intervention to embed practices in documented systems held by more than one person.

**Result:** A projection that survives scrutiny and a design that decays more slowly.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Project management gains forward as permanent.
**Why:** ~13% annual depreciation, tied to individuals. The eight-year India follow-up watched it happen.

**Don't:** Improve one person and call it a firm-level gain.
**Why:** When they leave, so does the gain. That is the mechanism, not a risk.

**Don't:** Spread a capability budget thinly for reach.
**Why:** Adjustment costs for managerial capital are ~50% higher than for physical capital. Thin doses do not accumulate.
