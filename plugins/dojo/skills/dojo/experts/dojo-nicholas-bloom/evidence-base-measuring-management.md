# Evidence Base — Measuring Management

> **Provenance.** Compiled July 2026 from the World Management Survey papers. Verify before quoting. `[CHECK]` entries are unverified to primary source.

## When to Use
- Designing a firm capability baseline.
- Benchmarking a firm population internationally.
- When "capability" is asserted without measurement.

## Fails When
- **The population is micro or own-account.** The instrument scores monitoring, targets and incentives. There is no structure to score. See `dose-and-population.md`.

## Headline

**Management is a number between 1 and 5. Over 11,000 firms in 34 countries have one. Your firms do not.**

---

## THE EVIDENCE

### The instrument
- **Study:** The Bloom–Van Reenen (BVR) methodology, deployed via the **World Management Survey**.
- **Coverage:** Over **11,000 firms in 34 countries**.
- **Method:** Scores firms from **1 (worst practices) to 5 (best practices)** on specific management practices relating to **monitoring, targets and incentives**. Aggregating yields a basic measure of the use of modern management practices.
- **Finding:** The aggregate measure is **strongly correlated with a wide range of firm performance measures — productivity, profitability and growth.**
- **Caveat:** Correlation at this stage; the causal claim comes from the India experiment. The instrument is designed for firms with a plant, a manager, and staff — its content presupposes an organisation.

### The cross-country picture
- **Study:** Bloom, N., Sadun, R. and Van Reenen, J., "Management as a Technology?", NBER Working Paper 22327 (June 2016, revised October 2017); also HBS WP 16-133, CEPR DP 11312, IZA DP 9987.
- **Coverage:** Over 11,000 firms, 34 countries.
- **Finding:** **Large cross-country differences in the adoption of basic management practices, with the US having the highest size-weighted average management score.**
- **Caveat:** Size-weighted. The unweighted picture differs, and the weighting choice is doing work — it says large US firms are exceptionally well managed, not that every US firm is.

### The distribution, not the average
- **Study:** Bloom, Eifert, Mahajan, McKenzie & Roberts (2013), QJE 128(1), Figure 1.
- **Countries:** US and India.
- **Sample:** **751 randomly chosen US manufacturing firms with 100 to 5,000 employees**, and similarly sized Indian firms.
- **Finding:** **A thick tail of badly run Indian firms**, producing a lower average: **2.69 for India versus 3.33 for the US**. Indian firms tend **not to collect and analyse data systematically** in their factories, and **not to set and monitor clear targets**.
- **Caveat:** **The tail is the finding, not the average.** The best Indian firms are not badly run. The distribution has a long left tail that has not been competed away. A policy that targets the average targets nothing.

### What the practices actually are
- **Study:** Bloom, Sadun & Van Reenen, "Management as a Technology?", NBER WP 22327.
- **Finding:** Three types of "best practice": (1) practices that **have always been better** — e.g. not promoting incompetent employees to senior positions, or collecting some information before making decisions; (2) **genuine managerial innovations** — Taylor's Scientific Management, Lean Manufacturing, Deming's Quality movement; (3) practices that **became optimal due to changes in the economic environment** over time.
- **Caveat:** The taxonomy is the answer to the contingency objection. Category (1) is very hard to argue is contingent.

### The complementarity with IT
- **Study:** Bloom, Sadun & Van Reenen (2012), cited in NBER WP 22327. `[CHECK]` — full citation unverified.
- **Finding:** **IT productivity was higher for firms with stronger incentives management** — careful hiring, merit-based pay and promotion, and vigorously fixing or firing under-performers. Firms with better management enjoy a **higher productivity pay-off from IT across a wide range of sectors**.
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]`. Relevant to any digital adoption pillar: **the return on IT is conditional on management.** Funding digitalisation in badly managed firms buys less than the business case assumes.

## How to Apply

1. **Score the population.** 1–5, monitoring, targets, incentives. It is a telephone-interview instrument and it is cheap.
2. **Report the distribution, not the mean.** The tail is the policy object.
3. **Benchmark internationally.** The WMS gives you a comparable scale — a rare thing.
4. **Check the size band.** The comparisons are for firms with roughly 100–5,000 employees. Do not apply them below that.
5. **Sequence IT after management** where the digital pillar is being designed — the IT payoff is conditional on incentives management.
6. **Do not score micros.** There is nothing there for the instrument.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy asserts that Western Cape firms have a capability deficit and proposes a capability pillar.

**Application:** The assertion is unmeasured. There is an international instrument that would settle it, on a 1–5 scale, comparable to 34 countries and 11,000 firms, delivered by telephone interview. Nobody has run it here. Recommendation: score the mid-sized manufacturing population (say 50–500 employees) against the WMS instrument. Three outcomes are possible and each implies a different strategy. If the province averages near 3.3, there is no deficit at that tier and the capability pillar should be redirected. If it averages near 2.7 with a thick tail, the tail is the target and the India evidence applies. If the distribution is bimodal, the strategy is about the laggards and not the mean. **At present the pillar is being designed without knowing which of the three is true.**

**Result:** A capability pillar with a baseline, a target, and an international benchmark.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assert a capability deficit without scoring.
**Why:** The instrument exists, it is cheap, and it is internationally comparable.

**Don't:** Report the mean.
**Why:** The finding in every developing country is the thick left tail. The mean conceals the policy object.

**Don't:** Fund IT adoption before management.
**Why:** The IT productivity payoff is higher for firms with stronger incentives management. Order matters.
