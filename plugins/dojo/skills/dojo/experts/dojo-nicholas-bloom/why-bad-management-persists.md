# Why Badly Managed Firms Persist

## When to Use
- When the theory of change assumes competition will reallocate share to good firms.
- When asked why low-productivity firms survive.
- When productivity dispersion is being treated as a transitional state.

## Fails When
- **The question is insolvency or exit policy.** Different subject.

## Core Concept

**The standard economist's answer is that competition drives badly managed firms out. In the one place this was tested carefully, it had not.**

**The finding:** because reallocation across firms appeared to be constrained by **limits on managerial time**, competition had **not** forced badly managed firms to exit.

**The mechanism, which is the useful part.** For a badly managed firm to lose its market, a well-managed firm must take it. Taking it requires expanding: more plants, more hires, more supervision. Expansion consumes **managerial attention**, which is the one input the well-managed firm cannot simply buy. So the good firm expands slowly, the bad firm keeps its customers, and the dispersion persists. In the Indian sample, a large part of the constraint on managerial time was **owners' unwillingness to delegate beyond the family** — which is a governance fact, not a market fact.

**The apparent contradiction, and its resolution.** The "Management as a Technology?" model predicts — and the data confirm — a **positive relationship between product market competition and average management quality**, partly through a larger covariance between management and firm size as competition strengthens. So competition *does* improve management. How does that square with competition failing to eliminate bad firms in India?

They operate through different channels. Competition **improves management within firms** (the pressure channel) and **shifts share toward the well-managed** (the reallocation channel). The pressure channel works. The **reallocation channel can be blocked** — by managerial time, by delegation failure, by anything that stops good firms from growing. **Never present either finding without the other.**

**Three implications for a provincial strategy.**

**First, the gap is real and durable.** Bad management is not self-correcting on any policy-relevant timescale. There is something for policy to do, which is not obvious a priori.

**Second, the binding constraint may be delegation, not skill.** If good firms cannot expand because owners will not delegate beyond the family, then the intervention is about governance and management depth — building a second management layer — rather than about teaching the owner more. Note that the India experiment's *second* main effect was **increased decentralisation of decision making**, as better information flow enabled owners to delegate more decisions to middle managers. The information intervention unlocked the delegation.

**Third, competition intensity is largely not a provincial variable.** In related work, competition effects were found to be overwhelmingly driven by policy changes such as Chinese WTO accession and the dismantling of the Multi-Fibre Agreement. A province does not set trade policy. Do not build a strategy on raising competitive pressure you do not control.

## How to Apply

1. **Refuse the self-correction assumption.** Ask what is constraining reallocation. If nothing is, competition will fix it; usually something is.
2. **Look for the delegation constraint.** Family ownership, no second layer, owner-does-everything. This is the SA mid-market's characteristic shape.
3. **Target management depth, not owner skill.** The constraint is often the absence of anyone to delegate to.
4. **Note the information-to-delegation channel.** Better information flow enabled delegation in India. That is a design pattern.
5. **Do not build on competition you do not control.**

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy assumes that improving the competitive environment will raise average firm productivity.

**Application:** Partly right, and incomplete. Competition does raise average management quality within firms — that prediction is confirmed. But it will not necessarily reallocate share away from badly managed firms, because reallocation requires the good firms to expand, and expansion is constrained by managerial time. In a mid-market dominated by family-owned firms where owners do not delegate beyond the family — a fair description of much of South African mid-sized manufacturing — the reallocation channel is precisely the one that is blocked. So the strategy should not rely on it. And it should note that competition intensity is set largely by national trade policy, which the province does not hold.

**Result:** A strategy that acts on the channel it can move — management depth and delegation — rather than the one it cannot.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume competition eliminates bad management.
**Why:** It did not in Indian textiles, and the reason — managerial time constraining reallocation — is general.

**Don't:** Present the India non-exit finding as "competition doesn't matter".
**Why:** Competition raises average management quality. Two channels; one worked, one was blocked.

**Don't:** Treat the constraint as owner skill.
**Why:** It is frequently delegation depth. Teaching the owner more does not create a second manager.
