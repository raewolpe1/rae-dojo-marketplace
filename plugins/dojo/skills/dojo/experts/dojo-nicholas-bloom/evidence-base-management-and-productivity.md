## THE EVIDENCE

### The headline share
- **Study:** Bloom, N., Sadun, R. and Van Reenen, J., "Management as a Technology?", NBER Working Paper 22327 (June 2016, revised October 2017).
- **Coverage:** Over 11,000 firms, 34 countries; structural estimation on panel data.
- **Finding:** **Differences in management practices account for about 30% of total factor productivity differences, both between countries and within countries across firms.**
- **Caveat:** This is a **model-based** figure — it comes from building on their formal model of management as a technology and structurally estimating it, not from a reduced-form regression. The number depends on the model. Note also that some abstract versions state the result as "about 30% of **cross-country** TFP differences" while others state "both between countries and within countries across firms" — **cite carefully and check which claim the specific paper version supports.**

### The puzzle it addresses
- **Study:** Bloom et al. (2013), QJE 128(1), Introduction.
- **Finding:** Economists have long puzzled over astounding productivity differences across firms and countries. **US plants in industries producing homogeneous goods like cement, block ice, and oak flooring display 100% productivity spreads between the 10th and 90th percentiles.**
- **Caveat:** The dispersion finding is not Bloom's own; he is citing the established literature to motivate the management explanation. But it is the number that makes the exercise worth doing: same product, same country, twice the output per unit of input.

### The model's other predictions — all confirmed
- **Study:** Bloom, Sadun & Van Reenen, NBER WP 22327.
- **Finding:** The model predicts, and the data strongly support: **(i)** a positive impact of management on firm performance; **(ii)** a **positive relationship between product market competition and average management quality** — part of which stems from the larger covariance between management and firm size as competition strengthens; **(iii)** a **rise in the level and a fall in the dispersion of management with firm age.**
- **Caveat:** Prediction (ii) is important and cuts against the India finding's surface reading. Competition **does** raise average management quality — but in India it had not forced badly managed firms to exit, because reallocation was constrained by managerial time. Both hold: competition improves management **within** firms and shifts share **toward** the well-managed, but the reallocation channel can be blocked. Do not present either finding without the other.

### The competition mechanism
- **Study:** Bloom, Sadun & Van Reenen, NBER WP 22327; and Bloom, Draca & Van Reenen (2015) `[CHECK]`.
- **Finding:** Findings on competition appear **very consistent with the model's predictions**. In related work, competition effects are found to be **overwhelmingly driven by policy changes** such as Chinese accession to the WTO and the subsequent reduction in tariffs and quotas (e.g. the dismantling of the Multi-Fibre Agreement).
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]` on the Bloom, Draca & Van Reenen citation. The policy-driven-competition point is relevant to any provincial competition or trade argument: competition intensity is largely set by national trade policy, not by local action.

## How to Apply

1. **Use 30% to size the pillar, and say it is model-based.** It is a large and defensible number, and the caveat protects it.
2. **Lead with the dispersion puzzle.** 100% spreads in block ice is the fact that makes the case, and it is more persuasive than the share.
3. **Hold the two competition findings together.** Competition raises average management quality; competition did not eliminate badly managed Indian firms. The reconciliation is the reallocation channel.
4. **Note that competition intensity is largely a national trade-policy variable.** A provincial strategy has limited purchase on it.
5. **Expect management to improve and converge with firm age.** Young-firm populations will show wide dispersion — that is normal, not a crisis.

## Examples

**Situation:** A capability pillar needs an evidence base and a sizing argument.

**Application:** The available claim is: differences in management practices account for around 30% of TFP differences both between countries and within countries across firms — a model-based estimate from over 11,000 firms in 34 countries. That is a large enough share to justify a pillar. Two disciplines follow. First, it is a claim about firms with an organisation to manage; it says nothing about own-account enterprise, which is most of your population. Second, it is an argument for *measuring* your firms before designing anything, because the 30% is a statement about variance explained, not about whether your particular firms have a management deficit. Score first, then size.

**Result:** A pillar justified by a real magnitude, with the population limit stated.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Quote 30% as a reduced-form empirical finding.
**Why:** It is derived from structurally estimating their model. Say so.

**Don't:** Say competition doesn't affect management.
**Why:** It does — positively, on average. What it failed to do in India was force bad firms to exit, because reallocation was constrained.

**Don't:** Apply the 30% to a micro-enterprise population.
**Why:** It is estimated on firms with organisations. Own-account enterprises have no management to vary.
