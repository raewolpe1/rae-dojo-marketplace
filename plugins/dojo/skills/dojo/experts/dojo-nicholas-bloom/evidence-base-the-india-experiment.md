# Evidence Base — The India Textile Experiment

> **Provenance.** Compiled July 2026 from the published QJE article and NBER working paper. Verify against the primary source before quoting. Entries marked `[CHECK]` are unverified.

## When to Use
- Whenever "Does Management Matter?" is cited.
- When a management, consulting, or capability intervention is proposed.
- When causal evidence on management is needed.

## Fails When
- **The proposal is a short course for micro-enterprises.** Load `dose-and-population.md` and route to McKenzie.

## THE EVIDENCE

### The study
- **Study:** Bloom, N., Eifert, B., Mahajan, A., **McKenzie, D.** and Roberts, J. (2013), "Does Management Matter? Evidence from India", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* **128(1): 1–51**. DOI 10.1093/qje/qjs044. Advance access 18 November 2012. Earlier as NBER Working Paper 16658 (2010/2011).
- **Country:** India.
- **Population:** **Large multi-plant textile firms.** Not small firms; not micro-enterprises.
- **Intervention:** A management field experiment. **Free consulting on management practices** provided to **randomly chosen treatment plants**, compared against a set of **control plants**. The consulting was delivered by an international consulting firm over an extended engagement — diagnostic phase followed by implementation support.
- **Finding:** Adopting these management practices **raised productivity by 17% in the first year**, through **improved quality and efficiency and reduced inventory**. **Within three years it led to the opening of more production plants.** It also **increased decentralisation of decision making**, as better information flow enabled owners to delegate more decisions to middle managers; and **increased the use of computers**, necessitated by the data collection and analysis involved in modern management.
- **Caveat — READ THIS.** **The headline figure differs across versions.** The **published QJE (2013) figure is 17%**. The **NBER working paper (w16658) reports 11%**, and the **September 2010 preliminary draft reports about 10%**. Cite the published figure and note the revision if the number is load-bearing. Do not cite a working-paper figure as if it were the published result.

### Why the firms had not already done it
- **Study:** As above.
- **Finding:** **Informational barriers were the primary factor explaining the lack of adoption.** The firms had not weighed these practices and rationally declined them. They did not know about them.
- **Caveat:** This is the finding that makes the intervention make sense, and it is context-specific — a family-owned Indian textile sector with limited exposure to modern practice. In a context where owners *do* know and decline, the same intervention would do nothing. **Test the barrier before assuming it is information.**

### Why competition had not eliminated the bad firms
- **Study:** As above.
- **Finding:** **Because reallocation across firms appeared to be constrained by limits on managerial time, competition had not forced badly managed firms to exit.**
- **Caveat:** The mechanism matters more than the finding. Well-managed firms could not expand fast enough to take the market because expansion consumes managerial attention, which cannot be bought — in this sample, substantially because owners would not delegate beyond the family. See `why-bad-management-persists.md`.

### The baseline distribution
- **Study:** As above, Figure 1.
- **Finding:** Management practice scores plotted for **751 randomly chosen US manufacturing firms with 100 to 5,000 employees** and for similarly sized Indian firms reveal **a thick tail of badly run Indian firms**, giving a lower average management score: **2.69 for India versus 3.33 for US firms**. Indian firms tend not to collect and analyse data systematically in their factories, and tend not to set and monitor clear targets.
- **Caveat:** Comparable size bands (100–5,000 employees). This is a mid-to-large firm comparison. It says nothing about micro-enterprises, which the instrument cannot score.

### The eight-year follow-up — the decay
- **Study:** Referenced in Bloom, Sadun & Van Reenen, "Management as a Technology?" (NBER WP 22327), footnote 16. `[CHECK]` — the follow-up's own citation unverified.
- **Country:** India.
- **Finding:** In the **eight-year follow-up to the Bloom et al. (2013) India experiment, the largest reason for a deterioration in management practices in the treatment plants was the attrition of the plant manager.**
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]` on the follow-up citation. But this is arguably the most important programme-design finding in the file: **the gains are attached to people and they leave.** Average plant manager tenure in the survey: **6.4 years in the post, 13.0 years in the company.**

## How to Apply

1. **Cite 17%, from the QJE, and say the WP said 11%.** Precision here protects the deliverable.
2. **State the dose every time.** Months of free intensive consulting. Not a course.
3. **State the population every time.** Large multi-plant textile firms.
4. **Test the informational barrier before assuming it.** If owners know and decline, information does nothing.
5. **Build in retention.** The gains left with the plant managers.
6. **Do not use this to justify BDS for micros.** See `dose-and-population.md`.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy cites "Does Management Matter?" to justify a management support programme for mid-sized Western Cape manufacturers.

**Application:** This is the legitimate use of the citation — mid-sized manufacturers with a management layer are close to the studied population. Three conditions before proceeding. First, **score them**: nobody knows where Western Cape mid-sized manufacturers sit on the 1–5 scale, and if they average 3.5 the Indian gap does not exist here. Second, **test the barrier**: the India result rests on informational barriers; if SA owners already know these practices and have declined them, the diagnosis is different. Third, **match the dose**: 17% came from months of intensive consulting, not a workshop series, and a programme that funds the workshop and cites the consulting result is misrepresenting its own evidence.

**Result:** A programme sized to the evidence it cites, aimed at a population the evidence covers.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Cite 11% or 17% interchangeably.
**Why:** 11% is the working paper; 17% is the published QJE. Use the published figure and note the revision.

**Don't:** Cite this result for a five-day course.
**Why:** The dose was months of intensive free consulting. The dose is not incidental to the result — it *is* the result.

**Don't:** Assume the informational barrier.
**Why:** It was the finding in Indian textiles. Elsewhere the barrier may be incentive or capability, and information would then be useless.
