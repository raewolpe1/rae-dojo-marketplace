# Management as a Technology

## When to Use
- When someone argues every firm is already optimally managed for its own circumstances.
- When management is described as culture, style, or context-dependent preference.
- When asking whether best practice transfers across contexts.

## Fails When
- **The question is the size of the effect.** Load `evidence-base-management-and-productivity.md`.

## Core Concept

**The question in the title of the paper is real and it has an answer: are some management practices akin to a technology that can explain firm and national productivity, or do they simply reflect contingent management styles?**

**The two positions.** The **best-practice view**, going back to Taylor (1911): some management practices are universally good and all firms would benefit from adopting them. The **contingency view**, going back to Woodward (1958): every firm is already adopting optimal practices, but these are different from firm to firm.

**Why the stakes are total.** If the contingency view is right, the WMS is measuring styles, the cross-country differences are cultural variation, and there is nothing for policy to do — a low score just means "different, and suited to here". If the technology view is right, a low score means a firm is using an inferior technology it could adopt, and the gap is a policy object.

**The taxonomy that settles it.** There are three types of "best practice":

1. **Practices that have always been better.** Not promoting incompetent employees to senior positions. Collecting some information before making decisions. It is very difficult to construct the context in which these are contingently optimal.
2. **Genuine managerial innovations** — in the same way that there are technological innovations. Taylor's Scientific Management. Lean Manufacturing. Deming's Quality movement. These were invented, they diffused, and firms that had not adopted them were not exercising a preference.
3. **Practices that became optimal due to changes in the economic environment over time.** These are genuinely contingent — on the era, not on the firm.

Category 1 is the direct refutation of contingency. Category 2 is the technology claim proper: management practice has an innovation history, a diffusion curve, and laggards.

**The formal apparatus.** The paper presents a **formal model of "Management as a Technology"** and **structurally estimates it** on panel data, recovering parameters including the depreciation rate and adjustment costs of managerial capital. The model **predicts** and the data **confirm**: a positive impact of management on firm performance; a positive relationship between competition and average management quality; and a rise in the level and fall in the dispersion of management with firm age. A style is not something you can write a production function for and estimate the depreciation rate of. A technology is.

**The implication that matters for a strategy.** If management is a technology, then a management gap is an **adoption** problem, and adoption problems have known instruments — information, demonstration, diffusion, competition. The India experiment confirms the diagnosis: **informational barriers were the primary factor** explaining non-adoption. That is a technology-diffusion finding, not a culture finding.

**The honest limit.** Categories 1 and 2 do not exhaust management. Some of what firms do genuinely is contingent — on sector, on scale, on labour market, on product. The claim is not that all management is technology. It is that **enough of it is, measurably, to explain about 30% of TFP differences**, which is more than enough to act on.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask which category.** Always-better, innovation, or era-contingent? Most of the argument dissolves once the practice is classified.
2. **Treat a management gap as an adoption gap.** Then ask what blocks adoption: information, incentive, or capability?
3. **Refuse the culture explanation** where category 1 practices are absent. Nobody's culture requires promoting incompetents.
4. **Look for the diffusion channel.** Technologies spread through demonstration, supply chains, mobility of managers, and multinationals. So does management.
5. **Concede the contingent part.** It exists, and conceding it strengthens the rest.

## Examples

**Situation:** A stakeholder argues that Western Cape firms' management practices reflect local conditions and that imported "best practice" is inappropriate.

**Application:** Classify the practices in question. If the gap is that firms do not collect and analyse data systematically, or do not set and monitor clear targets — the two specific gaps found in Indian manufacturing — those are category 1: always better, hard to argue as contingently optimal. If the gap is a specific HR or shift-pattern practice imported from a different labour market, that may be genuinely contingent and the objection is sound. **The objection is usually raised generically and it only works specifically.** Ask which practice.

**Result:** A conversation about specific practices rather than about culture.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Accept the contingency objection generically.
**Why:** It only works practice by practice. Category 1 practices defeat it directly.

**Don't:** Claim all management is technology.
**Why:** Category 3 exists. Conceding it is what makes the rest credible.

**Don't:** Treat a management gap as a culture problem.
**Why:** In the one place it was tested, the barrier was information — a diffusion problem with cheap instruments.
