# Turning Toward

## When to Use
- When someone feels ignored, dismissed, or emotionally alone in their relationship.
- When the micro-interactions of daily connection need to be understood.
- When Level 3 of the Sound Relationship House needs attention.

## Core Concept
Gottman discovered that relationships are built and eroded not in grand gestures but in mundane moments — what he calls "bids for connection." A bid is any attempt by one partner to get the other's attention, affection, humour, or support. Bids can be verbal ("Look at that sunset"), physical (a touch on the shoulder), or implied (a sigh, a look). They are the fundamental unit of emotional connection.

The partner can respond in one of three ways: turning toward (acknowledging the bid), turning away (ignoring or missing the bid), or turning against (responding with hostility or irritation). Gottman's research produced a striking finding: couples who eventually divorced turned toward each other's bids only 33% of the time. Couples who stayed together turned toward 86% of the time. This single metric — the bid-response ratio — predicted relationship stability with remarkable accuracy.

The implications are counterintuitive. The fate of a relationship depends less on how couples handle major conflicts than on how they handle the tiny moments of daily life — the comment about the weather, the request to pass the salt, the observation about something interesting at work. Each bid is an invitation to connect. Each turning toward is a deposit in the emotional bank account. Each turning away is a withdrawal. Over time, the balance determines whether the relationship thrives or starves.

## How to Apply
1. **Learn to see bids.** Many bids are subtle — a sigh, a look, a casual comment. The first practice is simply noticing when your partner is reaching for connection.
2. **Turn toward, even minimally.** Turning toward does not require elaborate engagement. A brief acknowledgment — "Mmm, yeah" or a nod — counts. What matters is that the partner's bid is received rather than ignored.
3. **Reduce turning against.** Turning against — "Can't you see I'm busy?" in response to a bid — is more damaging than turning away. It teaches the partner that bids for connection are punished.
4. **Track your own bid-response pattern.** For a week, notice how often you turn toward, turn away, or turn against your partner's bids. The pattern is often invisible until consciously observed.

## Examples
**Situation:** A woman says her husband is "checked out." He comes home, goes to his phone, responds in monosyllables, and shows no interest in her day. She has stopped trying to engage him because "what's the point?"
**Application:** Gottman would identify a bid-response failure. She has been making bids — telling him about her day, pointing things out, asking questions — and he has been turning away (absorbed in his phone) or turning against ("Can we talk about this later?"). Over time, her bids decreased because unreciprocated bids extinguish. She stopped reaching because reaching was not being met. The intervention is straightforward but difficult: he needs to put the phone down and turn toward. Not every time — but enough to cross the threshold from the disaster rate (33%) toward the master rate (86%). The initial bids may feel forced. That is normal. The practice builds the habit, and the habit rebuilds the connection.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Demand enthusiastic turning toward at all times.
**Why:** Sometimes a partner is genuinely busy, flooded, or exhausted. A deferred response — "I can see you want to talk. Give me ten minutes to finish this and I'm yours" — is a form of turning toward, not turning away.

**Don't:** Keep score.
**Why:** Tracking bids to prove your partner is failing is a pursue move, not a connection move. The awareness should inform your own behaviour, not become an accusation.
