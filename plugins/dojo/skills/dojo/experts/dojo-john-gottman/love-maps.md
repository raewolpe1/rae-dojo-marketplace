# Love Maps

## When to Use
- When someone feels they and their partner have become strangers.
- When the friendship foundation of the relationship needs strengthening.
- When Level 1 of the Sound Relationship House needs to be addressed.

## Core Concept
A Love Map is Gottman's term for the cognitive space one partner devotes to knowing the other's psychological world — their worries, stresses, joys, dreams, history, friends, values, and daily experience. Research shows that couples with detailed Love Maps navigate conflict better, recover from stress more effectively, and weather major life transitions more successfully than couples who have stopped learning about each other.

Love Maps erode naturally over time. In the early stages of a relationship, partners are intensely curious about each other — every conversation reveals something new. As the relationship matures, partners often assume they already know each other and stop asking. But people change. The partner you married five years ago is not the same person today — their stresses are different, their dreams may have shifted, their friendships may have evolved. Without ongoing curiosity, the Love Map becomes outdated, and the partners begin to feel like strangers sharing a house.

Gottman's research demonstrated that couples who maintained detailed, updated Love Maps were significantly more likely to stay together after major life transitions — the birth of a child, a career change, a health crisis, retirement. The Love Map provided a foundation of understanding that helped the couple adapt to change together rather than growing apart.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask open-ended questions about your partner's current inner world.** Not "How was your day?" but "What stressed you most today?" "What are you looking forward to this week?" "What's on your mind that you haven't told me?"
2. **Update continuously.** Your partner's best friend, biggest worry, favourite meal, and current dream may all have changed since you last asked. Love Maps require ongoing maintenance.
3. **Know the big and the small.** Love Maps include both major dimensions (life dreams, core values, formative experiences) and daily details (current work project, what book they are reading, what they had for lunch).
4. **Use Love Map questions as a practice.** The Gottman Card Decks app and published question sets provide structured prompts for deepening knowledge. Use them at dinner, on walks, before bed.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man realises during a therapy session that he does not know the name of his wife's new colleague who has been causing her significant stress for months. She has mentioned this person repeatedly, but he cannot recall the name. His wife is visibly hurt by this revelation.
**Application:** This is a Love Map failure. He has not been making cognitive room for her experience. His inability to recall the name communicates — accurately or not — that her world does not matter enough for him to attend to it. The intervention is not to memorise the name but to rebuild the practice of curiosity. The therapist might ask: "When she comes home and talks about her day, what are you doing?" Perhaps he is on his phone, half-listening, thinking about his own day. The practice change is simple but demanding: genuine attention. Look at her. Ask follow-up questions. Remember what she tells you. This is not a technique — it is the relational practice of treating your partner's inner world as important.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat Love Maps as a quiz.
**Why:** "What's my favourite colour?" asked as a test creates anxiety, not connection. Love Maps are about ongoing curiosity, not performance.

**Don't:** Assume your Love Map is current.
**Why:** People change constantly. A Love Map that was accurate a year ago may be significantly outdated today.
