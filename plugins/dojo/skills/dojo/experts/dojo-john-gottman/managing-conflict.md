# Managing Conflict

## When to Use
- When someone fights about the same thing repeatedly without resolution.
- When the difference between perpetual and solvable problems needs to be understood.
- When gridlocked conflict — where couples are stuck and neither will move — is the presenting issue.

## Core Concept
Gottman's research produced one of the most counterintuitive findings in relationship science: approximately 69% of all couple conflicts are perpetual. They are rooted in fundamental personality differences, lifestyle preferences, or core values that do not change. The introvert-extrovert couple will always negotiate between socialising and solitude. The spender-saver couple will always negotiate between enjoyment and security. The career-focused partner and the family-focused partner will always negotiate between ambition and presence.

The remaining 31% are solvable problems — specific, situational issues that have solutions: who takes the children to school, how to divide household chores, where to spend the holidays. These can be resolved through compromise, using soft start-ups and repair attempts.

The crucial mistake is treating perpetual problems like solvable ones — trying harder and harder to "win" the argument or find the "right" answer. When this happens, the perpetual problem becomes gridlocked — the couple stops discussing it productively and instead cycles through the same rigid positions with increasing resentment. Gridlocked conflicts are characterised by emotional disengagement, vilification of the partner's position, and a feeling of hopelessness about the issue.

Gottman discovered that the key to unlocking gridlocked perpetual problems is accessing the dream within the conflict. Each partner's rigid position contains a deeper meaning — a dream, a value, a life narrative — that makes the position essential to their identity. When these dreams are shared and understood, dialogue becomes possible even when resolution does not. Happy couples learn to live with their perpetual differences by maintaining ongoing, affectionate dialogue about them — what Gottman calls "dialogue with the perpetual problem" — rather than trying to eliminate them.

## How to Apply
1. **Classify the problem.** Is it perpetual or solvable? If the couple has fought about it for years without resolution, it is almost certainly perpetual.
2. **For solvable problems, use the Gottman method.** Soft start-up, accept influence, repair, compromise.
3. **For perpetual problems, explore the dreams.** Ask each partner: "What does your position on this issue mean to you? What dream or value does it represent? What would it feel like to give it up entirely?"
4. **Accept that dialogue, not resolution, is the goal.** Happy couples talk about perpetual problems with humour, affection, and curiosity. They do not resolve them. They dance with them.

## Examples
**Situation:** A couple has fought about neatness for fifteen years. She wants the house orderly. He leaves things everywhere. They have tried chore charts, cleaning services, separate spaces. Nothing resolves it. Both are exasperated.
**Application:** This is a perpetual problem. She is not going to become comfortable with clutter, and he is not going to become naturally tidy. The Gottman approach would stop trying to solve it and instead explore the dreams. For her, orderliness may represent control in a chaotic world, or it may connect to a childhood where chaos signalled danger. For him, relaxed messiness may represent freedom, or it may connect to a childhood where rigid order was suffocating. When they can see each other's dreams — "Order means safety for you. Freedom from order means freedom for me" — they can find temporary compromises that honour both without either partner "winning." The key word is temporary — they will need to renegotiate periodically because the underlying difference does not go away.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Try to solve perpetual problems.
**Why:** The attempt to solve the unsolvable produces gridlock, which produces the Four Horsemen. The alternative — ongoing, affectionate dialogue — is more sustainable and more realistic.

**Don't:** Dismiss perpetual problems as trivial.
**Why:** The dreams within the position are often deeply connected to identity, values, and life history. Dismissing them dismisses the person.
