# Gottman Assessment

## When to Use
- When someone asks about how Gottman conducts research on couples.
- When the observational methods — SPAFF coding, physiological monitoring, the Love Lab — need to be explained.
- When someone asks about the oral history interview and its predictive power.

## Core Concept
Gottman's approach to relationship science is grounded in direct observation rather than self-report. His research lab at the University of Washington — the "Love Lab" — was an apartment-like setting where couples were videotaped during conflict conversations, everyday interactions, and reunions. The interactions were coded using the Specific Affect Coding System (SPAFF), which categorises each moment of interaction into specific emotional behaviours — contempt, belligerence, defensiveness, interest, affection, humour, sadness, and so on. Simultaneously, physiological measures were recorded: heart rate, skin conductance, blood velocity, immune function markers.

This produced an extraordinarily granular dataset. Gottman could see not just what couples said but how they said it, how their bodies responded, and how each partner's behaviour affected the other's physiology in real time. From this data, he identified the specific behavioural and physiological patterns that predicted relationship satisfaction and stability over time.

The oral history interview is another diagnostic tool. Gottman discovered that how couples tell the story of their relationship — with fondness or disappointment, with "we" language or "I" language, with shared humour or mutual blame — predicts current satisfaction and future stability. Couples who tell their story with warmth and fondness, even when describing difficult periods, are significantly more likely to remain together.

Gottman's prediction claims — that he can predict divorce with over 90% accuracy from a brief observation — have generated both admiration and controversy. The claims are based on specific study conditions using retrospective coding and have been challenged on methodological grounds (see gottman-and-the-field.md for a balanced assessment). The predictive power is real but should be understood as pattern identification, not fortune-telling.

## How to Apply
1. **Use the oral history interview in clinical assessment.** How a couple tells their story reveals the current state of the bond. Look for fondness, disappointment, we-ness, chaos, or flatness.
2. **Track the behavioural ratios.** In clinical observation, count the positives and negatives. What is the ratio? Which Horsemen are present? Are repair attempts being made and received?
3. **Attend to physiology.** A partner who is visibly calm but has elevated heart rate is flooding internally. When possible, monitor physiological signs of distress.
4. **Use assessment to inform intervention.** The Gottman assessment identifies specific levels of the Sound Relationship House that need strengthening. Intervention follows assessment.

## Examples
**Situation:** A therapist asks a couple to tell the story of how they met. The wife speaks with warmth, recalling specific details and smiling at the memories. The husband is vague, contributes little, and says, "I don't really remember — it was a long time ago."
**Application:** This divergence in the oral history is clinically significant. Her warmth suggests she still holds fondness for the relationship's origins. His vagueness may indicate emotional disengagement — he has stopped investing cognitive and emotional resources in the relationship narrative. In Gottman's research, couples where one or both partners tell the story with flatness, confusion, or negativity are at significantly higher risk. The therapist would note this as a diagnostic indicator: the friendship foundation may be eroding, particularly his Love Maps and Fondness and Admiration.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use prediction as prophecy.
**Why:** Identifying risk patterns is not the same as predicting an individual couple's fate. The patterns are probabilistic, not deterministic. They inform intervention, not judgment.

**Don't:** Dismiss self-report entirely.
**Why:** Observational data is powerful but does not capture everything. What the couple says about their experience complements what the observer sees in their behaviour.
