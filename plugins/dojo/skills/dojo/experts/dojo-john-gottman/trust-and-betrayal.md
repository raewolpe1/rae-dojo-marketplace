# Trust and Betrayal

## When to Use
- When someone has experienced a betrayal of trust — infidelity, deception, broken promises.
- When the mechanics of trust-building and trust-erosion need to be understood.
- When the Gottman framework for recovery from betrayal needs to be explained.

## Core Concept
Trust, in Gottman's model, is not a feeling — it is a metric. It is the belief, built through accumulated experience, that your partner acts to maximise your interest and the relationship's interest, not just their own. Gottman formalised this through game theory: in every interaction, each partner faces a choice between maximising their own payoff or maximising the joint payoff. Trust is the accumulated evidence that the partner consistently chooses the relational option.

Trust builds incrementally — through thousands of small moments where a partner turns toward rather than away, chooses transparency over concealment, prioritises the relationship over convenience. And trust erodes incrementally — through thousands of small moments of turning away, choosing self-interest, failing to follow through. The dramatic betrayal — the affair, the major lie — is usually the visible collapse of a structure that has been weakening for years through accumulated micro-betrayals.

Gottman and Silver developed a three-phase model for recovery from major betrayal: Atonement, Attunement, and Attachment. In the Atonement phase, the betraying partner takes full responsibility, provides transparency, and tolerates the injured partner's pain without defensiveness. In the Attunement phase, the couple rebuilds emotional connection through the practices of the Sound Relationship House — Love Maps, turning toward, managing conflict. In the Attachment phase, the couple creates new shared meaning and rituals that mark the transition from the old relationship to the new one.

The research shows that couples can recover from infidelity — but recovery requires the betraying partner to do sustained, uncomfortable work: radical transparency, tolerance of repeated questioning, genuine remorse, and the rebuilding of trustworthy behaviour over months and years. The injured partner's work is to gradually allow trust to rebuild without demanding certainty that can never be provided.

## How to Apply
1. **Understand trust as built in small moments.** Daily reliability, emotional responsiveness, and consistent follow-through are the materials of trust.
2. **Recognise micro-betrayals.** Broken promises, white lies, turning away from bids, prioritising others over the partner — these erode trust before the dramatic breach makes the erosion visible.
3. **In betrayal recovery, follow the three-phase model.** Atonement first — no shortcuts. Attunement second — rebuilding the friendship. Attachment third — creating new meaning.
4. **Accept that trust rebuilding is slow.** There is no timeline that can be imposed from outside. The injured partner heals at their own pace.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man discovered his wife's emotional affair three months ago. She ended it and has been transparent since. He monitors her phone, questions her whereabouts, and cannot stop replaying scenarios. She is exhausted and says he needs to "move on."
**Application:** He is in the Atonement phase. His monitoring and questioning — while painful for both — are the attachment system's attempt to re-establish safety. Her request that he "move on" is understandable but premature. Gottman would tell her: you do not get to set the timeline. You broke the trust and rebuilding it requires you to tolerate his pain — to answer the same questions again, to provide transparency without resentment, to sit with his devastation without defending yourself. If she can sustain this — genuinely, not performatively — they will eventually move to Attunement, where the friendship can be rebuilt. If she cannot, and demands that he recover on her schedule, the trust will not heal.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Demand forgiveness on a timeline.
**Why:** Forgiveness is an organic process, not a deliverable. Demanding it communicates that the betrayer's comfort matters more than the injured partner's healing.

**Don't:** Treat the affair as the sole problem.
**Why:** The affair is usually the most visible symptom of accumulated relational failures — eroded friendship, missed bids, unaddressed conflicts. Recovery must address both the betrayal and the conditions that preceded it.
