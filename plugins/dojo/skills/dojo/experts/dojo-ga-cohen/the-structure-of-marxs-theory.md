# The Structure of Marx's Theory

## When to Use
- When Marxist analytical categories — productive forces, relations of production, exploitation, class — are relevant to understanding a development situation.
- When presenting historical materialism as a productive analytical method rather than as a political ideology.
- When *Karl Marx's Theory of History: A Defence* (1978; expanded 2000) provides the framework.

## Core Concept
In *Karl Marx's Theory of History: A Defence* (1978), Cohen reconstructed historical materialism in analytically rigorous form. He argued that Marx's theory of history — that the development of productive forces drives changes in social relations, which in turn drive changes in political and ideological structures — can be stated precisely and defended against philosophical objections.

The key claims: productive forces (technology, knowledge, human skill) tend to develop over time. Relations of production (property relations, class structures, modes of economic organisation) are explained by their tendency to promote the development of productive forces. When the relations of production no longer promote productive development — when they become "fetters" on the forces — revolution occurs and new relations of production replace the old.

Cohen's defence was not dogmatic. He acknowledged that Marx's theory is a tendency, not a law; that it faces counterexamples; and that it requires modification in light of historical evidence. But he argued that the core insight — that economic structures shape social, political, and ideological life — is a productive analytical method, not merely a political programme.

For development practice, the materialist method is a corrective to idealist analyses that explain development outcomes by reference to ideas, institutions, or culture without examining the economic structures that shape them. The Marxist question — who controls the productive resources, and how does this control shape everything else? — remains one of the most productive questions in development analysis.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask the materialist question.** "Who controls the productive resources in this context? Land, capital, technology, labour? How does this control shape the social, political, and institutional arrangements that the programme encounters?"
2. **Identify the class structure.** "What class relations characterise this context? Who appropriates the surplus, and through what mechanisms? The class structure shapes everything the programme can do."
3. **Assess the fetters.** "Are the existing property relations promoting or hindering productive development? If the existing arrangements are 'fetters' — preventing productive capacity from being realised — structural change is needed, not incremental reform."
4. **Use the method, not the dogma.** "Historical materialism is a method of analysis — asking how economic structures shape social life. It is not a dogma about the inevitable victory of the proletariat. Use the method; discard the teleology."

## Examples
**Situation:** A land reform programme has distributed land to smallholder farmers, but agricultural productivity has not increased as expected. The programme attributes the failure to "low capacity" among the new landholders.
**Application:** "The materialist question reveals what the capacity diagnosis conceals. The smallholder farmers have land — but do they control the other productive resources? Seeds, fertiliser, credit, market access, technical knowledge, water? If these remain controlled by the previous landlords, by commercial intermediaries, or by the state, the land distribution has changed the formal property relation without changing the actual relation of production. The farmers own land but cannot use it productively because the complementary productive forces are controlled by others. This is not a capacity problem — it is a structural problem. The relations of production (who controls what) have been partially changed (land redistributed) without being fully changed (the other productive resources remain concentrated). The 'low capacity' diagnosis locates the problem in the farmers. The materialist analysis locates it in the structure of productive relations. The remedy is not training for farmers but the redistribution of the productive resources — credit, inputs, market access — that make land productive."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat Marx as dogma.
**Why:** Cohen's analytical Marxism is rigorous, self-critical, and open to revision. It treats Marx's claims as hypotheses to be tested, not as truths to be defended.

**Don't:** Dismiss Marx as outdated.
**Why:** The materialist method — asking how economic structures shape social life — remains one of the most productive analytical tools available. Dismissing it because of the failures of Soviet communism is like dismissing democracy because of its failures in practice.
