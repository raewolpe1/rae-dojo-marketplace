# Incentives and Inequality

## When to Use
- When inequality is being justified by the claim that differential compensation is necessary to motivate productive behaviour.
- When the "we need to pay more to attract talent" argument needs to be interrogated rather than accepted.
- When revealing that incentive-based inequality is produced by choices — the talented choosing to withhold without extra pay — rather than by natural necessity.

## Core Concept
The incentive argument is the most common justification for inequality in both market economies and development organisations: we must pay more to attract and retain talent; without differential rewards, the talented will not produce; inequality is the price we pay for productivity. Cohen's challenge: the incentive argument treats as a natural fact what is actually a choice. The talented person who says "pay me more or I will produce less" is making a choice — a choice to exploit their bargaining position. The question for egalitarians is whether this choice should be accepted or challenged.

Cohen's distinction between two types of incentive argument is crucial. The first type: inequality compensates for genuine sacrifices — harder work, longer hours, more unpleasant conditions. This is potentially legitimate: if the work is genuinely more demanding, differential compensation may be justified. The second type: inequality bribes the talented into performing work they could perform equally well without the bribe. This is the problematic type: the inequality is produced not by genuine sacrifice but by the talented's refusal to work without extra pay. The egalitarian should accept the first type (possibly) and reject the second (definitely).

## How to Apply
1. **Classify the incentive.** "Is this inequality compensating for genuine sacrifice (harder work, longer hours, worse conditions) or bribing the talented into performance they could deliver without the bribe? The classification determines whether the inequality is potentially justified."
2. **Challenge the naturalisation.** "The claim that 'we must pay more to attract talent' treats the talented's demand as a natural fact. But it is a choice. The surgeon who demands premium pay could choose to operate for a standard wage. Her refusal is a choice — and egalitarians should interrogate it."
3. **Apply to development organisations.** "Development organisations that pay 'market rates' to attract international talent are accepting the incentive argument. Ask: would the talent refuse to work for a more equal salary? If so, they are choosing to exploit their bargaining position — and the organisation is capitulating."
4. **Imagine the alternative.** "What would happen if the talented chose differently — if they were motivated by commitment rather than by premium pay? The camping trip shows this is possible: on the camping trip, the talented friend contributes without demanding extra. Why not in the office?"

## Examples
**Situation:** A development consultancy firm justifies paying senior partners five times the salary of junior staff by arguing that the senior partners' expertise generates the revenue that supports the firm and pays the junior staff. Without the incentive of high partnership compensation, the senior partners would leave for the private sector.
**Application:** "The firm's argument is a textbook incentive justification: pay the talented more or they will leave. Cohen's interrogation: the senior partners' threat to leave is a choice — a choice to deploy their talent only where the financial reward is highest. This choice exploits their bargaining position: they have scarce expertise, the firm needs them, and they leverage this need to extract premium compensation. The junior staff, who may work equally hard but lack scarce expertise, have no comparable bargaining power. The inequality is produced not by differential contribution (the junior staff may contribute as much in their role) but by differential bargaining power. The firm has naturalised this bargaining-power differential as 'market rate' — but the market rate is simply the price set by the talented's collective refusal to work for less. An egalitarian firm would challenge this: compensating for genuine sacrifices (seniority requires more responsibility, travel, stress) while refusing to compensate for the mere possession of scarce expertise. The expertise is a social product — formed through education, mentoring, and institutional opportunity — and its deployment should serve the firm's mission, not the partner's personal wealth."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Deny that incentives have any effect.
**Why:** Incentives do affect behaviour — the question is whether the behaviour they affect (the talented withholding without extra pay) should be accepted or challenged.

**Don't:** Ignore genuine sacrifice differentials.
**Why:** Some work is genuinely harder, more dangerous, or more demanding. Compensation for genuine sacrifice is different from compensation for the mere possession of talent.
