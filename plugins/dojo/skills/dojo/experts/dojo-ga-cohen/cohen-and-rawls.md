# Cohen and Rawls

## When to Use
- When the difference principle is invoked to justify inequality: "this inequality is acceptable because it benefits the worst off."
- When challenging the incentive-based argument within the difference principle: the talented demand extra pay, and the resulting inequality is then justified because the extra production benefits everyone.
- When deepening the analysis of Rawlsian justice from an egalitarian perspective.

## Core Concept
Cohen's engagement with Rawls — developed across *Rescuing Justice and Equality* (2008) and numerous essays — is the most sustained and philosophically rigorous critique of Rawlsian liberalism from the left. Cohen respects Rawls enormously; he considers *A Theory of Justice* one of the great works of political philosophy. But he argues that Rawls permits too much inequality.

The target is the difference principle: Rawls's claim that inequality is justified if and only if it benefits the worst-off members of society. Cohen's objection is precise. The difference principle works through incentives: the talented demand higher pay, the economy grows as a result, and the worst off benefit from the larger economic pie. But Cohen asks: why do the talented demand higher pay? Is their refusal to produce without extra incentive a natural fact or a choice?

If it is a choice — and Cohen argues it is — then the talented are exploiting their bargaining position. They are saying: "pay me more or I will produce less." The society then permits the inequality and justifies it by the difference principle. But the inequality was produced by the talented's choice to withhold — a choice that violates the egalitarian principles that Rawls himself endorses. The difference principle, on Cohen's reading, permits the talented to extort inequality from a society committed to equality.

The deeper philosophical point: Rawls locates justice in institutional structures (the "basic structure" of society). Cohen argues that justice also requires an ethos — a shared commitment by individuals to act justly in their personal choices, not merely to support just institutions. A society with just institutions but self-interested individuals has not achieved justice.

## How to Apply
1. **Interrogate the incentive within the difference principle.** "The difference principle says inequality benefits the worst off. But ask: why is the inequality necessary? Because the talented demand it. And why do they demand it? Because they choose to. This choice — to withhold without extra pay — is the source of the inequality, and it is not justified by the principle that justifies the inequality it produces."
2. **Distinguish institutional from personal justice.** "Just institutions are necessary but not sufficient. If individuals within just institutions make unjust personal choices (the talented demanding premium pay), the injustice persists within the just structure."
3. **Apply to development.** "When development organisations justify salary differentials by 'market rates' and argue that the worst off benefit from the talent these rates attract, they are deploying the difference principle. Interrogate it: would the talent refuse to work for less? If so, they are extorting the organisation."
4. **Respect Rawls while pushing further.** "Rawls is an ally, not an enemy. His framework provides essential resources for justice. But it does not go far enough — it permits inequality that egalitarians should not accept."

## Examples
**Situation:** A government justifies salary premiums for senior civil servants by arguing that the premiums attract talent, and the talent produces better public services that benefit the poorest citizens — a textbook application of the difference principle.
**Application:** "The argument is formally valid under the difference principle: the inequality (salary premiums) benefits the worst off (through better services). But Cohen's interrogation reveals the premise the argument conceals: the talented senior civil servants demand the premiums. If they would serve without the premium — motivated by duty, commitment, or a sense of justice — the inequality would be unnecessary, and the services would be just as good. The inequality is produced not by the needs of the worst off but by the demands of the talented. The government has capitulated to the talented class's bargaining power and justified the capitulation by the difference principle. A genuinely just arrangement would challenge the talented: serve because justice demands it, not because the premium makes it worth your while. This is a demand for an egalitarian ethos — personal commitment to justice — not merely for just institutional design. The difference principle permits the talented to extract rents from their position and calls it justice. Cohen calls it what it is: extortion dignified by philosophy."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss Rawls.
**Why:** Rawls's framework is enormously valuable. Cohen's critique is internal — it uses Rawls's own egalitarian commitments to push the framework further. Dismissing Rawls loses the philosophical foundation.

**Don't:** Ignore the practical force of incentive arguments.
**Why:** In the real world, incentives matter. Cohen's point is not that incentives have no effect but that their effect is produced by choices that can be questioned — and that egalitarians should question them.
