# Rescuing Justice from Constructivism

## When to Use
- When the philosophical foundations of justice claims are at stake: are principles of justice discovered (moral realism) or invented (constructivism)?
- When Rawlsian constructivism — the claim that justice is what reasonable people would agree to from behind the veil of ignorance — needs to be challenged.
- When the practitioner needs confidence that injustice is genuinely wrong, not merely unpopular.

## Core Concept
In *Rescuing Justice and Equality* (2008), Cohen argues against Rawlsian constructivism — the method that derives principles of justice from what rational persons would choose in a hypothetical original position. Cohen's objection: constructivism conflates what people would choose with what is just. These are not the same thing. Rational self-interested persons in the original position might choose principles that are less than fully just — because the constructivist procedure builds in assumptions (about rational self-interest, about the need for incentives) that bias the outcome toward insufficiently egalitarian principles.

Cohen's alternative: justice is a matter of moral fact, not of hypothetical agreement. The claim that inequality is unjust is true or false — it does not depend on whether people would agree to it from behind a veil of ignorance. The philosophical task is to discover the true principles of justice through moral reflection, not to construct them through a decision procedure.

For development practice, this matters because it provides a firm foundation for justice claims. When the practitioner says "this programme is unjust," she is not merely expressing a preference or predicting what people would agree to. She is making a claim about moral reality — a claim that can be true or false, defended or refuted, but that has the full force of moral truth.

## How to Apply
1. **Ground justice claims in moral reality.** "This programme is unjust — not because stakeholders disapprove but because it violates genuine principles of equality and dignity. Justice is a moral fact, not a stakeholder preference."
2. **Distinguish justice from agreement.** "The fact that all parties agreed to the arrangement does not make it just. Parties may agree to unjust arrangements — because of power imbalances, because of limited alternatives, because of the influence of unjust background conditions."
3. **Test principles against intuition.** "Does this principle produce conclusions that are morally intolerable? If the difference principle permits inequality extorted by the talented, the principle is at fault — regardless of whether rational persons would choose it."
4. **Maintain philosophical seriousness.** "Justice claims require philosophical argument, not merely political assertion. The claim that a programme is unjust must be defended with reasons, not merely asserted with conviction."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme evaluation finds that a targeting mechanism excludes a vulnerable subgroup. The programme team argues that the targeting mechanism was agreed upon by all stakeholders during the design phase and therefore should not be changed.
**Application:** "The fact that all stakeholders agreed does not make the targeting just. If the mechanism excludes vulnerable persons, it is unjust — regardless of the agreement. Cohen's point: justice is not constructed by agreement. It is a moral fact that the agreement may or may not have captured. The stakeholders who agreed may have been influenced by power imbalances, by incomplete information, or by the doxa of the development field. Their agreement does not transform an unjust outcome into a just one. The evaluation should say plainly: the targeting mechanism is unjust because it excludes persons whose capabilities fall below the threshold. This is a justice claim — not a process objection."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss constructivism entirely.
**Why:** Rawlsian constructivism has genuine strengths — it takes pluralism seriously and provides a decision procedure for contested questions. Cohen's critique refines constructivism; it does not eliminate the need for procedures.

**Don't:** Present justice as obvious.
**Why:** Justice claims require philosophical argument. The fact that justice is a moral reality does not mean that its specific content is self-evident.
