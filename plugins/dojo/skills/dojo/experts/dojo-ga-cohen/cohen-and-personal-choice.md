# Cohen and Personal Choice

## When to Use
- When the gap between a practitioner's egalitarian principles and their personal lifestyle needs to be confronted — not as accusation but as philosophical inquiry.
- When the relationship between institutional justice and personal justice needs to be clarified.
- When *If You're an Egalitarian, How Come You're So Rich?* (2000) provides the framework.

## Core Concept
Cohen's most personally challenging argument is that justice requires not only just institutions but just personal choices. The egalitarian philosopher who argues for redistribution while accumulating personal wealth has a consistency problem. The development professional who works on poverty while earning a salary that places them in the global top decile has the same problem.

Cohen distinguishes between the Rawlsian position (justice is achieved through institutional design; personal choices within just institutions are the individual's own business) and his own position (justice requires an ethos — shared personal commitment to equality that governs personal choices, not merely institutional structures). A society with just institutions but self-interested personal choices has just the structure of justice, not its substance.

The personal-choice question is uncomfortable because it applies to everyone who claims egalitarian commitments. It does not demand saintliness — Cohen acknowledges that human motivation is complex and that personal sacrifice has limits. But it demands that the question be confronted: does your life reflect your principles? Not perfectly — but substantively.

For development practitioners, the question is particularly pointed. The practitioner who earns well while working on poverty, who flies business class while promoting sustainability, who lives in an expatriate compound while advocating for community integration — these contradictions are not merely aesthetic. They are evidence of the gap between institutional commitment and personal ethos.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask the question honestly.** "If you're an egalitarian, how come you're so rich? Not as accusation but as genuine inquiry. What does the gap between your income and the income of those you serve tell you about your commitments?"
2. **Identify the actionable changes.** "You cannot solve global inequality through personal sacrifice. But you can identify specific changes — in compensation, in lifestyle, in consumption, in giving — that bring your personal practice closer to your principles."
3. **Distinguish degrees.** "Cohen does not demand perfection. He demands that the question be confronted. Some practitioners make significant personal sacrifices. Others do not. The difference is not between saints and sinners but between those who confront the question and those who evade it."
4. **Connect personal to structural.** "Personal-choice reform is necessary but insufficient. It must be accompanied by structural reform — of compensation policies, of organisational hierarchies, of industry practices. Neither alone is justice."

## Examples
**Situation:** A senior development consultant earns $200,000 per year advising governments on poverty reduction strategies. She is genuinely committed to the work. She is also aware that her income places her in the global top 1%.
**Application:** "Cohen's question applies: if you're committed to poverty reduction, what does your $200,000 salary say about that commitment? The answer is not simple. The salary is produced by the structure of the development industry — the market for consulting expertise, the willingness of donors to pay for it, and the consultant's own willingness to accept it. The consultant did not create the structure. But she participates in it — and her participation reproduces it. The personal-choice question: could she work for less? Could she give more away? Could she challenge the industry's compensation norms from within? These are genuine choices — not forced by the structure but enabled by the consultant's bargaining position. Cohen would not demand that she work for free. He would demand that she confront the question: does your lifestyle reflect your commitment to the poor? The answer — honest, uncomfortable, productive — is the beginning of aligning personal practice with political principle."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use the question to shame.
**Why:** The personal-choice question is philosophical, not prosecutorial. Shaming produces defensiveness, not reflection. The question should open inquiry, not close it.

**Don't:** Reduce justice to personal behaviour.
**Why:** Personal choices matter, but structural reform matters more. The consultant who reduces her salary by 20% has not solved global inequality. Structural reform of the development industry's compensation practices would achieve more than any individual sacrifice.
