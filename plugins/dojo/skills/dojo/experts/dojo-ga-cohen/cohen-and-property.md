# Cohen and Property

## When to Use
- When existing property distributions are being treated as natural or just without examining how they were produced.
- When property rights are being used to justify inequality — "they own it, therefore they have a right to its proceeds."
- When the relationship between property, freedom, and equality needs to be analysed.

## Core Concept
Cohen's analysis of property connects his critique of self-ownership to his critique of capitalism. Property rights are not natural — they are institutional arrangements that societies create, enforce, and can modify. The question is not whether property rights should exist but how they should be structured, and in whose interest.

Existing property distributions are the product of historical processes — including conquest, colonisation, dispossession, and exploitation — that have no moral authority. The claim that current owners "deserve" their property because they (or their ancestors) "earned" it founders on the historical reality that most property was originally acquired through violence or appropriation. The libertarian who defends existing property distributions as the product of voluntary exchange ignores the involuntary origins of the property being exchanged.

For development practice, property analysis is essential. Land reform, resource rights, intellectual property, and the ownership of productive assets are development questions that cannot be addressed without interrogating the property arrangements that produce the inequality the programme aims to reduce.

## How to Apply
1. **Trace the property history.** "How did the current owners acquire this property? Through what historical process — purchase, inheritance, colonial grant, dispossession, state allocation? The legitimacy of the current distribution depends on the legitimacy of the process that produced it."
2. **Assess the distributive effects.** "How does the current property distribution affect equality? Does it concentrate productive resources in few hands? Does it exclude the majority from access to the means of production?"
3. **Challenge the naturalisation.** "Property rights are institutional arrangements, not natural facts. They can be restructured. The claim that 'property rights must be respected' conceals the question of whether the current property distribution is just."
4. **Design for equitable access.** "If the current property distribution is unjust, reform it. Land redistribution, community ownership, cooperative structures, and public ownership are all institutional alternatives that different contexts may require."

## Examples
**Situation:** A country's agricultural land is concentrated in the hands of a small commercial farming elite — a distribution that traces directly to colonial-era land grants. A land reform programme proposes redistribution, but the commercial farmers argue that their property rights must be respected because they have invested in and improved the land.
**Application:** "The commercial farmers' property claim rests on colonial-era grants — land appropriated from indigenous populations through colonial violence. The claim that they have 'invested in and improved' the land adds value through their labour — but the land itself was not legitimately acquired. The initial acquisition was unjust; subsequent improvements do not launder the original injustice. Cohen's analysis: the property distribution is the product of historical violence, not of voluntary exchange. The commercial farmers' 'property rights' are the institutional codification of colonial dispossession. This does not mean that redistribution is simple — the farmers have made genuine investments, and the practical questions of compensation and productivity are real. But the moral claim — 'our property rights must be respected' — cannot stand when the property was acquired through colonial dispossession. The redistribution programme should proceed with compensation for genuine improvements but without treating the colonial property distribution as a moral baseline."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reject all property rights.
**Why:** Property rights — appropriately structured — can serve individual autonomy, economic security, and productive investment. The issue is not property itself but its distribution and the process that produced it.

**Don't:** Treat existing distributions as just by default.
**Why:** Existing property distributions are historical products — often products of violence, colonialism, and exploitation. They must be evaluated, not assumed to be just.
