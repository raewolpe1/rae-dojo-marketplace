# Community and Justice

## When to Use
- When just institutions exist but the community ethos — the shared commitment to mutual concern — is absent, and the institutions are being undermined by self-interested personal choices.
- When arguing that justice requires more than institutional design: it requires persons who are genuinely committed to the well-being of others.
- When the camping trip ideal needs to be connected to institutional practice.

## Core Concept
Cohen argues that a society with just institutions but without a just ethos has not achieved justice. The ethos is the shared commitment of individuals to act justly in their personal choices — not merely to support just institutions from a distance. A society in which the wealthy support progressive taxation but accumulate personal wealth, in which professionals advocate for equality but demand premium salaries, in which development practitioners work for the poor but live in luxury — this society has just institutions and an unjust ethos.

The community ideal — persons motivated by mutual concern rather than self-interest — is not utopian. It describes how we actually live in small communities: families, friendships, and (as the camping trip shows) temporary groups of cooperators. The challenge is scaling this ethos. Cohen is honest about the difficulty: the conditions that sustain community in small groups (personal relationships, mutual visibility, shared experience) are harder to maintain in large societies. But the difficulty does not change the demand: justice requires community, not merely institutions.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess the ethos.** "Does this organisation operate with a community ethos — mutual concern, shared commitment, non-exploitation? Or does it operate with a market ethos — self-interest, competition, extraction of advantage?"
2. **Connect institutions to ethos.** "Just institutions require a just ethos to function. Anti-corruption institutions without a culture of integrity fail. Participatory mechanisms without a culture of genuine engagement fail. Design for both."
3. **Model community.** "Leadership should model the community ethos: shared sacrifice, transparent compensation, genuine commitment to those the organisation serves. If leadership does not model community, the institution will not sustain it."
4. **Acknowledge the difficulty.** "Community at scale is hard. The conditions of modern society — anonymity, complexity, inequality — work against it. Acknowledge the difficulty while maintaining the demand."

## Examples
**Situation:** A development organisation has excellent policies: gender policy, anti-corruption policy, environmental policy, safeguarding policy. But staff describe a culture of competition, hierarchy, and self-interest. The policies exist on paper; the community ethos does not exist in practice.
**Application:** "The organisation has just institutions without a just ethos. The policies are the institutional structure; the culture is the ethos — and they are misaligned. The anti-corruption policy exists, but staff cut corners when nobody is watching. The gender policy exists, but male staff dominate meetings while female staff do the invisible work. The safeguarding policy exists, but power imbalances within the organisation go unchallenged. The remedy is not more policies. It is a community ethos: a shared commitment to mutual concern that governs personal behaviour, not merely institutional design. This requires: leadership that models the ethos (shared sacrifice, not hierarchical privilege); transparent practices (open salary scales, visible decision-making); and accountability to values, not merely to procedures (asking 'did we act with mutual concern?' not merely 'did we follow the policy?')."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present community as achievable by institutional design alone.
**Why:** Institutions create the conditions for community but cannot produce it. Community requires personal commitment — an ethos that governs choices institutions cannot reach.

**Don't:** Demand perfection.
**Why:** The community ethos is an aspiration, not a demand for saintliness. The demand is for genuine effort toward mutual concern, not for perfect selflessness.
