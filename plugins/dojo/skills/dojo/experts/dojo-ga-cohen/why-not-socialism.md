# Why Not Socialism?

## When to Use
- When the principles governing small communities (sharing, reciprocity, mutual concern) are being dismissed as inapplicable to larger social arrangements.
- When presenting Cohen's camping trip thought experiment as a challenge to the naturalisation of market logic.
- When asking why principles that are obviously just among friends are abandoned when we organise society.

## Core Concept
In *Why Not Socialism?* (2009), Cohen presents a deceptively simple thought experiment. Imagine a group of friends on a camping trip. They share food, equipment, and tasks. No one hoards. No one charges others for the use of their skills. The person who is good at fishing does not demand extra food in exchange for fish. The person with the best tent does not rent it to others. The group operates on two principles: equality (each person has an equal claim to the benefits of the trip) and community (each person is motivated by concern for others, not by self-interest).

Cohen's question: why are these principles — which we naturally apply among friends — abandoned when we organise society? Why does the person who shares generously on the camping trip demand market rates for their labour in the economy? Why does the person who would never exploit a friend's need on the trip exploit the needs of strangers in the market?

The standard objection is feasibility: camping trip principles work among friends but cannot scale to society. Cohen takes this objection seriously — he distinguishes desirability from feasibility. The camping trip demonstrates that socialist principles (equality and community) are desirable — we spontaneously apply them when we are not distorted by market incentives. The question of whether they are feasible at scale is separate and harder. But the feasibility question does not affect the desirability claim: even if we cannot achieve a camping-trip society, we should want to — and we should organise our efforts to get as close as possible.

## How to Apply
1. **Apply the camping trip test.** "Would this arrangement be acceptable among friends on a camping trip? If the answer is no — if the arrangement involves exploitation, hoarding, or the leveraging of advantage — ask why it is acceptable in society."
2. **Separate desirability from feasibility.** "The objection that socialism is impractical does not address the question of whether it is desirable. If socialist principles are desirable — and the camping trip shows they are — then the practical question is how to approximate them, not whether to abandon them."
3. **Challenge the naturalisation of self-interest.** "The camping trip shows that self-interest is not the natural human motivation — mutual concern is. Self-interest is the motivation that market institutions cultivate. Different institutions could cultivate different motivations."
4. **Design for community.** "Institutions that reward self-interest produce self-interest. Institutions that reward mutual concern produce mutual concern. Design development institutions for community, not for competition."

## Examples
**Situation:** A development organisation operates a competitive grants programme in which community organisations compete for funding. The programme is designed to "incentivise quality" — the best proposals receive funding, and weaker organisations receive nothing.
**Application:** "Apply the camping trip test. On the camping trip, if one friend had better cooking skills and another had weaker skills, would the group give all the food to the good cook and let the weak cook go hungry? They would not — because among friends, the principle is mutual support, not competition. The grants programme applies a different principle: competition. The organisations that are already strong (with grant-writing capacity, donor networks, and institutional infrastructure) win the grants. The organisations that are weak (genuinely grassroots, lacking proposal-writing cultural capital) lose — not because they are less effective but because they are less competitive in the specific terms the programme rewards. The programme says this incentivises quality. Cohen would say it rewards structural advantage and calls it merit. An alternative: allocate some funding through competitive mechanisms (where competition genuinely serves quality) and some through collaborative mechanisms (where community organisations support each other, share resources, and build capacity collectively). Design for community, not only for competition."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present socialism as immediately achievable.
**Why:** Cohen is clear that the feasibility question is genuine and hard. The camping trip demonstrates desirability, not feasibility at scale.

**Don't:** Dismiss the thought experiment as naive.
**Why:** The thought experiment reveals a genuine moral truth: we already know how to live justly. We do it naturally among friends. The question of why we abandon these principles in society is philosophically serious, not naive.
