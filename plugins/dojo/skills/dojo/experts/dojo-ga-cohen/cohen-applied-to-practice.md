# Cohen Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When translating Cohen's philosophical analysis into practical professional exercises.
- When conducting equality audits of organisations, programmes, or policies.
- When the practitioner needs concrete steps for applying egalitarian principles.

## Core Concept
Five practices emerge from Cohen's work for the development professional:

First, the equality audit. For any organisation or programme, assess the distribution of compensation, power, knowledge, and voice. Map the inequalities. Name them. Ask: is each inequality justified by genuine reasons (compensating for sacrifice, addressing genuine need) or by unjustified reasons (incentive arguments, market rates, historical privilege)?

Second, the exploitation scan. For any economic relationship the programme promotes or facilitates (contract farming, microfinance, labour markets), ask: does one party extract surplus from another who has no realistic alternative? If so, the programme is facilitating exploitation — regardless of whether both parties "consent."

Third, the freedom disaggregation. For any "freedom" the programme promotes (market access, labour market flexibility, economic opportunity), ask: freedom for whom? At whose expense? Name both the beneficiaries and the bearers of the cost.

Fourth, the camping trip check. For any organisational practice that feels uncomfortable, apply the camping trip test: would this be acceptable among friends on a shared project? If not, it needs justification — and "market rates" may not suffice.

Fifth, the personal-choice examination. For the practitioner personally: does your life reflect your egalitarian commitments? Not perfectly — Cohen does not demand saintliness — but substantively. The gap between your principles and your practice is the personal-choice question.

## How to Apply
1. **Conduct the equality audit annually.** "Map compensation ratios, power distributions, and knowledge hierarchies within the organisation. Compare with the egalitarian standard. Identify the gaps and propose reforms."
2. **Screen programme relationships for exploitation.** "For every economic relationship the programme promotes, ask: is there surplus extraction without realistic alternatives? If so, redesign the relationship — don't merely facilitate it."
3. **Disaggregate every freedom claim.** "When the programme promotes 'economic freedom,' specify: whose freedom? At whose cost? If the answer is 'freedom for the powerful at the cost of the vulnerable,' the programme is not promoting freedom."
4. **Confront the personal-choice question honestly.** "The practitioner who earns a comfortable salary while working on poverty is not hypocritical — but is not exempt from the question. What does your personal life say about your egalitarian commitments?"

## Examples
**Situation:** A development practitioner wants to apply Cohen's egalitarian principles but feels overwhelmed by the gap between the philosophical demand (radical equality) and the practical reality (a deeply unequal world and an unequal industry).
**Application:** "The gap between the demand and the reality is real. Cohen does not deny it. But the gap is not a reason to abandon the demand — it is the space within which egalitarian work occurs. Five practical steps, in order of difficulty. First, the equality audit: conduct it within your own organisation. Map the compensation ratios. Name the inequalities. This takes one day and produces a document that can catalyse reform. Second, the exploitation scan: in your next programme design, screen every economic relationship for exploitation. Name what you find. Third, the freedom disaggregation: in your next policy proposal, specify whose freedom is expanded and whose is constrained. Fourth, the camping trip check: apply it to one organisational practice per month. Share the results with your team. Fifth, the personal-choice examination: once per year, honestly assess whether your personal life reflects your egalitarian commitments. Not as self-flagellation but as strategic assessment: what am I willing to change, and what am I not? The philosophical demand for radical equality remains. Your practice will fall short. But the practice — the audit, the scan, the disaggregation, the check, the examination — moves the organisation and the practitioner in the right direction."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the protocol as sufficient.
**Why:** These practices diagnose inequality and exploitation. They do not, alone, produce structural change. The diagnosis must lead to reform — institutional, organisational, and personal.

**Don't:** Skip the personal-choice question.
**Why:** The personal-choice question is uncomfortable. It is also non-negotiable for anyone who claims egalitarian commitments. Cohen insists: your life is evidence of your principles.
