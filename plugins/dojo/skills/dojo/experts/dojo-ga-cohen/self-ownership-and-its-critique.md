# Self-Ownership and Its Critique

## When to Use
- When property rights, market outcomes, or talent-based rewards are being justified by appeal to self-ownership: "I own myself, I own my talents, therefore I own what my talents produce."
- When Nozick's libertarian argument needs to be engaged with seriously and dismantled rigorously.
- When the self-ownership thesis is operating as the unexamined philosophical foundation of market-based development.

## Core Concept
In *Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality* (1995), Cohen engages with Nozick's self-ownership thesis — the claim that each person owns themselves, and that this self-ownership entails the right to the fruits of one's labour without taxation or redistribution. This thesis is the philosophical foundation of libertarian capitalism: if I own myself, and I produce something through my own effort, then the product is mine, and any attempt to take it (through taxation) is a form of forced labour.

Cohen's critique proceeds in two stages. First, he acknowledges the intuitive force of self-ownership. The claim that you own yourself — that your body, your labour, your choices are yours — has genuine moral appeal. It captures something important about autonomy and dignity. Cohen does not dismiss this intuition; he takes it seriously.

Second, he shows that self-ownership, consistently applied, produces morally intolerable conclusions. If self-ownership is absolute — if no redistribution can be justified because it violates the self-owner's rights — then a society in which some persons starve while others have surplus is just, because the surplus-holders acquired their wealth through legitimate self-owned labour. This conclusion is morally monstrous. The self-ownership thesis, consistently applied, permits the powerful to keep everything while the powerless have nothing — because the powerful "earned" their position through the exercise of their self-owned talents.

The deeper point: talent is not earned. The person who has a talent for surgery, or finance, or management did not choose that talent — it is a product of genetic endowment, social conditions, and opportunities the person did not create. Self-ownership treats talent as a personal possession to be deployed for personal gain. The egalitarian treats talent as a social endowment that creates obligations to the community.

## How to Apply
1. **Interrogate the self-ownership premise.** "When someone says 'I earned it, it's mine,' ask: what did you earn? Your talent? Your opportunities? Your social position? The conditions that made your earning possible? If these were not earned, the earnings themselves have a social dimension."
2. **Apply the starvation test.** "Does this arrangement permit some persons to starve while others have surplus? If the justification is 'the surplus-holders earned it,' the self-ownership thesis is producing a morally intolerable conclusion."
3. **Distinguish self-ownership from autonomy.** "The intuition behind self-ownership — that persons should have autonomy over their bodies and choices — is valid. But autonomy does not entail unlimited property rights. You can respect autonomy while requiring that the fruits of socially produced talent be shared."
4. **Connect to development.** "The global self-ownership argument: wealthy countries 'earned' their wealth through their own efforts. Therefore they have no obligation to share. But their wealth was produced through colonial extraction, structural advantage, and global institutions designed to benefit them. The 'earning' claim is false at the global level."

## Examples
**Situation:** A policy debate over taxation features the argument that high earners should not be taxed heavily because they "earned" their income through talent, effort, and risk-taking, and that taxation is a form of forced redistribution that violates their property rights.
**Application:** "The argument rests on the self-ownership thesis: I own myself, I own my talents, therefore I own what my talents produce. Cohen's critique: talent is not earned. The surgeon's dexterity, the financier's analytical ability, the entrepreneur's social confidence — these are products of genetic endowment, family socialisation, educational opportunity, and social conditions. The high earner did not create these advantages — she was born into them, or was placed in conditions that developed them. Self-ownership treats these unearned advantages as personal property; equality treats them as social endowments that create obligations. The taxation debate is not about whether the high earner 'deserves' to keep her income. It is about whether unearned talent, deployed in a social context that makes its exercise profitable, creates obligations to those who lack the same advantages. Cohen's answer: it does. Not because the high earner is bad but because justice requires that the benefits of socially produced advantages be shared."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss the self-ownership intuition.
**Why:** The intuition that persons have autonomy over their own bodies and choices is genuinely important. Cohen takes it seriously. The critique is not that self-ownership is wrong but that it does not entail the libertarian conclusions drawn from it.

**Don't:** Reduce the critique to "tax the rich."
**Why:** The philosophical point is deeper than fiscal policy. It is about the moral status of talent, the social production of advantage, and the obligations that unearned privilege creates.
