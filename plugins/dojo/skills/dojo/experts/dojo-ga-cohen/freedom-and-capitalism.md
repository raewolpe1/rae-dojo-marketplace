# Freedom and Capitalism

## When to Use
- When "freedom" is invoked to justify market arrangements without specifying whose freedom is being expanded and whose is being constrained.
- When the claim that capitalism is the system of freedom needs to be interrogated.
- When Cohen's argument that the proletarian is unfree under capitalism is relevant.

## Core Concept
Cohen argues that capitalism does not produce freedom — it produces freedom for some at the expense of others. The property owner is free to use their property as they choose. But the propertyless worker is not free — she must sell her labour to survive, and her "choice" of employer is constrained by the fact that all employers are property owners who benefit from her lack of alternatives.

The key argument: the proletarian's unfreedom is not merely an unfortunate circumstance — it is constitutive of capitalism. The capitalist system requires a class of persons who must sell their labour because they own nothing else. This requirement is not accidental — it is structural. The freedom of the capitalist class is purchased by the unfreedom of the working class.

Cohen makes this concrete through the analogy of the collectively unfree individual. Any individual worker can leave the working class (by becoming an entrepreneur, by acquiring property). But the working class as a whole cannot leave — capitalism requires that most people remain workers. The individual's exit is possible only because most individuals do not exit. This is collective unfreedom: each worker is individually "free" to leave, but the class as a whole is not free, because the system requires their continued presence.

For development practice, the freedom audit is essential. When "economic freedom" is promoted — free markets, deregulation, privatisation — ask: freedom for whom? The freedom to start a business requires capital. The freedom to choose an employer requires alternatives. The freedom to negotiate a wage requires bargaining power. Without these preconditions, "economic freedom" is the freedom of the powerful to operate without constraint while the powerless remain constrained by the very structures that the powerful's freedom creates.

## How to Apply
1. **Disaggregate freedom.** "Whose freedom is expanded by this arrangement? Whose is constrained? Name both. The freedom of the employer to hire and fire is the unfreedom of the worker to refuse exploitative terms."
2. **Identify the structural compulsion.** "Is the worker choosing to sell her labour, or is she compelled by the absence of alternatives? If compelled, the arrangement is not freedom — it is structural coercion dressed in the language of choice."
3. **Apply to development.** "When programmes promote 'economic freedom' — market liberalisation, privatisation, deregulation — ask: freedom for whom? The rural farmer who is 'free' to sell her crops on the open market but has no bargaining power, no storage, no market information, and only one buyer is not free. She is structurally compelled."
4. **Distinguish formal from substantive freedom.** "The formal freedom to participate in the market is meaningless without the substantive conditions — capital, information, bargaining power, alternatives — that make market participation genuinely free."

## Examples
**Situation:** A structural adjustment programme promotes labour market flexibility — making it easier for employers to hire and fire workers. The programme argues this increases freedom: employers are free to respond to market conditions, and workers are free to seek better opportunities.
**Application:** "The freedom analysis is asymmetric. The employer's freedom to fire is genuinely expanded — they can now adjust their workforce to market conditions without constraint. The worker's freedom is formally expanded (she can seek other employment) but substantively constrained: if there are few employers, if the labour market is oversupplied, if she has dependants and no savings, her 'freedom' to seek alternatives is theoretical. In practice, she is more vulnerable — more easily dismissed, less protected, more dependent on the employer's willingness to retain her. The programme has expanded the freedom of the propertied class (employers) and reduced the security of the propertyless class (workers) while describing both changes as 'freedom.' Cohen's freedom audit: label each freedom and its beneficiary. The employer's freedom to fire is real. The worker's freedom to seek alternatives is, for most workers, notional. The programme has produced what Cohen diagnoses: freedom for some purchased at the cost of unfreedom for others."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Deny that capitalism provides any freedom.
**Why:** Capitalism does provide freedoms — freedom of contract, freedom of movement, freedom of enterprise. The point is not that these freedoms are illusory but that they are asymmetrically distributed.

**Don't:** Romanticise alternatives.
**Why:** Historical alternatives to capitalism (Soviet communism, Maoist collectivism) produced their own unfreedoms. The argument is not that any alternative is better but that capitalism's claim to be "the system of freedom" must be interrogated.
