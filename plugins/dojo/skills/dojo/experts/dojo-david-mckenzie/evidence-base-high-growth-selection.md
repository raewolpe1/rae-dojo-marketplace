# Evidence Base — Identifying and Backing High-Growth Firms

> **Provenance.** Figures compiled July 2026 from the sources named in each entry. Verify against the primary source before quoting in a client deliverable. Entries marked `[CHECK]` have not been verified to the primary source and must not be quoted without checking.

## When to Use
- Business plan competitions, accelerators, selective grant instruments, gazelle programmes.
- When asked whether high-growth potential can be identified ex ante.

## Fails When
- **The population is survivalist.** This file is about the small transformational group.

## Headline

**This is the strongest positive result in the MSME evidence base.** Selection plus a large cheque works. Selection plus a workshop does not.

## THE EVIDENCE

### The Nigeria business plan competition — the flagship result
- **Study:** McKenzie (2017), *American Economic Review* 107(8): 2278–2307, "Identifying and Spurring High-Growth Entrepreneurship: Experimental Evidence from a Business Plan Competition".
- **Country:** Nigeria (YouWiN! — Youth Enterprise With Innovation in Nigeria, launched late 2011).
- **Intervention:** A national business plan competition. Almost 24,000 applications to start a new business or expand an existing one. Top 6,000 applications selected for a **4-day business plan training course**. Plans then scored by a panel. Top-scoring plans overall and within region won automatically; **729 additional winners were randomly selected from 1,841 semi-finalists**, providing experimental variation from roughly US$34–36 million in grants. Each winner received approximately **US$50,000**. Applicants tracked over five years.
- **Finding:** Winning leads to **greater firm entry, more survival, higher profits and sales, and higher employment**, including increases of **over 20 percentage points in the likelihood of a firm having ten or more workers**. In 2014, new firms were **22.9 percentage points** more likely to have more than ten employees as a result of winning, against a control group in which **11% had ten or more workers in 2014 and 12% in 2016**. Reported elsewhere: for new firms, a **23pp** increase in the likelihood of ten or more workers alongside a **23% increase in profits**; for existing firms, a **21pp** increase in the likelihood of ten or more workers `[CHECK]`.
- **Caveat:** Nigeria; young applicants (under 40); a self-selected applicant pool of people willing to write a business plan; grants of a size (US$50,000) far above anything typical in South African MSME programming. **The result is about selection *and* cheque size jointly** — neither alone is the finding.

### Resilience through a macroeconomic shock
- **Study:** McKenzie (2017), as above — extended follow-up after Nigeria's recession.
- **Country:** Nigeria.
- **Intervention:** As above.
- **Finding:** The businesses proved **resilient to the economic downturn**. Experimental winners with new companies remained **24.1 percentage points** more likely to still be in operation and **11.1 percentage points** more likely to have ten or more workers than non-winners. Experimental winners with existing companies were **9.5 percentage points** more likely to have survived and **12.7 percentage points** more likely to have ten or more workers.
- **Caveat:** The recession result matters for South African application: it is evidence that the effect is not a fair-weather artefact.

### Innovation effects
- **Study:** McKenzie (2017), as above.
- **Country:** Nigeria.
- **Finding:** Participants showed an increase in innovative practices of the order of **14% to 22%** relative to non-participants `[CHECK]`. Much of the innovation was **not new to the world but new to parts of Nigeria** — and was impactful for that reason.
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]` on the specific range. The "new to the local market, not new to the world" framing is directly transferable to a provincial innovation strategy and is probably the more useful part of the finding.

### General equilibrium check
- **Study:** McKenzie (2015 working paper version / 2017 AER).
- **Country:** Nigeria.
- **Finding:** The author tested and found that **YouWiN! firms did not destroy or generate jobs in other firms outside the experimental sample** to a degree that would materially change the estimate. Accounting for spillovers changed the estimated employment creation by around 100 jobs.
- **Caveat:** Nigeria is a large, growing, non-saturated market. **This displacement result should not be assumed to transfer to a saturated local market**, which is precisely the condition in much South African township retail. See `external-validity-and-scale.md`.

### What the competition actually bought
- **Interpretation (McKenzie's own):** The competition did two separable things — it **identified** firms with genuine growth potential (Figure 2 of the paper shows the programme successfully selected individuals more likely than the average firm in the economy to reach ten or more workers), and it **financed** them at a scale that mattered.
- **Caveat:** The identification component may be the more valuable and the less replicable. It required a scored, panel-assessed, competitive process over 24,000 applications. Provinces attempting this at 1/50th the scale will have a much weaker screen.

## How to Apply

1. **Treat selection as the intervention.** The screen is not administrative overhead; it is the mechanism.
2. **Match the cheque size to the transformation required.** US$50,000 was decisive. A R50,000 grant to the same firm would not have been.
3. **Accept a small programme.** A few hundred firms, not fifteen thousand. That is what the evidence supports.
4. **Use the randomised margin.** YouWiN! randomised among semi-finalists — a design that is both fair (all were qualified) and evaluable. This is a directly copyable design feature.
5. **Do not assume the no-displacement result transfers.** Nigeria was not saturated. Your township market may be.
6. **Steal the innovation framing.** "New to this market, not new to the world" is the right ambition for a provincial innovation agenda.

## Examples

**Situation:** A province wants a high-growth firm programme and has R30 million.

**Application:** On the Nigerian design, R30m at a meaningful cheque size (say R750,000 — still far below US$50,000 but plausibly transformative for a small SA firm) funds about 40 firms. That is the honest scale. The design: open competitive application, panel scoring, and — critically — **randomise among the qualified semi-finalists who cannot all be funded**. This gives a fair allocation *and* a rigorous evaluation for free. Expected effect, applying the Nigerian result with heavy discount for cheque size and market saturation: a meaningful increase in the probability of reaching ten-plus employees among winners, with displacement unknown and worth measuring.

**Result:** A small, selective, evaluable instrument built on the strongest evidence in the field.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Run the competition and then spread the money thinly.
**Why:** The finding is selection *and* cheque size. Thin cheques to many winners is a different, untested intervention.

**Don't:** Cite the 20+pp employment effect for a programme with a small grant.
**Why:** The effect is attached to US$50,000. It does not travel with the format alone.

**Don't:** Assume no displacement.
**Why:** Nigeria was a large, growing market. Saturation is the condition under which the result fails, and it is the SA township condition.
