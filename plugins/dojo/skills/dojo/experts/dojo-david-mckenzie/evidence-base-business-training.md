# Evidence Base — Business Training and Management Support

> **Provenance.** Figures compiled July 2026 from the sources named in each entry. Verify against the primary source before quoting in a client deliverable. Entries marked `[CHECK]` have not been verified to the primary source and must not be quoted without checking.

## When to Use
- Any proposal involving training, BDS, mentoring, coaching, or management support to enterprises.
- When "training doesn't work" or "we need capacity building" is asserted.

## Fails When
- **The training is for wage employment** (job search, vocational) — different literature.

## Headline

| Intervention | Effect on practices | Effect on profits | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conventional business training (finance, records, marketing) | +0.05 to 0.10 of practices taught | Small, usually insignificant | Weak treatment, not a weak theory |
| Personal initiative (psychology-based) training | Substantial | **+30%** | Supported |
| Individual consulting | Positive | Positive | Expensive |
| Group-based consulting | Positive | Positive | Cheaper and more effective than individual `[CHECK]` |

## THE EVIDENCE

### The critical review
- **Study:** McKenzie & Woodruff (2014), *World Bank Research Observer* 29(1): 48–82, "What Are We Learning from Business Training and Entrepreneurship Evaluations around the Developing World?"
- **Country:** Multi-country review.
- **Intervention:** Business training programmes, heterogeneous in length, content, and firm type.
- **Finding:** Relatively modest impacts of training on the survivorship of existing firms over short horizons; **stronger evidence that training programmes help prospective owners launch new businesses more quickly**.
- **Caveat:** The review's principal message is methodological. Many evaluations suffer small sample sizes, measure impacts only within a year of training, and have problems with survey attrition and with the measurement of profits and revenues — all of which limit the conclusions that can be drawn.

### The reassessment — why the nulls are uninformative
- **Study:** McKenzie (2021), World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 9408, "Small Business Training to Improve Management Practices in Developing Countries: Reassessing the Evidence for 'Training Doesn't Work'".
- **Country:** Multi-country synthesis.
- **Intervention:** Business training across the RCT literature.
- **Finding:** Most studies show **statistically significant impacts of training on business practices**, of the order of **+0.05 to +0.10 in the proportion of practices implemented** — i.e. for every 20 practices taught, firms invited to training implement on average **one additional practice**. Effects on sales and profits found in these studies are consistent with the predicted effects given the observed change in practices. Almost all of the first wave of randomised trials **lacked the power to detect a 25% increase in profits**; confidence intervals are typically wide, most including the possibility that training increased profits and sales by 25%.
- **Caveat:** This is the single most important entry in this file. It means "training doesn't work" is **not** supported by the literature — the correct conclusion is that most existing training programmes have effects too weak to generate statistically significant effects on profits. Business practices matter (their association with profits and sales is strong); short courses do not change them much.

### Personal initiative training — the exception that should reshape design
- **Study:** Campos, Frese, Goldstein, Iacovone, Johnson, McKenzie & Mensmann (2017), *Science* 357(6357): 1287, "Teaching personal initiative beats traditional training in boosting small business in West Africa".
- **Country:** Togo.
- **Intervention:** Three-arm RCT among microenterprise owners: control (n=500); a leading traditional business training programme (n=500); psychology-based personal initiative training teaching a proactive mindset and entrepreneurial behaviours (n=500). Four follow-up surveys over 2 years.
- **Finding:** Personal initiative training **increased firm profits by 30%**, compared with a **statistically insignificant 11%** for traditional training. **The training paid for itself within one year.**
- **Caveat:** Togo; microenterprises; a specific curriculum delivered with fidelity. The result is about the *mechanism* (proactive mindset, self-starting behaviour) rather than about "training" generically.

### Personal initiative at seven years — and the gender divergence
- **Study:** Campos, Frese, Iacovone, Johnson, McKenzie & Mensmann (2025), *American Economic Review: Insights* 7(4): 463–479, "Long-Term and Lasting Impacts of Personal Initiative Training on Entrepreneurial Success". (Earlier as World Bank PRWP 10938, 2024.)
- **Country:** Togo (7-year follow-up of the 2017 trial).
- **Intervention:** As above.
- **Finding:** Long-lasting average impacts of **US$91 higher profits per month** — **larger than the two-year impacts**.
- **Caveat:** **The long-term impacts differ sharply by gender.** The impact for men grows over time as they accumulate capital and increase self-efficacy; **the impact for women dissipates**, and capital build-up is much more limited. Any programme built on this finding will build a gender gap unless the capital-accumulation channel is addressed for women directly.

### Business practices and firm performance
- **Study:** McKenzie & Woodruff (2017), "Business Practices in Small Firms in Developing Countries", *Management Science*. `[CHECK]` — journal/volume unverified.
- **Country:** Multi-country (business practices instrument applied across samples).
- **Intervention:** Measurement, not treatment — a 26-item instrument covering marketing, stock-keeping, record-keeping and financial planning.
- **Finding:** Business practices are strongly associated with firm profits and sales. This reconciles the training literature: practices matter, training barely moves them.
- **Caveat:** Association, not causation, in the cross-section.

### Group-based consulting
- **Study:** Iacovone, McKenzie and co-authors. `[CHECK]` — full citation unverified.
- **Country:** `[CHECK]`
- **Intervention:** Group-based versus individual consulting for SMEs.
- **Finding:** By leveraging peer-learning effects, group-based consulting can be **cheaper and more effective** than individual interventions in improving SME performance.
- **Caveat:** Cited here from a VoxDev summary of McKenzie's work; **verify the primary source before use**. Directionally, this is the most policy-relevant recent finding for BDS cost structures.

## How to Apply

1. **Refuse the label.** Ask what the training teaches, for how long, with what follow-up. "Business training" is not a treatment.
2. **Expect one practice in twenty.** Then ask whether one additional practice out of twenty could plausibly produce the profit effect the proposal claims. It cannot.
3. **Switch the mechanism, not the budget.** If the proposal is conventional training, the evidence-based redesign is personal initiative training, not more hours of the same.
4. **Plan for the gender divergence at the outset.** The seven-year Togo result is the warning: without a capital channel for women, the intervention's benefits diverge over time.
5. **Never cite the nulls as evidence of no effect.** They were underpowered.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy proposes accredited BDS to 15,000 micro-enterprises with a target of improved profitability.

**Application:** On the evidence, expect practices to move by about one in twenty and profits not to move measurably. The redesign: replace the curriculum with personal initiative training (30% profit effect, pays for itself within a year), cut the reach to what can be delivered with fidelity, and build an explicit capital-access component for women participants because the seven-year evidence shows their gains otherwise dissipate.

**Result:** A smaller programme with a real expected effect, rather than a large one with none.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Conclude "training doesn't work".
**Why:** Not supported. The programmes tested were weak; the trials were underpowered. Those are different claims.

**Don't:** Assume all training is the same.
**Why:** 30% versus a statistically insignificant 11% is the difference between the two curricula in the same trial, in the same country, at the same time.

**Don't:** Report the Togo average without the gender split.
**Why:** At seven years the effect grows for men and dissipates for women. The average will be used to justify something that does not work for half the participants.
