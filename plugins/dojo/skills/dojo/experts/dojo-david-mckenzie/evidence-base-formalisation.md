# Evidence Base — Formalisation

> **Provenance.** Figures compiled July 2026 from the sources named in each entry. Verify against the primary source before quoting in a client deliverable. Entries marked `[CHECK]` have not been verified to the primary source and must not be quoted without checking.

## When to Use
- Any registration drive, one-stop shop, formalisation incentive, or simplification agenda.
- Whenever formalisation is claimed to produce tax revenue, finance access, or firm growth.

## Fails When
- **The question is protective regulation or informal workers' rights.** Route to Chen — different frame entirely.

## Headline

| Claim | Evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Formalisation can be induced cheaply | Yes — registration responds to cost reduction and information | Supported |
| Business registration → tax registration | No | **Not supported** |
| Formalisation → tax revenue | No (registration ≠ tax registration) | **Not supported** |
| Carrots (information, free registration) work | Weakly at best | Not supported |
| Sticks (inspection, enforcement) work | Modestly | Partially supported `[CHECK]` |
| Formalisation → firm growth | Little evidence | Untested at scale |

## THE EVIDENCE

### "Registration" is not one thing
- **Study:** Kanbur & Keen (2015), cited in McKenzie's work; and the review literature.
- **Country:** Conceptual / multi-country.
- **Intervention:** N/A — a definitional point with large empirical consequences.
- **Finding:** Almost no attention has been given to the reality that **firms nearly always face several different explicit or implicit tax regimes**. "Registration" in recent studies covers a highly heterogeneous set of processes: obtaining a business licence from a chamber of commerce or municipal government; registering with a registrar of legal entities, of businesses, or of microenterprises; obtaining a taxpayer identification number; registering at a one-stop shop encompassing multiple agencies; or registering with multiple agencies and receiving multiple licences and certificates.
- **Caveat:** This is the most important entry in this file for strategy drafting. **A formalisation target is meaningless unless it specifies which registration.** Most South African policy documents do not.

### The Malawi result — registration without tax
- **Study:** Campos, Goldstein & McKenzie, work on formalisation assistance and bank information in Malawi. `[CHECK]` — full citation, journal and figures unverified. Includes Campos, Goldstein & McKenzie (2015), "Short-term Impacts of Formalization Assistance and a Bank Information Session on Business Registration and Access to Finance for Firms in Malawi".
- **Country:** Malawi.
- **Intervention:** Low-cost formalisation assistance, with and without a bank information session.
- **Finding:** A **cheap intervention increased firm registration but failed to impact tax registration**, negating potential tax revenue. Access-to-finance effects appeared where the bank information session was combined with registration.
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]`. The headline is well-attested in secondary summaries of McKenzie's work; the specific magnitudes are not verified here. The structural point stands: **the fiscal rationale for formalisation drives does not survive contact with the evidence**, because the registration you can induce is not the registration that raises revenue.

### Demand for formalisation — Sri Lanka
- **Study:** de Mel, McKenzie & Woodruff (2013), "The Demand for, and Consequences of, Formalization among Informal Firms in Sri Lanka", *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*. `[CHECK]` — volume/pages unverified.
- **Country:** Sri Lanka.
- **Intervention:** Randomised offers: information about registration; reimbursement of registration costs; and larger cash payments conditional on registering.
- **Finding:** Information and cost reimbursement alone induced **little formalisation**. Larger payments induced more — but a substantial share of firms **still did not register even when paid**, indicating that the barrier is not primarily the direct cost of registration.
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]` magnitudes. The interpretive point is robust: if firms will not formalise when paid amounts comparable to months of profit, then "reducing red tape" is not addressing the binding constraint on their formalisation.

### Carrots versus sticks
- **Study:** de Andrade, Bruhn & McKenzie (2016), "A Helping Hand or the Long Arm of the Law? Experimental Evidence on What Governments Can Do to Formalize Firms", *World Bank Economic Review*. `[CHECK]` — volume/pages unverified.
- **Country:** Brazil (Belo Horizonte).
- **Intervention:** Randomised arms including information about registration, free registration and accounting support, and **inspector visits**.
- **Finding:** Information and free registration had **no significant effect**. **Only the enforcement arm — inspector visits — increased formalisation**, and modestly.
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]`. Brazil, urban, a specific municipal enforcement regime. The uncomfortable policy implication — that the only thing that reliably formalises firms is enforcement — should be stated plainly, and then weighed against the welfare consequences for survivalist firms, which is Chen's terrain, not McKenzie's.

### Entry regulation and formalisation — the review
- **Study:** Bruhn & McKenzie (2014), "Entry Regulation and the Formalization of Microenterprises in Developing Countries", *World Bank Research Observer*. `[CHECK]` — volume/pages unverified.
- **Country:** Multi-country review.
- **Intervention:** Entry regulation reforms, one-stop shops, simplification.
- **Finding:** Reforms to entry regulation have produced **at most modest effects on the formalisation of existing informal firms**; where effects appear, they are largely on new firm registration rather than on the conversion of existing informal firms.
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]`. Consistent with Schoar's structural point: the informal firm is already outside the regulation, so relieving the regulation does not relieve a binding constraint on it.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask which registration.** Business licence? Registrar? Taxpayer ID? One-stop shop? Multiple agencies? Without this the target is undefined and the evidence cannot be applied.
2. **Delete the tax-revenue rationale** unless the intervention specifically produces *tax* registration. Registration and tax registration are different objects and the evidence separates them.
3. **Do not expect carrots to work.** Information and cost reduction have repeatedly produced little. If the theory of change runs through reducing the cost of registration, it is not supported.
4. **Be honest about sticks.** Enforcement works modestly. Whether it should be used against survivalist firms is a normative question that the evidence does not answer.
5. **Separate new-firm registration from conversion of existing informals.** Reforms affect the former far more than the latter.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy proposes a formalisation drive targeting 20,000 informal township enterprises, justified by tax base broadening and improved access to finance.

**Application:** Verdict: the tax rationale is not supported — cheap interventions raise business registration but not tax registration, which negates the revenue benefit. The finance rationale is partially supported but only where registration is bundled with a bank relationship, not from registration alone. The reach target is implausible: even paid substantial sums, many firms decline to register, so the cost of registration is not the binding constraint. Redesign: drop the tax claim; bundle registration with a specific financial product and a bank engagement; cut the target by an order of magnitude; and state which registration is meant.

**Result:** A formalisation initiative with a rationale that survives evaluation.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Claim tax revenue from a formalisation drive.
**Why:** The registration you can induce cheaply is not tax registration. The revenue does not materialise.

**Don't:** Treat "registration" as a single act.
**Why:** Firms face several different explicit or implicit regimes. The word is doing too much work.

**Don't:** Assume reducing registration cost will formalise the informal sector.
**Why:** Firms decline to register even when paid amounts comparable to months of profit. Cost is not the binding constraint.
