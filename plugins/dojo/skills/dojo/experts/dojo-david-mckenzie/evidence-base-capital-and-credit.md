# Evidence Base — Capital, Credit and Subsidies

> **Provenance.** Figures compiled July 2026 from the sources named in each entry. Verify against the primary source before quoting in a client deliverable. Entries marked `[CHECK]` have not been verified to the primary source and must not be quoted without checking.

## When to Use
- Any grant, loan, guarantee, matching grant, or subsidy proposal.
- When "access to finance" is named as the binding constraint.

## Fails When
- **The subject is consumer or household credit** — different literature.

## Headline

| Intervention | Effect | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Cash/in-kind capital grants to microenterprises | Real returns **4.6–5.3%/month** average (Sri Lanka) | Supported — but concentrated |
| The same, for female-owned enterprises | **No positive return detected** | The average is misleading |
| In-kind vs cash grants | Cannot reject equality (Sri Lanka) | Form less important than expected |
| Matching grants | Inconclusive → no effect → negative across studies | **Not supported on current evidence** |
| Wage subsidies | Employment rises during subsidy; **no lasting impact** | Temporary by construction |
| Large selective grants (US$50k) | >20pp increase in 10+ worker probability | Supported — see high-growth file |

## THE EVIDENCE

### Returns to capital in microenterprises — the foundational result
- **Study:** de Mel, McKenzie & Woodruff (2008), *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 123(4): 1329–1372, "Returns to Capital in Microenterprises: Evidence from a Field Experiment".
- **Country:** Sri Lanka.
- **Intervention:** Randomised cash and in-kind grants to microenterprises. Two sizes: 10,000 LKR (≈ three months of median reported profits) and 20,000 LKR (≈ six months). Both large relative to firm size.
- **Finding:** Average real return to capital of **4.6%–5.3% per month (55%–63% per year)**, substantially higher than market interest rates. Returns vary with **entrepreneurial ability** and with **household wealth**; they do **not** vary with risk aversion or uncertainty. Cash and in-kind treatments could not be rejected as equal; the treatment effect was approximately linear (20,000 LKR ≈ double the effect of 10,000 LKR).
- **Caveat:** **Treatment impacts were significantly larger for male-owned enterprises; no positive return was found for female-owned enterprises.** The earlier working paper reported the average as 5.7%/month; cite the published QJE figures. Sri Lanka, post-tsunami context, small firms.

### Why not women? — ruling out the obvious explanation
- **Study:** de Mel, McKenzie & Woodruff, "Are Women More Credit Constrained? Experimental Evidence on Gender and Microenterprise Returns", *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*. `[CHECK]` — volume/pages unverified.
- **Country:** Sri Lanka.
- **Intervention:** As above, analysed by gender.
- **Finding:** The gender gap in returns is **not explained by risk aversion**. Sri Lankan female microenterprise owners were **more** likely than male owners to take risky gambles in lottery games; controlling for the risk-aversion interaction does not change the female interaction, and the result is robust to an alternative risk measure.
- **Caveat:** The paper rules out an explanation rather than establishing one. Do not present the gender gap as understood.

### Returns to capital — Mexico
- **Study:** McKenzie & Woodruff (2006) and subsequent work with Mexican retail SMEs. `[CHECK]` — citations and figures unverified to primary source.
- **Country:** Mexico.
- **Intervention:** Cash and in-kind grants to microenterprises and retail SMEs.
- **Finding:** Returns to capital among the smallest urban microenterprises (under ~US$200 invested) of around **15% per month**; in later work with retail SMEs, estimated returns of **at least 20–33% per month**, concentrated among the firms with the highest financial constraints. Start-up costs of Mexican microenterprises tend to be very low and returns high, which suggests entry costs are unlikely to provide an empirical basis for poverty traps.
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]` — these figures are drawn from secondary summaries. Verify before quoting. The concentration finding (effects in the most constrained firms) is the policy-relevant part.

### Matching grants — the unsupported instrument
- **Study:** McKenzie and co-authors (2015), "The Additionality Impact of a Matching Grant Program for Small Firms: Experimental Evidence from Yemen"; reviewed alongside Tan & Lopez-Acevedo (2005), Bruhn & Love (2012), Bruhn et al. (2012), Rijkers et al. (2010), Karlan et al. (2015), Fiala (2018), Cravo & Piza (2016), Piza et al. (2016), Hristova & Coste (2016).
- **Country:** Yemen and multi-country review.
- **Intervention:** Matching grant facilities for small firms.
- **Finding:** **There is not yet solid evidence that the creation of jobs in SMEs is attributable to matching grants.** The literature spans inconclusive results, negative effects, and non-effects.
- **Caveat:** The heterogeneity across studies is itself partly a reporting-quality problem — more transparency in reporting would narrow the conflicting conclusions. This is a "not supported" verdict, not an "unsupported" one: the instrument has been tried repeatedly without a clear positive result.

### Wage subsidies — temporary by construction
- **Study:** de Mel, McKenzie & Woodruff, "Labor Drops: Experimental Evidence on the Return to Additional Labor in Microenterprises", *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*. `[CHECK]` — volume/pages unverified.
- **Country:** Sri Lanka.
- **Intervention:** Wage subsidies provided to randomly chosen microenterprises to test whether hiring additional labour benefits such firms and whether a short-term subsidy has a lasting effect on firm employment. Twelve rounds of surveys tracking dynamics to four years after treatment.
- **Finding:** Firms **increased employment during the subsidy period**; there was **no lasting impact on firm employment** after it ended.
- **Caveat:** Microenterprises in Sri Lanka. The result does not speak to wage subsidies in larger firms or in labour-market-attachment programmes for youth. But it is decisive against the claim that a temporary subsidy produces permanent jobs in this population.

### The flypaper effect — form of transfer matters for some firms
- **Study:** Fafchamps, McKenzie, Quinn & Woodruff, "Microenterprise growth and the flypaper effect: Evidence from a randomized experiment in Ghana", *Journal of Development Economics*. `[CHECK]` — volume/pages unverified.
- **Country:** Ghana.
- **Intervention:** Randomised cash versus in-kind grants to microenterprises.
- **Finding:** In-kind grants produced growth in female-owned firms **above subsistence level**; cash grants did not — cash is fungible and leaks to the household.
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]`. Note the tension with the Sri Lankan result (cash ≈ in-kind). The reconciliation is likely about household bargaining and firm size, not about the instrument.

## How to Apply

1. **Never quote 4.6–5.3% without the gender caveat.** The average conceals a zero for half the population.
2. **Ask about the cheque size relative to the firm.** The Sri Lankan grants were three to six months of median profits. A grant that is small relative to existing capital will do nothing.
3. **Kill or test matching grants.** On current evidence they are not supported for job creation. If political commitment requires them, attach a real evaluation.
4. **Reframe wage subsidies honestly.** They buy employment while they run. If that is the objective (a demand shock, a crisis response), say so. They do not produce lasting jobs.
5. **Ask whether the constraint is capital or capability.** High returns in the constrained subset means capital binds *there*. It does not mean capital binds everywhere.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy proposes a matching grant facility for SMEs with an employment target.

**Application:** The instrument has been tested repeatedly and the literature runs from inconclusive to no effect to negative; there is not yet solid evidence that SME job creation is attributable to matching grants. Verdict: not supported for the stated outcome. Redesign options: (a) a competitive selective grant at a cheque size that matters — the instrument with the strongest evidence; or (b) retain the matching grant but as a funded experiment with a pre-registered evaluation, at a scale you can afford to have fail.

**Result:** Either a better instrument or a real test — not a third repetition of an unproven one.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Cite the 4.6–5.3% return as a general case for microenterprise lending.
**Why:** No positive return was detected for female-owned enterprises in the same experiment.

**Don't:** Attribute permanent employment to a temporary wage subsidy.
**Why:** Twelve survey rounds over four years in Sri Lanka: employment rose during the subsidy and there was no lasting impact.

**Don't:** Assume cash and in-kind are interchangeable.
**Why:** Sri Lanka could not reject equality; Ghana found in-kind worked for female-owned firms above subsistence where cash did not. The context determines it.
