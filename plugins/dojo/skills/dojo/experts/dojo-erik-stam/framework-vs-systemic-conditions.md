# Framework Conditions vs Systemic Conditions

## When to Use
- When allocating pillar responsibility to a provincial or regional authority.
- When sequencing implementation phases.
- When a strategy makes commitments its owner cannot deliver.

## Fails When
- **The actor is national** and does hold the framework conditions — then the split matters less.

## Core Concept

The split is the most operationally useful part of the model, and it is the part that strategies most often collapse.

**Framework conditions** — formal institutions, culture, physical infrastructure, demand — are **deep, slow, and mostly not in a regional authority's gift**. National legislation sets formal institutions. Culture moves over generations. Physical infrastructure sits with national utilities, other departments, and municipalities. Demand is set by markets and by procurement policy that is largely national.

**Systemic conditions** — networks, leadership, finance, talent, new knowledge, support services — are **interactive, faster-moving, and substantially within a regional actor's reach**. A province can convene networks. It can provide leadership for the ecosystem. It can co-fund finance instruments. It can influence talent through TVET and university partnerships. It can seed knowledge institutions. It can commission and quality-assure support services.

**Why the collapse matters.** A strategy that mixes the classes without naming them does two damaging things. It **over-commits** on framework conditions — promising regulatory reform the province cannot legislate, cultural change it cannot cause, and infrastructure it does not own — which produces a delivery record of failure against its own targets. And it **under-invests** in systemic conditions, where its actual leverage lies, because those look less impressive in a strategy document than "reforming the regulatory environment".

**The honest architecture.** Framework conditions belong in a strategy as *advocacy and influence* commitments with advocacy indicators. Systemic conditions belong as *delivery* commitments with delivery indicators. These are different kinds of promise and should not sit in the same table with the same verbs.

**The caution.** This does not mean framework conditions should be ignored. Demand in particular can be moved by a province through procurement, and the fact that it is classed as a framework condition should not be an excuse. The point is to be honest about the instrument and the timescale.

## How to Apply

1. **Classify every pillar and initiative** as framework or systemic.
2. **Assign the verb accordingly.** Framework: advocate, influence, coordinate, lobby, align. Systemic: deliver, fund, convene, commission, build.
3. **Assign the indicator accordingly.** Framework: advocacy outputs and influence milestones. Systemic: delivery and output measures.
4. **Sequence by timescale.** Systemic conditions can move within a strategy cycle. Framework conditions largely cannot, and should not carry near-term targets.
5. **Test the demand exception.** Procurement is a framework condition a province can genuinely move. Do not use the classification to duck it.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial strategy commits to "reforming the regulatory environment for MSMEs" with a five-year target.

**Application:** Formal institutions are a framework condition. A province can influence but rarely legislate — much of the binding regulation is national (labour, tax, sectoral) or municipal (by-laws, zoning, licensing). The commitment as written will not be delivered and will appear as a failure in year five. Rewrite as two commitments: an **advocacy** commitment on national regulation with influence indicators, and a **delivery** commitment on the municipal by-law and licensing layer, where the province has real convening power and can show results.

**Result:** A commitment the owner can keep, and an honest account of the one it cannot.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Put framework and systemic commitments in the same table with the same verbs.
**Why:** They are different kinds of promise with different owners and timescales.

**Don't:** Promise cultural change within a strategy cycle.
**Why:** Culture is a framework condition. It moves over generations.

**Don't:** Use the classification to avoid demand.
**Why:** Procurement is the one framework condition a province can move directly, and it is the one most often neglected.
