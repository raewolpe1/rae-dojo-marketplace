# Perry Preschool and Abecedarian

## When to Use
- When presenting the empirical foundations for early childhood investment arguments.
- When explaining the channels through which early programmes produce long-term effects.
- When assessing the generalisability of these landmark studies to other contexts.

## Fails When
- **You treat Perry and Abecedarian as proof that all ECD programmes work.** They are proof that specific high-quality programmes for specific disadvantaged populations produced specific long-term gains. Generalising requires understanding the mechanisms and conditions.
- **You emphasise IQ gains as the primary outcome.** The most important finding from both studies is that IQ gains faded but life outcomes improved. The mechanism is through non-cognitive skills, not sustained cognitive gains.

## Core Concept
The Perry Preschool Programme (1962–67) and the Carolina Abecedarian Project (1972–77) are the two most important sources of evidence for the long-term effects of early childhood intervention. Both were RCTs serving disadvantaged African-American children, with follow-up data extending into participants' 40s and 50s.

Perry provided two years of half-day preschool plus weekly home visits to 58 treatment children (with 65 controls) in Ypsilanti, Michigan. Abecedarian provided full-time, year-round centre-based care from infancy to age five for 57 treatment children (with 54 controls) in Chapel Hill, North Carolina. Both programmes were of exceptionally high quality, with trained staff, structured curricula, and low child-to-staff ratios.

Heckman's reanalysis of these datasets (with various collaborators) transformed the interpretation of their results. The headline finding from both studies — significant IQ gains for treatment children — proved temporary; IQ differences largely faded by age ten. Earlier analysts treated the IQ fadeout as disappointing. Heckman showed that this was a misinterpretation.

The lasting effects were not on IQ but on non-cognitive skills — what Heckman calls character skills. Treatment children in Perry showed higher achievement motivation, better classroom behaviour, greater persistence, and stronger social skills. These non-cognitive gains persisted and produced cascading benefits: higher educational attainment, higher employment rates, higher earnings, lower crime, better health, and reduced welfare dependence.

The benefit-cost analysis is striking. Perry's estimated benefit-cost ratio ranges from 7:1 to 12:1, depending on the discount rate and the inclusion of crime reduction benefits. Abecedarian's is similar. These returns materialise over decades, with the largest benefits accruing in the 30s and 40s through sustained employment and reduced crime.

## How to Apply
1. **Evaluate ECD programmes on long-term life outcomes, not just short-term cognitive gains.** IQ gains may fade while non-cognitive gains persist and compound. Assessment at ages three or five captures the least important effects.
2. **Design ECD programmes that explicitly develop non-cognitive skills.** Self-regulation, persistence, social competence, and executive function are the channels through which early programmes produce lasting benefits.
3. **Be cautious about generalising Perry and Abecedarian results.** The programmes were exceptionally high-quality, serving extremely disadvantaged populations with substantial resources. Programmes of lower quality or serving less disadvantaged populations may produce smaller effects.

## Examples
**Situation:** A South African province evaluates its ECD programme after two years and finds modest cognitive gains but no significant effect on school readiness measures. Funders question whether to continue.
**Application:** Heckman's analysis of Perry and Abecedarian would reframe this evaluation. Two-year cognitive gains are the wrong measure of success. Perry showed that cognitive gains faded within five years of programme completion — yet the programme produced massive long-term benefits through non-cognitive channels. The evaluation should assess: are children in the programme developing better self-regulation, persistence, and social skills? Are programme classrooms structured to develop these skills? Are practitioners trained to model and reinforce character development? If the programme is developing non-cognitive skills but not producing cognitive test score gains, it may still be generating large long-term returns that will only be visible decades hence. The funder should invest in measuring non-cognitive outcomes and extending the follow-up period rather than concluding from short-term cognitive measures that the programme has failed.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use Perry's benefit-cost ratio as a universal estimate of ECD returns.
**Why:** Perry's ratio reflects a specific programme, population, quality level, and economic context (including high crime reduction benefits valued at US prices). South African benefit-cost ratios will differ based on local programme quality, wages, crime rates, and social conditions.

**Don't:** Conclude that IQ-focused programmes are justified because Perry showed initial IQ gains.
**Why:** Perry's lasting benefits came through non-cognitive channels, not cognitive ones. Programmes that focus exclusively on cognitive stimulation without developing character skills may miss the mechanism that actually produces long-term returns.
