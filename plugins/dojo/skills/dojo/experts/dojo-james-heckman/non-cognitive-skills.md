# Non-Cognitive Skills

## When to Use
- When explaining the role of character skills in economic outcomes.
- When advising on programme design that should develop both cognitive and non-cognitive skills.
- When evaluating programmes that may have effects through non-cognitive channels.

## Fails When
- **You define non-cognitive skills negatively — as "everything that isn't IQ."** Heckman provides positive definitions: conscientiousness, perseverance, self-regulation, sociability, emotional stability, openness — each with distinct economic effects.
- **You treat non-cognitive skills as fixed personality traits.** They are developable — malleable through environments, programmes, and deliberate practice, with different critical periods than cognitive skills.

## Core Concept
Heckman's work on non-cognitive skills (which he increasingly refers to as "character skills" to emphasise their developability) represents one of his most important contributions to economics. The core finding is that non-cognitive skills — conscientiousness, perseverance, self-regulation, sociability, emotional stability — predict economic and social outcomes at least as strongly as cognitive skills, and in some domains more strongly.

The evidence comes from multiple sources. Analysis of the Perry Preschool data showed that the programme's lasting benefits operated through character skills, not IQ gains. The GED testing programme showed that high-school dropouts who passed cognitive tests but lacked non-cognitive skills earned less and had worse life outcomes than graduates with similar cognitive abilities. Cross-national data shows that conscientiousness and emotional stability predict earnings, employment, health, and longevity independently of cognitive ability.

The economic model is straightforward. Employers value workers who show up reliably (conscientiousness), who persist through difficulty (perseverance), who manage their behaviour and emotions (self-regulation), and who work effectively in teams (sociability). These skills affect productivity directly and reduce the costs of supervision and monitoring. Labour markets reward these skills — and penalise their absence — through wages, employment stability, and career progression.

For policy, the implications are significant. First, education systems that focus exclusively on cognitive development miss half the human capital production function. Curricula, pedagogy, and assessment should develop and measure non-cognitive skills alongside academic competencies. Second, non-cognitive skills are more malleable at later ages than cognitive skills, which provides a window for intervention beyond early childhood — particularly in adolescence, where character development is active. Third, evaluation of human capital programmes should measure non-cognitive outcomes. A programme that improves conscientiousness without raising test scores may be producing substantial long-term economic returns that cognitive measures miss entirely.

## How to Apply
1. **Include non-cognitive skill measurement in all human capital evaluations.** Use validated instruments (Big Five, BRIEF, SDQ for children) alongside cognitive assessments.
2. **Design curricula and programmes that explicitly develop character skills.** Self-regulation, executive function, and social-emotional learning should be deliberate programme components.
3. **When estimating returns to education or training, account for non-cognitive channels.** Programmes that appear to have weak effects on test scores may have strong effects on character development that manifest in later economic outcomes.

## Examples
**Situation:** A South African NGO operates an after-school programme in the Western Cape that focuses on sport, mentorship, and structured group activities rather than academic tutoring. An evaluation finds no effect on school grades. Funders question the programme's value.
**Application:** Heckman's framework would reframe the evaluation. The programme's activities — sport requiring discipline and teamwork, mentorship modelling self-regulation and goal-setting, group activities developing sociability and conflict resolution — are precisely the inputs that develop non-cognitive skills. The absence of grade improvements does not mean the programme has no effect; it means the evaluation measured the wrong outcome. If participants are developing better self-regulation (reduced disciplinary incidents), improved perseverance (increased school attendance, reduced dropout), and stronger social skills (better peer relationships, reduced conflict), the programme is building human capital that will manifest in employment, earnings, and reduced crime in the medium term. The evaluation should measure: attendance and dropout rates, disciplinary records, validated non-cognitive assessments (such as the Devereux Student Strengths Assessment), and where possible, follow participants into the labour market. The economic returns to these character investments may substantially exceed the returns to academic tutoring.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat non-cognitive skills as unmeasurable.
**Why:** Validated psychometric instruments exist for measuring conscientiousness, self-regulation, grit, social-emotional competence, and executive function. The claim that these skills cannot be measured reflects unfamiliarity with the measurement tools, not genuine unmeasurability.

**Don't:** Assume that non-cognitive skill development is a luxury that only wealthy countries can afford.
**Why:** Non-cognitive skills are if anything more important in contexts of scarcity, where perseverance, self-regulation, and social competence are essential for navigating constrained opportunity structures. Developing these skills is not a luxury — it is foundational to poverty reduction.
