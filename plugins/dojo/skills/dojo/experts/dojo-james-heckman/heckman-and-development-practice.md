# Heckman and Development Practice

## When to Use
- When translating Heckman's econometric and human capital contributions into practical guidance for development practitioners.
- When designing programmes or evaluations that should reflect Heckman's insights about selection, heterogeneity, and skill formation.
- When advising organisations on how to move beyond simplistic evaluation and human capital approaches.

## Fails When
- **You reduce Heckman's practical relevance to a single message.** Heckman's contributions span evaluation methodology, human capital policy, inequality analysis, and labour market design. Extracting only the ECD message, or only the anti-RCT message, misrepresents the breadth of practical implications.
- **You present the implications as requiring advanced econometric capacity that practitioners cannot access.** Many of Heckman's insights can be implemented without structural estimation — heterogeneity analysis, attention to selection, lifecycle perspective, non-cognitive skill measurement.

## Core Concept
Heckman's work carries at least seven practical implications for development practitioners that go beyond his best-known contributions.

First, selection is everywhere. Every programme operates through selection — who is eligible, who enrols, who completes, who is measured. Practitioners who ignore selection at any stage will misinterpret their programme's effects. This does not require estimating a Heckman correction; it requires thinking systematically about who is in the programme and why, and how those selection processes affect the conclusions drawn from programme data.

Second, average effects are insufficient for programme design. Knowing that a programme "works on average" provides no guidance on whom to target, how to modify the design, or whether to scale. Every evaluation should report effects by subgroup at minimum, and ideally should estimate how effects vary across the distribution of participant characteristics. This is achievable with standard statistical methods and should be a routine requirement in terms of reference for evaluations.

Third, the lifecycle perspective transforms cost-benefit analysis. Most programme evaluations assess costs and benefits over a horizon of one to three years. For human capital programmes — ECD, education, training — the relevant horizon is decades. Short-horizon evaluation systematically undervalues programmes with compounding returns (early childhood) and overvalues programmes with immediate but transient effects (short-term job placements).

Fourth, non-cognitive skills deserve equal attention. Programme designs that focus exclusively on cognitive or technical skill development miss half the human capital production function. Incorporating character skill development — conscientiousness, self-regulation, perseverance, sociability — into programme design and measuring these outcomes in evaluations captures returns that cognitive-only approaches miss.

Fifth, general equilibrium effects matter for scaling decisions. A pilot programme operates in a market that it does not meaningfully affect. A scaled programme changes the market — training 100,000 workers affects wages, employment patterns, and employer behaviour. Scaling decisions should be informed by analysis of these market-level effects, not just extrapolation of pilot results.

Sixth, structural understanding enables programme improvement. Knowing that a programme increased employment by five percentage points is useful for accountability. Knowing why — which mechanism, for which participants, under what conditions — is useful for programme improvement. Evaluations should be designed to generate mechanistic understanding, not just impact estimates.

Seventh, theory should discipline empirics. Programme theories of change should be explicit, testable, and grounded in economic models of behaviour. An evaluation without a theory of change is measuring without understanding; a theory of change without empirical testing is asserting without evidence. The combination — theory-driven evaluation with structural and reduced-form components — produces the most useful evidence for policy.

## How to Apply
1. **Build selection analysis into every evaluation design.** At minimum, document who participates and who does not, and analyse how participants differ from non-participants on observable characteristics. At best, model the selection process explicitly.
2. **Require heterogeneity analysis in evaluation terms of reference.** Specify key subgroups (gender, age, education, location, baseline characteristics) and mandate that evaluators report effects for each subgroup, not just the pooled sample.
3. **Extend evaluation horizons for human capital programmes.** Budget for follow-up data collection at three, five, and ideally ten or more years post-programme. The most policy-relevant effects of human capital programmes may not be visible in the short term.

## Examples
**Situation:** A Southern African Development Community regional body is developing guidelines for evaluating youth employment programmes across member states. They ask for advice on what the guidelines should require.
**Application:** Drawing on Heckman's contributions, the guidelines should mandate seven elements. First, a theory of change grounded in a labour market model — specifying whether the programme operates through human capital accumulation, job search assistance, signalling, or demand creation, and how each mechanism produces employment effects. Second, selection analysis — documenting who participates, who drops out, and how these groups differ, with explicit attention to how employer selection of participants (in wage subsidy or placement programmes) affects the interpretation of results. Third, heterogeneity reporting — effects by gender, education level, urban/rural location, and baseline employment history, not just pooled averages. Fourth, non-cognitive outcome measurement — validated instruments for self-regulation, perseverance, and social skills alongside employment and earnings measures. Fifth, general equilibrium consideration — analysis of displacement effects (did participants take jobs from non-participants?) and wage effects (did the programme affect wage levels?), at minimum through qualitative assessment and ideally through quantitative estimation. Sixth, follow-up requirements — minimum 24-month post-programme measurement with provisions for longer follow-up for programmes with human capital components. Seventh, structural parameters — where feasible, estimation of reservation wages, search intensity, employer hiring thresholds, and skill production functions that can inform programme redesign. Not every evaluation can implement all seven elements at full rigour, but every evaluation should address each element at some level of analysis. The guidelines should be structured as a hierarchy: mandatory minimum requirements (theory of change, selection documentation, heterogeneity reporting) and recommended additional analysis (GE estimation, structural parameters, extended follow-up).

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present Heckman's practical implications as requiring PhD-level econometric capacity.
**Why:** While structural estimation requires advanced skills, many of Heckman's most important insights — attend to selection, report heterogeneity, measure non-cognitive skills, extend time horizons — can be implemented with standard programme evaluation capacity.

**Don't:** Extract only the ECD message from Heckman's work.
**Why:** The early childhood investment case is Heckman's most publicly visible contribution, but his methodological contributions (selection correction, treatment effect heterogeneity, structural versus reduced-form debate) are equally important for development practice and apply across all programme types, not just ECD.
