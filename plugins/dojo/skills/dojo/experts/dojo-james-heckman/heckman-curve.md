# The Heckman Curve

## When to Use
- When explaining the relationship between the timing of investment and the rate of return.
- When advising on budget allocation across ages and programme types.
- When challenging the conventional allocation that concentrates spending on school-age and adult populations.

## Fails When
- **You present the curve as a universal empirical fact rather than a theoretical result derived from specific evidence.** The shape of the curve depends on assumptions about the technology of skill formation, the population, and programme quality.
- **You use the curve to dismiss investment in adolescents or adults.** The curve shows declining returns, not zero returns. For individuals who missed early investment, later intervention is still valuable — just less cost-effective than early intervention would have been.

## Core Concept
The Heckman Curve is a stylised graph showing the rate of return to human capital investment as a function of the age at which the investment occurs. The curve declines from left to right: prenatal and early childhood investments yield the highest returns, primary and secondary education yields moderate returns, and remedial programmes for adults yield the lowest returns.

The curve is derived from the technology of skill formation. Dynamic complementarity — early skills raise the return to later investment — means that investing early creates a multiplier that compounds over the lifecycle. Self-productivity — skills beget skills — means that early gains are amplified by subsequent development. The combination produces a curve where earlier investment is systematically more productive.

Heckman emphasises that the curve represents returns to an optimally designed programme at each age, holding programme quality constant. The implication is not that any early programme beats any late programme — a high-quality adult training programme may yield higher returns than a poor-quality ECD programme. The curve compares the best use of investment at each age and shows that the best use is most productive earliest.

The curve also incorporates the insight that remediation is costlier than prevention. Correcting a two-year developmental deficit at age fifteen requires more intensive (and expensive) intervention than preventing the deficit at age two. The biological and developmental windows for certain skills make early development more efficient, and closing gaps after those windows requires greater effort per unit of gain.

For budget allocation, the curve implies that societies systematically overinvest in later remediation and underinvest in early prevention. South Africa is a clear example: substantial resources flow to matric support, adult basic education, and youth employment programmes while early childhood investment remains comparatively underfunded relative to its potential return.

## How to Apply
1. **Use the curve to reframe budget debates.** When arguing for ECD investment, present it not as a new spending line but as a reallocation that increases the return per rand of human capital investment.
2. **Acknowledge the curve's limitations.** It does not mean that adult investment is worthless, that all early programmes are effective, or that the curve's shape is invariant across populations.
3. **Combine the curve with local evidence.** The general shape is supported by economic theory and international evidence, but the specific returns depend on programme quality, population characteristics, and local conditions.

## Examples
**Situation:** A South African civil society coalition advocates for a National ECD Guarantee, arguing that ECD should be prioritised over the Youth Employment Service (YES) programme. Government officials respond that youth unemployment is a more immediate crisis.
**Application:** Heckman's framework would not dismiss youth employment as irrelevant but would reframe the allocation question. The Heckman Curve shows that R1 invested in quality ECD for a disadvantaged two-year-old generates a higher lifetime return than R1 invested in a 22-year-old's employment subsidy — because the two-year-old's gains compound over 60 years of life through dynamic complementarity. However, this does not help the 22-year-old who missed early investment and needs employment now. The policy response is twofold: maintain some investment in youth employment (acknowledging the lower return) while substantially increasing ECD investment (capturing the higher return). Over time, as early investment produces better-prepared youth, the need for remedial youth programmes diminishes. The current budget allocation — heavy on remediation, light on prevention — perpetuates the cycle it claims to address.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present the Heckman Curve as a simple bar chart showing that preschool investment "pays" and adult investment "doesn't."
**Why:** The curve is a marginal analysis showing that the next dollar is most productive when spent early. It does not say that existing adult programmes are worthless or that all reallocation should move to early childhood.

**Don't:** Ignore that the curve assumes quality investment at each stage.
**Why:** A low-quality ECD programme may yield lower returns than a high-quality adult programme. The curve compares optimal investments at each age, not actual programmes of varying quality.
