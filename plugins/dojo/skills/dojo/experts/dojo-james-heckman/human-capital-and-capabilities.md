# Human Capital and Capabilities

## When to Use
- When discussing what human capital means in Heckman's framework and how it differs from simpler definitions.
- When connecting economic analysis of skills to broader development frameworks.
- When advising on how to measure human capital for policy purposes.

## Fails When
- **You equate human capital with schooling.** Heckman's framework defines human capital as the stock of cognitive and non-cognitive skills that individuals bring to economic and social life. Schooling is one input; family, early childhood, health, and character development are others.
- **You ignore the measurement problem.** Heckman is clear that measuring human capital requires measuring multiple skill dimensions. Single-indicator proxies (years of schooling, IQ, test scores) are inadequate.

## Core Concept
Traditional human capital theory, following Becker and Mincer, measured human capital primarily through years of schooling and estimated its value through the wage premium associated with additional schooling. Heckman has substantially enriched this framework by arguing that human capital is multidimensional, produced across the lifecycle, and shaped by both cognitive and non-cognitive skills.

In Heckman's framework, human capital is the vector of skills that enables an individual to function productively in economic and social life. This vector includes cognitive skills (intelligence, literacy, numeracy, problem-solving), non-cognitive or character skills (conscientiousness, perseverance, self-regulation, sociability, emotional stability), and health capital (physical and mental health that enables sustained productive activity).

This multidimensional view has several implications. First, a year of schooling is not a unit of human capital — it is an input that produces human capital with varying efficiency depending on school quality, student preparation, and complementary inputs. Two children with ten years of schooling can have vastly different human capital stocks depending on the quality of their schooling and their non-school investments. Second, human capital accumulation does not begin at school entry and does not end at school exit. The lifecycle of skill formation extends from conception through old age, with different periods being more productive for different skills. Third, the return to human capital depends on the full vector of skills, not just cognitive achievement. Employers value reliability, teamwork, and self-management alongside technical competence.

Heckman has noted some alignment with Amartya Sen's capability approach — both frameworks emphasise that human welfare is about what people can do and be, not just what they earn. But Heckman's framework is grounded in production functions, rates of return, and empirical measurement, while Sen's operates at a more philosophical level. The connection is productive: Heckman provides the empirical machinery for measuring and producing capabilities that Sen's framework identifies as intrinsically valuable.

## How to Apply
1. **Measure human capital multidimensionally.** Assessments should include cognitive skills, non-cognitive skills, and health status. Relying on test scores alone underestimates human capital and misidentifies who is well-developed and who is not.
2. **When estimating returns to education, control for skill quality.** Returns to years of schooling conflate time spent in school with skills acquired. The relevant parameter is the return to skills, which requires direct skill measurement.
3. **Design programmes that develop the full vector of human capital.** Skills development programmes should address cognitive, non-cognitive, and health dimensions together, recognising the complementarities between them.

## Examples
**Situation:** A South African analysis finds that the return to an additional year of schooling is declining over time — from approximately 15% in 2000 to 10% in 2015. The conclusion drawn is that education is becoming less valuable.
**Application:** Heckman would challenge this conclusion. The declining Mincerian return to schooling may reflect declining school quality rather than declining returns to skills. If an additional year of school in 2015 produces fewer skills than an additional year in 2000 (due to curriculum changes, teacher quality declines, or expanding access to lower-quality institutions), then the return per year of schooling declines even if the return per unit of skill is constant or rising. The relevant analysis is whether the return to measured skills (literacy, numeracy, non-cognitive competencies) has declined — which requires survey data that measures skills directly. South Africa's paradox of high education spending and poor learning outcomes suggests that years of schooling is a particularly poor proxy for human capital in this context, where a child can complete nine years of schooling and remain functionally illiterate.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that increasing school enrolment automatically increases human capital.
**Why:** Enrolment without learning is not human capital formation. South Africa has near-universal primary enrolment but very low learning levels. The binding constraint is skill production within schools, not access to schools.

**Don't:** Treat cognitive and non-cognitive skills as substitutes.
**Why:** They are complements. Strong cognitive skills without self-regulation produce underachievement. Strong character without cognitive foundations limits economic returns. The full vector of human capital requires development across both dimensions.
