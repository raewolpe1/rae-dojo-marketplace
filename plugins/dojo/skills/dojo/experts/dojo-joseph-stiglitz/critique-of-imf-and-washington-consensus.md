# Critique of IMF and Washington Consensus

## When to Use

- When IMF conditionality, structural adjustment, or austerity policies are under discussion.
- When the Washington Consensus framework is being applied or evaluated.
- When the user needs the most rigorous critique of one-size-fits-all market liberalisation prescriptions.

## Fails When

- **The IMF's role in a specific crisis needs detailed historical analysis.** This file covers the general critique, not specific crisis histories.
- **The user needs a defence of the Washington Consensus.** For a balanced treatment, also consult Bhagwati and Krueger perspectives.

## Core Concept

Stiglitz's critique of the IMF and the Washington Consensus is the most influential insider attack on the post-war international financial architecture. As World Bank chief economist during the East Asian crisis (1997–1998), he observed the IMF prescribing fiscal austerity, tight monetary policy, and structural reforms to economies experiencing capital flight — a prescription he argues was catastrophically wrong.

The Washington Consensus — the term coined by John Williamson in 1989 — prescribed ten reforms: fiscal discipline, reorientation of public expenditure, tax reform, financial liberalisation, unified exchange rates, trade liberalisation, openness to FDI, privatisation, deregulation, and secure property rights. Stiglitz's critique is not that all of these are wrong, but that they were applied as a uniform package without regard to country conditions, sequencing, or institutional prerequisites.

The specific failures Stiglitz identifies include: fiscal austerity during recessions deepens the downturn (pro-cyclical policy when counter-cyclical policy is needed); rapid capital account liberalisation exposes economies to volatile short-term capital flows without the regulatory capacity to manage them; privatisation without regulatory frameworks produces private monopolies, not competitive markets; and trade liberalisation without safety nets destroys livelihoods before new ones emerge.

The East Asian crisis crystallised the critique. Countries with fiscal surpluses and high savings — Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea — were hit by capital flight. The IMF prescribed austerity, high interest rates, and structural reforms (including opening capital markets further). Stiglitz argued this was the opposite of what was needed: fiscal stimulus to offset the private sector contraction, lower interest rates to prevent corporate bankruptcy, and capital controls to halt the outflow. Malaysia, which ignored the IMF and imposed capital controls, recovered faster than the countries that followed IMF advice.

## How to Apply

1. **Assess whether the prescription matches the diagnosis.** Is austerity being prescribed for a demand-deficiency recession? Is capital account liberalisation being proposed without regulatory capacity? Is privatisation being pursued without competition frameworks?
2. **Insist on sequencing.** Even good reforms become destructive in the wrong order. Financial liberalisation before regulation. Trade liberalisation before safety nets. Privatisation before competition law.
3. **Examine the distributional consequences.** Austerity cuts fall disproportionately on the poor through reduced social spending, public sector retrenchment, and healthcare cuts. Who bears the costs?
4. **Question the conditionality.** IMF conditions often go far beyond crisis management into structural reforms that serve creditor interests rather than debtor country development.

## Examples

**Situation:** A Southern African country negotiating an IMF programme is told that fiscal consolidation, public sector wage bill reduction, and energy subsidy removal are conditions for receiving loan disbursement.
**Application:** Stiglitz's framework asks several questions. Is the fiscal deficit the cause of the problem or a symptom? If external commodity price shocks caused the revenue shortfall, austerity addresses the symptom. Public sector wage bill reduction — in a country with acute shortages of teachers, nurses, and extension officers — destroys human capital that took decades to build. Energy subsidy removal, without compensating transfers to the poorest, is regressive. The alternative: protect social spending, implement counter-cyclical fiscal policy (borrow to invest during downturns), and address structural revenue challenges through progressive tax reform rather than expenditure cuts.
**Result:** Negotiate IMF conditions that protect development spending and social safety nets while addressing genuine structural fiscal challenges.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Reject all IMF engagement as harmful.
**Why:** Some countries genuinely face fiscal crises that require external support. The critique is of the standard prescription, not of the institution's existence.

**Don't:** Assume that opposing the Washington Consensus means opposing all reform.
**Why:** Stiglitz advocates reform — but sequenced, context-sensitive, and institutionally grounded reform, not ideological prescription.
