# Beyond GDP

## When to Use

- When GDP is being used as the primary measure of development success and its limitations need articulation.
- When alternative measurement frameworks — well-being, sustainability, inclusion — are needed.
- When the user needs to understand why what we measure shapes what we do.

## Fails When

- **The user needs specific measurement methodologies.** This file covers the conceptual case for moving beyond GDP, not the technical construction of alternative indices.
- **GDP growth is genuinely the most relevant metric.** In recovery from acute crisis, GDP growth may be the most useful short-term indicator.

## Core Concept

Stiglitz co-chaired the Commission on the Measurement of Economic Performance and Social Progress (the Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi Commission, 2009) — one of the most influential efforts to articulate why GDP is an inadequate measure of societal well-being and what should replace it.

The critique has several dimensions. GDP measures production, not welfare — a country can have rising GDP while most citizens' living standards stagnate (as in the United States for decades). GDP ignores distribution — aggregate growth means nothing if all gains accrue to the top. GDP counts "bads" as goods — pollution cleanup adds to GDP, as do prisons and healthcare spending on preventable diseases. GDP ignores environmental degradation — a country can boost GDP by depleting its natural capital, leaving future generations poorer. GDP ignores unpaid work — household labour, care work, and community service are invisible. And GDP says nothing about sustainability — current GDP can be high while the foundations of future prosperity are being eroded.

The alternative framework Stiglitz and colleagues propose focuses on: material living standards (income, consumption, wealth — including distribution); health (life expectancy, morbidity, subjective health); education (quality, not just enrolment); personal activities (including work conditions and leisure); political voice and governance; social connections; environment (current conditions and sustainability); and insecurity (economic and physical).

For development practice, the implication is that programme evaluation and national development planning should use multidimensional measures, not GDP alone. South Africa's high GDP per capita (by African standards) coexists with extreme inequality, high unemployment, poor health outcomes, and environmental stress — GDP alone gives a misleading picture.

## How to Apply

1. **Use multidimensional indicators in development assessment.** GDP per capita alongside Gini coefficient, unemployment rate, life expectancy, education quality, environmental indicators.
2. **Disaggregate everything.** National averages hide distributional reality. Break indicators down by income quintile, geography, gender, race.
3. **Include sustainability.** Are current economic gains depleting natural capital, running down infrastructure, or accumulating debt? If so, current GDP overstates well-being.
4. **Measure what matters for policy.** If the goal is reducing poverty, measure poverty. If the goal is inclusion, measure inclusion. GDP is a proxy for none of these.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial development strategy is evaluated primarily on GDP growth, which has been positive, but public satisfaction with government services is declining and unemployment remains high.
**Application:** Stiglitz's beyond-GDP framework exposes the disconnect. GDP growth driven by mining and financial services can coexist with rising unemployment (capital-intensive sectors don't create mass employment), stagnant median incomes (growth benefits accrue to capital and skilled labour), deteriorating public services (if fiscal revenue doesn't keep pace or is misspent), and environmental degradation (mining without rehabilitation). The evaluation framework should include: employment (not just growth); median household income (not just GDP per capita); access to quality services (health, education, water); environmental quality; and subjective well-being. These tell a very different story from GDP alone.
**Result:** Redesign the evaluation framework to measure multidimensional well-being, not just aggregate output.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Abandon GDP entirely.
**Why:** GDP remains a useful measure of aggregate production. The point is not to discard it but to supplement it with measures that capture what GDP misses — distribution, sustainability, well-being.

**Don't:** Create indices so complex that no one uses them.
**Why:** The practical challenge is finding measures that are rigorous enough to be meaningful and simple enough to influence policy. A dashboard of 5–8 key indicators is more useful than a single composite index.
