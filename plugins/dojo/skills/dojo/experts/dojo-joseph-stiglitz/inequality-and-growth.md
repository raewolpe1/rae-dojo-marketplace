# Inequality and Growth

## When to Use

- When the relationship between inequality and economic growth is under analysis.
- When South Africa's extreme inequality (one of the world's highest Gini coefficients) is under discussion.
- When the argument that inequality is not just unfair but economically destructive needs articulation.

## Fails When

- **The user needs measurement tools for inequality.** This file covers the analytical framework, not Gini calculations.
- **The inequality is between countries rather than within them.** Stiglitz's primary focus is within-country inequality, though he addresses global inequality through the globalisation lens.

## Core Concept

Stiglitz's central argument, developed most fully in The Price of Inequality (2012) and The Great Divide (2015), is that excessive inequality is not just a moral problem — it is an economic problem. High inequality reduces growth, undermines democracy, and destabilises economies. This contradicts the conventional wisdom (associated with Okun's "big tradeoff") that redistribution reduces efficiency.

The mechanisms are multiple. First, demand: when income concentrates at the top, aggregate demand falls because the wealthy save a higher proportion of income than the poor. Consumer spending — the engine of most economies — weakens. Second, investment: inequality distorts investment away from productive capacity toward financial speculation, luxury goods, and rent-seeking. Third, human capital: inequality reduces investment in education and health for the majority, because the poor cannot afford these investments and public provision is undermined by political capture. Fourth, political economy: extreme inequality allows the wealthy to capture political processes — shaping tax policy, regulatory frameworks, and trade rules in their favour. This creates a vicious cycle: inequality begets policies that produce more inequality.

For South Africa — the world's most unequal country by most measures — Stiglitz's framework is devastatingly relevant. The Gini coefficient exceeds 0.63. The top 10% capture over 65% of national income. Unemployment exceeds 30%. And the inequality is deeply racialised, reflecting the structural legacy of apartheid. Stiglitz's analysis suggests that this inequality is not just morally unacceptable — it is the binding constraint on growth, because it suppresses domestic demand, distorts investment, undermines human capital development, and captures political processes.

## How to Apply

1. **Treat inequality as an economic variable, not just a social one.** Include distributional analysis in all economic assessments. How does the intervention affect different income groups?
2. **Identify the mechanisms.** Through which channels does inequality affect the specific economy? Demand suppression? Human capital underinvestment? Political capture? Rent-seeking?
3. **Design policies that are growth-enhancing AND equality-enhancing.** Progressive taxation, public investment in education and health, competition policy, and labour market institutions can simultaneously improve efficiency and equity.
4. **Challenge the tradeoff narrative.** When someone argues that redistribution reduces growth, demand the evidence. The cross-country evidence does not support the tradeoff in most cases.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's National Development Plan identifies inequality as a challenge but treats it primarily as a social justice issue, separate from the economic growth strategy.
**Application:** Stiglitz's framework reframes inequality as a core economic constraint. South Africa's extreme inequality suppresses domestic demand (the majority cannot afford the goods the economy produces), distorts investment (toward financial services, luxury property, and imports rather than productive capacity serving the domestic market), reduces human capital (the majority cannot access quality education and healthcare), and enables rent-seeking (concentrated economic power translates into regulatory capture). The NDP's growth strategy — which emphasises infrastructure investment and exports — cannot succeed if domestic demand remains suppressed by inequality. Inequality is not a side issue; it is the binding constraint.
**Result:** Integrate inequality reduction into the core growth strategy, not as a social policy add-on but as an economic prerequisite.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Argue that growth will automatically reduce inequality.
**Why:** Growth can coexist with rising inequality — as South Africa's post-apartheid experience demonstrates. The structure of growth matters as much as the rate.

**Don't:** Treat inequality reduction as only about transfers.
**Why:** Stiglitz emphasises pre-distribution (market structure, labour institutions, education access) as much as redistribution (taxes and transfers). Fixing the market structures that generate inequality is more sustainable than redistributing after the fact.
