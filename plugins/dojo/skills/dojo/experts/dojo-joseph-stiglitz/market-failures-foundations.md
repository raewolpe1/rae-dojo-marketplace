# Market Failures Foundations

## When to Use

- When the general theory of why markets fail needs articulation — beyond information asymmetry to the full taxonomy.
- When development interventions need justification on market failure grounds.
- When the user needs to distinguish between different types of market failure and their appropriate remedies.

## Fails When

- **The specific market failure is informational.** Use information-asymmetry-and-markets.md for that.
- **The user needs a specific policy recommendation.** This file covers the taxonomy; specific policy files address domains.

## Core Concept

Stiglitz's framework identifies several categories of market failure, each with distinct characteristics and remedies. Information asymmetries (his signature contribution) are one category. Others include: externalities (costs or benefits not reflected in market prices — pollution is the classic example), public goods (goods that are non-rivalrous and non-excludable — national defence, basic research, clean air), market power (monopoly and oligopoly that distort prices and reduce output), missing markets (markets that should exist but don't — insurance for many risks, long-term credit for the poor), and coordination failures (situations where the market outcome depends on what others do, and no one moves first).

For development economics, the critical insight is that developing countries typically suffer from multiple, interacting market failures simultaneously. Credit markets fail (information asymmetry). Infrastructure is underprovided (public goods). Environmental resources are overexploited (externalities). Markets are concentrated (market power). And coordination failures prevent investment because each potential investor waits for others to move first.

The policy implication is that market liberalisation — the standard Washington Consensus prescription — addresses the wrong problem. The problem in most developing countries is not that government is distorting otherwise well-functioning markets. The problem is that markets fail in multiple, reinforcing ways, and institutional correction is required. Liberalisation without institutional correction removes government "distortions" but leaves market failures uncorrected — often making outcomes worse.

## How to Apply

1. **Classify the market failure.** Is it information asymmetry, externality, public goods, market power, missing markets, or coordination failure? The classification determines the remedy.
2. **Assess interactions.** Market failures rarely occur in isolation. Credit market failure plus infrastructure undersupply plus coordination failure creates a development trap that no single intervention can break.
3. **Design institutional remedies matched to the failure.** Externalities require pricing or regulation. Public goods require public provision or subsidy. Information failures require institutional design. Market power requires competition policy.
4. **Evaluate interventions against the market failure, not against a perfect market.** The comparison is not "government intervention vs perfect market" but "government intervention vs failed market."

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African municipality wants to promote small enterprise development but finds that despite training programmes and business support, enterprises don't grow.
**Application:** Multiple market failures interact. Credit markets fail (banks won't lend to small enterprises due to information asymmetry and lack of collateral). Infrastructure is inadequate (unreliable electricity, poor transport links raise costs). Product markets are concentrated (large retailers and manufacturers exercise market power that squeezes small suppliers). And coordination failures prevent clusters from forming (each enterprise needs others nearby to share suppliers, skills, and customers, but no one locates first). The solution requires addressing multiple failures simultaneously: development finance for credit, public infrastructure investment, competition policy to open product markets, and deliberate cluster development to overcome coordination failure.
**Result:** Design a multi-instrument intervention package that addresses the specific combination of market failures, rather than a single-instrument approach (training alone, credit alone).

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume that identifying a market failure automatically justifies government intervention.
**Why:** Government failure is also real. The question is whether the proposed intervention improves on the failed market outcome, accounting for the government's own information and incentive problems.

**Don't:** Treat all market failures as equally important.
**Why:** In any context, some market failures are binding constraints and others are secondary. Prioritise interventions that address the binding failures.
