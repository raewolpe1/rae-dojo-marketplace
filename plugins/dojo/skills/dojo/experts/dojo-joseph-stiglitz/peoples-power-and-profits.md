# People, Power, and Profits

## When to Use

- When the relationship between concentrated economic power and democratic governance is under analysis.
- When big tech, platform monopolies, data extraction, or digital economy governance are relevant.
- When the user needs Stiglitz's most recent synthetic argument about capitalism, democracy, and reform.

## Fails When

- **The user needs detailed tech regulation analysis.** This file covers the framework, not regulatory specifics.
- **The context is purely digital without a development or governance dimension.**

## Core Concept

People, Power, and Profits (2019) represents Stiglitz's most comprehensive synthesis of his market failure, inequality, and institutional arguments. The central thesis is that the American economy — and by extension the global capitalist system — has become a rent-seeking machine in which concentrated economic power translates into political power, which in turn shapes the rules to further concentrate economic power. The result is a vicious cycle of inequality, democratic erosion, and economic inefficiency.

The specific contemporary manifestations include: tech platform monopolies (Google, Amazon, Facebook) that extract data from users without adequate compensation and use network effects to crush competition; financial sector extraction (banking, insurance, asset management capturing an increasing share of national income); pharmaceutical rent-seeking (patent gaming, lobbying against price regulation); fossil fuel subsidies and lobbying against climate action; and the erosion of labour bargaining power through anti-union legislation, gig economy classification, and globalisation threats.

The reform agenda addresses each dimension: antitrust enforcement against tech monopolies, data privacy regulation, financial sector re-regulation, patent reform, fossil fuel subsidy elimination, labour law reform, campaign finance reform, and progressive taxation. The connecting thread is that market rules are political creations, and democratic reform of those rules is the path to both economic efficiency and social justice.

For developing countries, the relevance is both direct and indirect. Directly: tech platform monopolies, pharmaceutical patents, and financial sector practices affect developing economies. Indirectly: the concentration of power in developed countries shapes the global rules — trade agreements, IP regimes, financial regulations — that constrain developing country policy space.

## How to Apply

1. **Map the power-politics-policy cycle.** Who has economic power? How do they translate it into political influence? What rules result? Who benefits?
2. **Apply competition analysis to digital markets.** Platform monopolies in developing countries — mobile money, e-commerce, ride-hailing — can extract rents just as effectively as in developed countries.
3. **Protect policy space.** Global rules shaped by concentrated corporate interests constrain developing country policy. Use available flexibilities and advocate for reform.
4. **Strengthen democratic institutions.** Transparency, freedom of information, campaign finance regulation, and independent media are essential counterweights to economic power.

## Examples

**Situation:** A Southern African country's digital economy is increasingly dominated by two or three platforms — a mobile money provider, a ride-hailing service, and a social media-integrated e-commerce platform — that extract data and transaction fees while the regulatory framework is minimal.
**Application:** Stiglitz's people-power-profits framework applies directly. These platforms exhibit network effects (users join because other users are there), data extraction (user data is harvested without adequate compensation or consent), and market power (once dominant, they can raise fees and exclude competitors). Without regulation, they will extract increasing rents from the economy. The policy response: establish data protection regulation (users own their data), enforce interoperability requirements (prevent lock-in), apply competition law to platform markets (prevent abuse of dominance), and ensure that platform workers have labour protections. The goal is not to block digital innovation but to ensure it serves broad development rather than concentrated extraction.
**Result:** Develop a regulatory framework for digital platforms that prevents rent extraction while enabling innovation.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume that technology companies are inherently developmental.
**Why:** Technology companies can be extractive or developmental depending on the institutional framework. Without regulation, network effects produce monopoly and rent extraction, not innovation and inclusion.

**Don't:** Focus on individual tech firms while ignoring the systemic pattern.
**Why:** The problem is not one bad company but a systemic failure to regulate market power in the digital economy. Platform regulation requires a framework, not firm-by-firm intervention.
