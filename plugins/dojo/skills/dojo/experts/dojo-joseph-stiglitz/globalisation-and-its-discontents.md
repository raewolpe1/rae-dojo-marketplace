# Globalisation and Its Discontents

## When to Use

- When globalisation's impact on developing countries — for good and ill — is under discussion.
- When the rules governing international trade, finance, and intellectual property are being examined.
- When the user needs Stiglitz's argument that globalisation can work for development if its rules are reformed.

## Fails When

- **The user needs a defence of free trade.** Stiglitz supports trade but criticises how trade agreements are structured. For a free trade defence, consult Bhagwati.
- **The context is anti-globalisation activism rather than policy reform.** Stiglitz is a reformer, not a de-globaliser.

## Core Concept

Stiglitz's Globalization and Its Discontents (2002) — one of the most influential economics books of the century — argues that globalisation has enormous potential to benefit developing countries but has been managed in ways that systematically favour the wealthy and powerful. The problem is not globalisation itself but the rules governing it and the institutions that enforce those rules.

The specific critiques include: trade agreements that open developing country markets to developed country exports while maintaining subsidies and protections in developed countries (particularly in agriculture); intellectual property regimes (TRIPS) that raise drug prices in the poorest countries while generating rents for pharmaceutical companies; capital account liberalisation that exposes developing economies to volatile short-term flows; and IMF conditionality that imposes austerity on debtor countries while protecting creditor interests.

The alternative Stiglitz proposes is not de-globalisation but reformed globalisation: trade agreements that account for development needs and asymmetric capacity; intellectual property regimes that balance innovation incentives with access; managed capital accounts that allow countries to control volatile flows; and international financial institutions that serve debtor countries' development needs rather than creditor countries' financial interests.

## How to Apply

1. **Examine the rules, not just the outcomes.** Who wrote the trade agreement? Whose interests does the intellectual property regime serve? Who set the conditions on the loan?
2. **Assess asymmetry.** Do the rules impose obligations on developing countries that developed countries have not accepted? Do they open developing country markets while protecting developed country sectors?
3. **Use available policy space.** TRIPS flexibilities, capital account management tools, and industrial policy space exist — countries should use them rather than accepting rules as immutable.
4. **Advocate for rule reform.** The Doha Round, SDR reform, TRIPS flexibility — there are specific institutional reforms that would make globalisation more development-friendly.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa is negotiating trade agreements that would require stronger intellectual property protection for pharmaceuticals, potentially raising drug prices in a country with the world's largest HIV-positive population.
**Application:** Stiglitz's framework is directly applicable. TRIPS-plus provisions in trade agreements extend patent protection beyond what TRIPS requires, delaying generic competition and keeping drug prices high. For South Africa — where antiretroviral access is a matter of life and death for millions — accepting stronger IP protection transfers wealth from patients and the public health system to pharmaceutical companies. The country should use every TRIPS flexibility available: compulsory licensing, parallel importation, and Bolar provisions. Trade negotiators should resist TRIPS-plus provisions and insist on public health safeguards. The broader principle: trade rules must serve development, not extract rents from the vulnerable.
**Result:** Reject IP provisions in trade agreements that would raise drug prices, and use existing TRIPS flexibilities to their fullest extent.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Blame globalisation for all development failures.
**Why:** Many development failures have domestic causes — governance, policy choices, institutional weakness. Globalisation is a constraint and an opportunity, not a cause of all ills.

**Don't:** Advocate for autarky or withdrawal from global markets.
**Why:** Stiglitz's argument is for reformed globalisation, not de-globalisation. Trade, technology transfer, and international cooperation are essential for development — but on fairer terms.
