# Climate and Inequality

## When to Use

- When climate change is being discussed through a distributional lens — who caused it, who suffers, who pays.
- When climate policy must account for equity between and within countries.
- When the argument that climate and inequality are inseparable is needed.

## Fails When

- **The user needs climate science.** This file covers the economic and distributional analysis, not the science.
- **The context is purely environmental without a development or equity dimension.**

## Core Concept

Stiglitz frames climate change as simultaneously the largest market failure in history (carbon emissions as an unpriced externality) and the deepest inequality issue of our time. The distributional dimensions are multiple: the countries that have contributed most to accumulated emissions (the industrialised North) are not the countries that suffer most from the consequences (the Global South); within countries, the poorest are most vulnerable to climate impacts and least able to adapt; and future generations bear the costs of current emissions without having a voice in current decisions.

This framing has direct policy implications. Carbon pricing — the economist's standard prescription — must be designed with distributional consequences in mind. A carbon tax that raises energy prices without compensating the poor is regressive. Cap-and-trade systems that allocate permits to existing emitters reward historical polluters. Climate finance from rich to poor countries is not charity but compensation for damage caused.

Stiglitz advocates for a combination of carbon pricing (with revenue recycled to offset regressivity), green industrial policy (public investment in renewable energy, efficiency, and adaptation), climate finance transfers (from historical emitters to vulnerable countries), and reform of global economic rules that constrain developing countries' policy space for green transformation.

## How to Apply

1. **Integrate distributional analysis into all climate policy.** Who bears the cost of the carbon tax? Who benefits from renewable energy subsidies? Who loses from fossil fuel phase-out?
2. **Design compensation mechanisms.** Carbon pricing revenues should fund social protection for those affected by the transition — displaced fossil fuel workers, communities dependent on coal mining, households facing higher energy costs.
3. **Insist on climate finance.** Rich countries' failure to meet climate finance commitments is not a budgetary issue — it is a refusal to pay for damage caused.
4. **Link climate and development planning.** Climate adaptation is development. Drought-resistant agriculture, flood resilience, renewable energy access — these serve both climate and development goals.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's Just Energy Transition is designed to shift the economy from coal dependence to renewable energy, but coal-dependent communities in Mpumalanga face economic devastation.
**Application:** Stiglitz's climate-inequality framework is directly relevant. The transition is necessary (coal is a major carbon emitter and increasingly uncompetitive), but the costs fall disproportionately on the poorest — coal miners, transport workers, communities whose entire local economy depends on mining. A just transition requires: social protection for displaced workers (unemployment insurance, retraining), economic diversification investment in affected communities (renewable energy manufacturing, agriculture, tourism), participation of affected communities in transition planning, and international climate finance to support the transition (South Africa's historical emissions are modest; the transition benefits global emissions reduction). Without these measures, the transition reproduces the inequality pattern: the benefits (cleaner air, cheaper energy) are diffuse while the costs (job losses, community decline) are concentrated.
**Result:** Design the energy transition with explicit distributional analysis and compensation mechanisms.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat climate policy as purely technical (pricing carbon) without distributional analysis.
**Why:** Climate policies that ignore distribution will be regressive, politically unsustainable, and unjust.

**Don't:** Frame climate finance as aid.
**Why:** Climate finance from historical emitters to vulnerable countries is compensation for damage caused, not generosity. The framing matters for political mobilisation and moral accountability.
