# Sequencing of Reforms

## When to Use

- When economic reform is being planned and the sequence of implementation is critical.
- When premature liberalisation — trade, financial, capital account — is being proposed.
- When the user needs to understand that the order of reforms matters as much as their content.

## Fails When

- **The reform in question is unambiguously beneficial regardless of sequence.** Transparency improvements, for example, are hard to sequence badly.
- **The user needs a detailed implementation timeline.** This file covers the analytical framework, not project management.

## Core Concept

Stiglitz's sequencing argument is one of his most practically important contributions to development policy. The insight is simple but powerful: even reforms that are desirable in the long run can be destructive if implemented in the wrong order. The Washington Consensus failed not only because some of its prescriptions were wrong, but because it applied them simultaneously and uniformly, ignoring the institutional prerequisites for each reform.

The key sequencing principles include: build regulatory capacity before liberalising (financial regulation before financial liberalisation; competition law before privatisation; labour market institutions before trade liberalisation); establish safety nets before removing protections (social protection before subsidy removal; retraining before trade adjustment); create institutions before removing government (contract enforcement before privatisation; land titling before land market liberalisation); and manage the pace of adjustment (gradual liberalisation allows learning and institutional adaptation; rapid liberalisation overwhelms institutional capacity).

The East Asian developmental states illustrate correct sequencing: they opened their economies gradually, maintaining capital controls while building domestic financial capacity, liberalising trade in stages while domestic industries built competitiveness, and investing heavily in education and infrastructure before exposing workers to global competition. The contrast with the shock therapy approach to Russia and the structural adjustment approach to Africa is stark.

## How to Apply

1. **Map the prerequisites for each reform.** Before liberalising, ask: what institutional capacity is needed? Is it in place?
2. **Sequence institutional development before liberalisation.** Regulation before deregulation. Competition before privatisation. Safety nets before adjustment.
3. **Allow time for learning and adaptation.** Gradual reform allows institutions and firms to adapt. Rapid reform overwhelms capacity.
4. **Monitor distributional consequences during transition.** If reform is producing concentrated losers, safety nets and adjustment programmes are needed — and should have been established before the reform.

## Examples

**Situation:** A Southern African country plans to liberalise its energy market — unbundling the state utility, allowing independent power producers, and eventually introducing wholesale market competition.
**Application:** Sequencing is critical. Unbundling without an independent regulator creates an unregulated monopoly (or oligopoly). Allowing IPPs without a transparent procurement framework invites corruption. Introducing wholesale competition without adequate transmission infrastructure creates bottlenecks and market manipulation opportunities. The correct sequence: establish the independent regulatory authority first (with capacity and independence), then develop the procurement framework for IPPs, then allow IPP entry, then build transmission capacity, and only then consider wholesale market competition. Each step requires the institutional foundation laid by the previous step. South Africa's own energy reform struggles illustrate the costs of getting the sequence wrong.
**Result:** Sequence energy market liberalisation to match institutional readiness at each stage.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use sequencing as an excuse for indefinite delay.
**Why:** Sequencing is about getting the order right, not about avoiding reform. Countries that delay all reform while "building institutions" may never reform. The discipline is to sequence, not to postpone.

**Don't:** Assume that sequencing can be determined in advance for all contexts.
**Why:** The correct sequence depends on existing institutional capacity, which varies by country and sector. Sequencing requires diagnosis, not prescription.
