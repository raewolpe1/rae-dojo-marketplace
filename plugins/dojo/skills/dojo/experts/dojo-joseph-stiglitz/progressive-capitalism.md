# Progressive Capitalism

## When to Use

- When an alternative to market fundamentalism is needed that is not anti-market.
- When the debate is about reforming capitalism to serve development and equity.
- When the user needs Stiglitz's positive agenda — what to build, not just what to critique.

## Fails When

- **The user wants a radical anti-capitalist framework.** Stiglitz is a reformer, not a revolutionary.
- **The context requires detailed policy design.** This file covers the conceptual framework; specific policy files address domains.

## Core Concept

Stiglitz's People, Power, and Profits (2019) and subsequent work articulate a positive alternative to both market fundamentalism and state socialism: "progressive capitalism." The framework accepts that markets are essential for economic coordination but insists that markets must be embedded in institutions that ensure they serve broad prosperity rather than concentrated wealth.

The pillars of progressive capitalism include: robust competition policy (breaking up monopolies, preventing rent-seeking), progressive taxation (taxing wealth, capital gains, and corporate profits at rates that fund public goods), public investment (in infrastructure, education, health, and research), financial regulation (preventing the financial sector from extracting rents from the real economy), labour market institutions (minimum wages, collective bargaining, worker protections), social protection (universal healthcare, unemployment insurance, pensions), environmental regulation (carbon pricing, pollution controls, biodiversity protection), and democratic governance (preventing economic power from translating into political power).

The intellectual foundation is straightforward: because markets with imperfect information are not efficient (the Greenwald-Stiglitz theorem), government intervention can improve outcomes. The practical challenge is ensuring that government interventions actually serve the public interest rather than being captured by the interests they are supposed to regulate.

## How to Apply

1. **Start with market structure.** Before adding government programmes, examine whether the market structure itself generates inequality — through monopoly, rent-seeking, or barriers to entry.
2. **Design interventions that are both efficient and equitable.** Public investment in education is both growth-enhancing and equality-enhancing. Competition policy is both efficiency-improving and rent-reducing.
3. **Build institutional safeguards against capture.** Independent regulators, transparent procurement, campaign finance reform, and freedom of information protect public institutions from private capture.
4. **Measure success broadly.** GDP is insufficient. Progressive capitalism is measured by median living standards, inclusion, environmental sustainability, and democratic vitality.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's economic policy debate is polarised between those advocating radical economic transformation (including nationalisation) and those advocating market liberalisation and reduced government intervention.
**Application:** Stiglitz's progressive capitalism offers a third path. The market fundamentalists are wrong: South Africa's markets are concentrated, information-poor, and exclusionary — liberalisation without institutional reform will deepen inequality. The nationalisers may be wrong too: state ownership without institutional capacity and accountability risks replacing private rent-seeking with public rent-seeking. Progressive capitalism asks different questions: How can competition policy break up concentrated markets? How can public investment in education, health, and infrastructure create the conditions for broad-based private sector growth? How can financial regulation redirect credit from consumption to productive investment? How can labour market institutions ensure that workers share in productivity gains? These are market-shaping interventions, not market-replacing ones.
**Result:** Frame South Africa's economic reform agenda around market-shaping institutions rather than the false binary of free markets versus nationalisation.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Present progressive capitalism as a new invention.
**Why:** The Nordic model, the post-war social democratic consensus, and elements of the East Asian developmental state all embody aspects of progressive capitalism. It is a synthesis, not a novelty.

**Don't:** Assume that progressive capitalism can be achieved without political struggle.
**Why:** Those who benefit from the current system — monopolists, rent-seekers, tax avoiders — will resist reform. Progressive capitalism requires political mobilisation, not just good policy design.
