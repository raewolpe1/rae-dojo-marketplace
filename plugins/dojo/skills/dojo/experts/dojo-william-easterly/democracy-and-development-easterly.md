# Democracy and Development (Easterly Version)

## When to Use
- When debating whether democracy supports or hinders development.
- When the developmental-authoritarian argument is being advanced.
- When evaluating democracy-support programmes.

## Fails When
- **The framework is read as deterministic.** Easterly argues for democracy on multiple grounds while acknowledging case-by-case complexity.

## Core Concept

Easterly's position on democracy and development is integrated with the broader rights-based framework. The argument:

**Democracy is more than instrumentally valuable.** Political freedom — voting, free speech, freedom of association — has value in itself, not just as a means to other ends. The "first development, then democracy" argument concedes too much to authoritarianism.

**Democracy supports the conditions for development.** Free speech enables the information flows that decentralised problem-solving requires. Free association enables the civic infrastructure that holds the state accountable. Electoral competition disciplines extractive government behaviour. The link between political freedom and the conditions for development is not accidental.

**The historical record supports the link.** Successful sustained developers — including Western Europe, North America, the East Asian transitions over time — have generally been democracies or have moved toward democracy during their development. The cases of sustained development under stable authoritarianism are limited and contested.

**The authoritarian-developmental cases.** Singapore is the strongest case for sustained authoritarian development; even there, the model has involved political-rights expansion over time, and the model has been hard to replicate. Korea and Taiwan developed under authoritarianism but transitioned to democracy during the catch-up phase. China's case is the largest contemporary test; the slowdown under recentralisation since Xi is consistent with the framework's prediction that the model has limits.

**Democracy is not sufficient.** Many nominal democracies have failed at development. Democratic transitions in highly unequal societies with weak state capacity have been fragile. Democracy in extractive political-economic contexts can produce populism rather than inclusive development. The framework recognises this; it does not predict that democracy alone produces development.

**The Acemoglu-Robinson convergence.** Easterly's view aligns substantially with Acemoglu and Robinson on the democracy-development link. The institutional-inclusive framework provides the broader theoretical structure; the empirical work supports the link.

**The democracy-support critique.** Even though democracy and development are linked, the development industry's "democracy support" programming has often been ineffective or counterproductive. Top-down democracy-promotion has the same structural problems as top-down development. The work of democratic deepening is local; outsiders can support but cannot substitute.

**The implications for development practice:**

- Treat democracy and rights as integral to development, not separate from it.
- Resist authoritarian-developmental temptations.
- Support democratic institutions while being modest about external influence on them.
- Engage anti-democratic backsliding (in any country) as a development setback, not just a political one.

**The South African case.** The 1994 democratic transition was both a political achievement and a development foundation. The post-1994 difficulties have included partial erosion of democratic capability (state capture; weakening of accountability institutions; threats to media freedom in some moments). The framework supports treating democratic restoration as development work — not separable from the economic and social work.

**The contemporary global context.** The 21st century has seen substantial democratic backsliding globally. The framework's interpretation: this is a development crisis as much as a political crisis. The retreat from democracy threatens the foundations on which inclusive development depends. South Africa's continuing democratic commitment — under substantial domestic and global pressure — is a development asset.

## How to Apply

1. **Treat democracy as integral to development**, not separable.
2. **Resist authoritarian-developmental temptations.**
3. **Support democratic institutions** with appropriate modesty about external influence.
4. **Engage democratic backsliding** as a development concern.

## Examples

**Situation:** A debate about South African Chapter 9 institutions — Public Protector, Auditor-General, Human Rights Commission, etc. — and their independence from political interference.

**Application:** The framework: the Chapter 9 institutions are part of the democratic infrastructure that supports development. Their effectiveness depends on independence from the executive; threats to that independence are development threats, not just political ones. Defending the institutions is development work.

**Result:** A policy framing that treats democratic institutional protection as developmental.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Reduce the framework to "all good things go together".
**Why:** Real tensions exist; the framework is about which approach generally produces better outcomes, not about denying complexity.

**Don't:** Use the framework to dismiss the difficulty of democratic development.
**Why:** It is difficult. The argument is that suppressing democracy makes it worse, not easier.
