# Planners vs Searchers

## When to Use
- When designing or evaluating any development intervention.
- When the question of top-down vs bottom-up approach is at issue.
- When evaluating institutional design for problem-solving.

## Fails When
- **The dichotomy is treated as absolute.** Real cases involve some of both; the question is the balance.

## Core Concept

The planners-vs-searchers distinction is Easterly's central organising framework, developed in *The White Man's Burden* (2006).

**Planners** are people (often in donor agencies, governments, large NGOs) who:
- Design comprehensive solutions to development problems.
- Roll out solutions at scale.
- Treat development as a project to be managed.
- Tend to be remote from the populations they aim to help.
- Have little personal exposure to whether their solutions work.
- Operate within bureaucratic incentive structures that reward activity and reporting.
- Tend to apply uniform solutions across diverse contexts.

**Searchers** are people who:
- Have skin in the game — personal exposure to whether their solution works.
- Try things, get feedback, adjust, iterate.
- Operate in specific local contexts they understand intimately.
- Are typically embedded in markets or communities where they're accountable.
- Have limited authority and limited reach but produce real results.

The argument:

**Searchers generate the real problem-solving that produces development.** The historical record of development, across countries and centuries, is largely a story of searchers — entrepreneurs, local leaders, traders, farmers, household decision-makers — making decisions, finding solutions, accumulating successes, and gradually transforming economies.

**Planners systematically underperform.** The top-down development plans of the 20th century — Soviet planning, Latin American import-substitution-industrialisation, African post-colonial state-led development, structural adjustment, MDG-driven planning — produced disappointing or counterproductive results far more often than not.

**The structural reason for planner failure:** Development problems are diverse, local, and context-dependent. Comprehensive plans cannot incorporate the local knowledge that solutions require. Even well-designed plans face implementation drift, capture, and the unforeseen consequences that local knowledge would have anticipated. The planner mode is structurally mismatched to the problem.

**The structural reason for searcher success:** Searchers operate close to the problem, with feedback, with stakes, with iteration. The mode matches the structural requirements of development problem-solving.

**The development-industry preference for planners.** Donors and large institutions naturally prefer plans — they can be designed, funded, reported on, and evaluated. Searchers are harder to fund and harder to scale up through bureaucratic mechanisms. The institutional preference is part of why aid often fails.

**The implication for practice.** Development practice should aim to support searchers rather than substitute for them. The work is building conditions — rights, infrastructure, accountable basic services, market access, credit — in which searchers can function. The work is not designing the searchers' decisions for them.

**The qualifications.** Some functions are inherently government-and-large-scale: vaccination programmes, infrastructure, monetary policy, criminal justice. These require planning. Even here, however, the design should integrate searcher-mode elements — local discretion, feedback, iteration.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose any development design** as more planner or more searcher in mode.
2. **Identify the searchers** in any specific context and ask how they can be supported.
3. **Resist comprehensive design** that crowds out searcher work.
4. **Build conditions for searchers** rather than substituting for them.

## Examples

**Situation:** South African informal-settlement upgrading programme.

**Application:** The Easterly lens: residents of informal settlements are already searchers — they have built their houses, organised their utilities, set up local commerce, formed governance structures. The standard upgrading programme is planner-mode — design housing units, lay out streets, install services, allocate to residents. The planner mode often produces worse outcomes than the searcher mode it replaces. Alternative: support residents' own searcher work — secure tenure, basic services to existing settlements, finance for incremental upgrading, local-government partnership. The result is incremental but durable. The instinct to replace searchers' work with planners' design typically produces worse outcomes.

**Result:** A policy approach that supports rather than substitutes for local searchers.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use the framework to oppose all state action.
**Why:** Some functions require planning; the question is the mode for the specific problem.

**Don't:** Romanticise searchers as always right.
**Why:** Searchers make mistakes too. The framework is about which mode matches the problem, not which mode is universally superior.
