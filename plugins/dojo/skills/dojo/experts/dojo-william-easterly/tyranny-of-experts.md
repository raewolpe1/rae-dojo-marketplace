# Tyranny of Experts

## When to Use
- When technocratic, expert-led development is being defended.
- When the role of rights in development is at issue.
- When debating the appropriate relationship between expertise and political agency.

## Fails When
- **The framework is used to dismiss all expertise.** Easterly's argument is about authoritarian expert governance, not about technical knowledge per se.

## Core Concept

*The Tyranny of Experts* (2013) extends Easterly's critique by connecting the planner-mentality of the development industry to a deeper political-philosophical pattern: the privileging of technocratic governance over individual rights.

**The historical argument.** Western development happened mostly through the gradual extension of political and economic rights — extension of the franchise, secure property rights, freedom of contract, rule of law, freedom of speech and association. The story of Western development is partly a story of rights expansion.

**The post-colonial development industry took a different path.** It treated newly independent countries as objects of technical management rather than subjects of political agency. The development industry's professionalisation involved economists, planners, and experts designing development from the outside, often in active collaboration with authoritarian regimes that suppressed the local political agency that Western development had depended on.

**The autocratic-developmental-state romance.** The romanticisation of authoritarian developmental states (Korea, Singapore, China) has covered the political costs of these models. The "Asian values" argument and the "developmental authoritarianism" defence rest on what Easterly calls a "tyranny of experts" — the privileging of technocratic management over the political freedoms that historically underpinned Western development.

**The continuing pattern in development practice.** The contemporary development industry's preference for "evidence-based policy", "results-based management", "data-driven decisions" — while not inherently authoritarian — combines with the planner mentality to produce a development practice that under-values political agency and over-values technical expertise.

**The rights argument.** Political and economic rights are not consequences of development; they are constitutive of it. The "first development, then rights" sequencing is empirically poorly supported. The countries that developed most successfully also typically expanded rights along the way. The countries that suppressed rights for the sake of catch-up growth (Soviet Union, China at various periods, autocratic-developmental cases) have either failed at frontier growth or remain partial.

**The aid-and-authoritarianism collaboration.** Easterly documents the historical pattern of aid going to authoritarian regimes that promised technocratic development. The collaboration has not produced the promised development; it has often perpetuated the authoritarianism.

**Implications:**

- Centre rights — political, economic, property — as foundation of development practice.
- Resist romanticisation of authoritarian developmental models.
- Treat political agency as constitutive of development, not separable from it.
- Be sceptical of technocratic claims that political processes are obstacles to development.
- Engage rights work as development work, not separate from it.

**For South Africa:** the country's transition from apartheid (an authoritarian-developmental model for the white minority, exclusionary toward the African majority) to constitutional democracy in 1994 is partly a vindication of the rights-based argument. The post-1994 difficulties are not a refutation of the rights framework but a confirmation of its difficulty under specific institutional and political-economic conditions. The temptation to "we need a developmental authoritarianism" appears periodically in South African discourse; the framework supports resistance to this temptation.

## How to Apply

1. **Centre rights** in development practice — not as add-on but as foundation.
2. **Resist romanticisation** of authoritarian developmental models.
3. **Engage political agency** as constitutive of development.
4. **Be sceptical of technocratic claims** that bypass political process.

## Examples

**Situation:** South African development debate where impatience with democratic consultation prompts calls for "executive action" on infrastructure or housing.

**Application:** The Easterly approach: the temptation to bypass political process for technocratic efficiency is the tyranny-of-experts trap. The political process is not an obstacle to development; it is part of development. The infrastructure rolled out without political legitimacy and accountability tends to produce capture, corruption, and outcomes worse than the more inclusive process would have produced. The case for the executive-action approach should be evaluated against this. The framework supports political-process engagement even at the cost of speed.

**Result:** A policy commitment to inclusive political process even under impatience.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use the framework to dismiss technical expertise.
**Why:** Expertise is valuable; the argument is against expertise overriding rights.

**Don't:** Treat the framework as anti-state.
**Why:** The state has substantial development role; the question is whether it respects rights and political agency.
