# Invisible Hand of Development

## When to Use
- When the role of decentralised vs centralised problem-solving is at issue.
- When debating the market mechanism in development.
- When evaluating bottom-up alternatives to top-down programmes.

## Fails When
- **The framework is taken as advocating laissez-faire.** Easterly's argument is about decentralised problem-solving within an institutional framework, not about absent government.

## Core Concept

Easterly draws explicitly on the Smithian tradition — the idea that decentralised actors making local decisions produce coordinated outcomes more effectively than central planning can. The argument applied to development:

**Local actors have local knowledge that central planners lack.** Each entrepreneur knows their market; each household knows its situation; each farmer knows their land. The aggregate of these local knowledges is greater than any central planner can possess. Development policy that works with local knowledge tends to outperform policy that overrides it.

**Decentralised feedback aggregates information.** Markets, in their best functioning, aggregate the information of millions of decentralised decisions. Prices, sales, profits and losses provide feedback that adjusts behaviour. The result is coordinated outcomes without central direction.

**The mechanism extends beyond pure markets.** Civil-society organisations, local government, community institutions, religious congregations, and informal networks all involve decentralised problem-solving. The "invisible hand" is broader than markets narrowly understood; it includes the social mechanisms by which decentralised actors solve coordination and collective-action problems.

**Central planning systematically loses information.** When central planners attempt to direct outcomes, they necessarily compress the diverse local knowledge into manageable categories, losing variation. The compressed picture often miscapture the underlying reality. The implementation that follows fails to match what local knowledge would have known.

**The framework's institutional requirements.** Decentralised problem-solving works under specific conditions: property rights, contract enforcement, freedom of association, freedom of speech, rule of law, accountable basic services. Without these, markets become extractive, civil society is repressed, local actors cannot operate. The state's role is to provide these conditions, not to substitute for the decentralised actors.

**The contrast with Sachs-style big-push.** Sachs argues for centrally designed comprehensive aid packages to break poverty traps. Easterly argues that the historical record on such packages is poor and that the conditions for decentralised problem-solving are what's needed instead.

**The framework's limits.** Some functions are inherently collective and centralised — infrastructure at scale, monetary policy, vaccination programmes, criminal justice, externalities management. Easterly acknowledges these. The argument is not that decentralisation solves everything; it is that the development industry systematically over-uses centralised approaches where decentralised ones would work better.

**The political-economy connection.** The framework is integrated with the rights-based argument. Decentralised problem-solving requires the rights that protect decentralised actors from arbitrary power. Where rights are denied, decentralised actors cannot operate; the invisible hand is paralysed.

**For South Africa:** the country has substantial decentralised problem-solving capacity — entrepreneurial activity, civil-society organisations, local government structures, traditional authorities, informal networks. The development question is partly how to enable this capacity rather than substitute for it. The instinct of the policy machinery to design comprehensive solutions can crowd out the decentralised work that's already underway.

## How to Apply

1. **Look for decentralised problem-solving already happening** in any context.
2. **Build conditions** (rights, infrastructure, accountable basic services) rather than substituting for the decentralised actors.
3. **Resist the planner instinct** to centralise solution design.
4. **Recognise the framework's institutional requirements** — decentralised problem-solving needs the rights and state functions that support it.

## Examples

**Situation:** South African township economic-development planning.

**Application:** The Easterly lens: substantial decentralised economic activity already exists in townships — informal trading, small-scale services, transport, retail, education and skills provision, faith-based welfare. The development question is how to support this — secure tenure for informal businesses, accessible business registration and tax compliance, electricity and water reliability, transport infrastructure that lowers costs, financial services. The instinct to design comprehensive "township revitalisation programmes" risks substituting for the searchers' work rather than supporting it.

**Result:** A development approach that supports existing decentralised activity rather than designing alternatives to it.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Read the framework as anti-state.
**Why:** The framework requires substantial state action — rights, infrastructure, basic services. The argument is about the type of state action.

**Don't:** Treat all centralisation as automatically bad.
**Why:** Some functions require centralisation. The question is fit between mechanism and problem.
