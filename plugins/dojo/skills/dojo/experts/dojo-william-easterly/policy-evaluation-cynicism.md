# Policy Evaluation Cynicism

## When to Use
- When development effectiveness claims are being made or evaluated.
- When designing M&E for a development programme.
- When the gap between programme rhetoric and outcomes is at issue.

## Fails When
- **All evaluation work is dismissed.** Easterly supports rigorous evaluation; the critique is of overstated claims.

## Core Concept

Easterly has developed a sustained sceptical position on the claims of effectiveness made within the development industry. The argument:

**Programme effectiveness is harder to establish than commonly claimed.** Even RCT-based impact evaluations face external-validity, publication-bias, and Hawthorne-effect problems. Non-RCT evaluations face larger problems. Headline claims of effect should be evaluated against the methodological work behind them, which is usually weaker than the headline suggests.

**Donor-funded evaluation is structurally biased.** Donors fund evaluations of their own programmes; evaluators have incentives (career, future funding) to find effects; publication bias toward positive findings is well-documented. The published evaluation literature systematically overstates effectiveness.

**Results-based management has perverse effects.** When effectiveness measurement is built into management, the indicators become targets and get gamed, distorted, or manipulated. Real outcomes diverge from measured outcomes. The result is plausible-looking evaluation data accompanying unimpressive real outcomes.

**The "what works" literature is shakier than it claims.** Cochrane reviews, J-PAL summaries, USAID "evidence-based" frameworks aggregate findings that individually are often methodologically weak. The aggregation does not fix the underlying methodological issues.

**Specific examples of effectiveness claims that didn't hold up.**

- The Burnside-Dollar "aid works under good policies" finding (2000) was central to aid policy in the 2000s; Easterly and co-authors showed it was statistically fragile (Easterly, Levine, Roodman 2004).
- The Millennium Villages Project's effectiveness claims were substantially overturned by independent evaluation (Bump et al. various; Mitchell et al.).
- Microfinance's transformative impact claims, repeated for decades, were substantially walked back by RCT evidence in the 2010s.
- Education-spending-equals-education-outcomes claims have faced repeated empirical setbacks.

**The appropriate stance.** Cynicism not as nihilism but as calibration. Some interventions work in some contexts; the work of careful evaluation is establishing which under what conditions. The headline claims of effectiveness should be treated with substantial scepticism; the underlying evidence should be examined directly.

**Implications for development practice:**

- Read evaluation reports critically, including methodology.
- Distinguish methodologically strong from methodologically weak claims.
- Be sceptical of strong "what works" claims.
- Use multiple sources to triangulate effectiveness claims.
- Recognise the political-economy of the evaluation industry.

**The South African dimension.** The country's M&E culture has grown substantially, including the National Treasury's M&E framework, Department of Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation (DPME) work, and various sectoral M&E systems. The development industry's evaluation work is also substantial in the country. The framework supports calibrated engagement: rigorous evaluation as goal; healthy scepticism of headline claims; attention to methodology and incentive structure.

**The relationship with Pritchett.** Easterly and Pritchett share substantial ground on RCT scepticism and on the appropriate humility about effectiveness claims. Pritchett's PDIA framework provides one constructive answer; Easterly's bottom-up framework provides another. They are complementary rather than competing.

## How to Apply

1. **Read evaluation evidence critically**, including methodology.
2. **Calibrate confidence** in effectiveness claims based on underlying evidence quality.
3. **Be alert to publication bias and donor-funding effects.**
4. **Combine multiple sources** to triangulate claims.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African policy debate where claims are made for the effectiveness of a specific anti-poverty intervention based on programme evaluations.

**Application:** The Easterly approach: examine the evaluations directly. Methodology? Sample size? Comparison group? Evaluator independence? Publication history (or is this a single grey-literature study)? Replications? In most cases, the evidence is weaker than the headline claim. Calibrate the policy confidence accordingly. Strong policy claims warrant strong evidence; weak evidence warrants modest claims.

**Result:** A policy debate grounded in calibrated rather than over-confident reading of evidence.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use the framework to dismiss all evaluation.
**Why:** Rigorous evaluation is valuable. The critique is about overstated claims.

**Don't:** Use the framework as cover for opposing programmes you dislike.
**Why:** Apply the scepticism evenhandedly across programmes you favour and oppose.
