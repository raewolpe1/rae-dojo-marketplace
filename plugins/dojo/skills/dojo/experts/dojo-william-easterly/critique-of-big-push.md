# Critique of the Big Push

## When to Use
- When Sachs's poverty-trap/big-push argument is being advanced.
- When debating whether large aid increases can break development bottlenecks.
- When framing the alternative to comprehensive aid programmes.

## Fails When
- **The critique is read as opposing all aid.** Easterly supports humanitarian aid and specific cooperation.

## Core Concept

Jeffrey Sachs's *The End of Poverty* (2005) argued that the world's poorest countries are stuck in poverty traps — interlocking conditions (low capital, low health, low education, low infrastructure) that mutually reinforce and prevent escape. The proposed remedy: a coordinated "big push" of aid, large enough to break the trap simultaneously across the relevant dimensions. The Millennium Villages Project was Sachs's flagship test of the approach.

Easterly's critique:

**The empirical evidence for stable poverty traps is weak.** Most countries that have escaped poverty did so without massive coordinated aid. Most countries that have received substantial aid have not produced the expected breakthroughs. The empirical pattern does not support the trap-and-push framework.

**Many of the same dimensions cited as trapping conditions have improved without the big push.** Health (vaccines, public-health infrastructure), education (school enrollment expansion), some infrastructure — these have improved substantially in many poor countries without coordinated aid efforts. The trap-as-mechanism is not consistent with this pattern of partial improvement.

**The Millennium Villages Project results have been disappointing.** Multiple independent evaluations have shown that MVP villages did not substantially outperform comparable non-MVP villages once spillover and control problems were properly addressed. The Pritchett-Sandefur critique was particularly sharp. The flagship test of the big-push approach did not work as advertised.

**The "trap" framing is politically convenient for donors.** It positions external actors as essential rescuers and provides justification for continued aid flows. The political-economy is convenient regardless of whether the empirics support the framework.

**The alternative diagnosis.** Poor countries are not stuck in technical traps that aid can break; they have political-economic and institutional structures that produce poor development outcomes. Aid that bypasses the political-economic structure does not address the underlying problem. The work is institutional and political, not capital-injection.

**The Sachs response.** Sachs has argued that the MVP results were misrepresented, that some specific gains were real, that the broader vision of comprehensive coordinated investment remains valid. Easterly has maintained that the comprehensive empirical case for the big-push framework is weak.

**The methodological dimension.** The big-push framework involves substantial assumptions — about which conditions are binding, about the coordination required, about the response to investment. Each assumption is methodologically contestable; the evidence for the full framework is weaker than its advocates claim.

**The implications for development practice:**

- Be sceptical of big-push proposals.
- Treat poverty as politically and institutionally produced, not as trap-and-break problem.
- Build conditions (rights, accountable institutions, openness) rather than launching comprehensive aid programmes.
- Support specific interventions where evidence is strong, in modest scope.
- Recognise that escape from poverty has happened historically without big-push aid.

**For South African context:** the big-push framework is less directly relevant since South Africa is middle-income rather than extreme-poor. But the underlying argument — that comprehensive aid interventions can solve structural problems — appears in domestic policy debates about housing, education, healthcare. The Easterly scepticism applies: comprehensive interventions face the same structural limits, regardless of whether the source is donor or domestic.

## How to Apply

1. **Be sceptical of "big push" or comprehensive intervention proposals.**
2. **Treat poverty as politically produced**, not as a technical trap.
3. **Support specific interventions** within modest scope where evidence is strong.
4. **Build conditions** rather than launching comprehensive programmes.

## Examples

**Situation:** Proposal for a comprehensive South African government anti-poverty programme integrating grants, services, skills, and infrastructure in selected districts.

**Application:** The Easterly lens: the proposal echoes the big-push framework. The historical record of such programmes is mixed at best. The dimensions cited — grants, services, skills, infrastructure — each have some evidence for specific effects in specific contexts, but the comprehensive integrated approach has not generally outperformed the sum of its parts. Better: pursue each component separately with strong evaluation; allow local adaptation; resist the integration-as-magic narrative.

**Result:** A more modest and evidence-disciplined approach to anti-poverty programming.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Conclude that no anti-poverty intervention works.
**Why:** Specific interventions have evidence; the framework supports them within scope.

**Don't:** Treat the Sachs-Easterly debate as personal.
**Why:** The debate is intellectual; both have made substantive contributions.
