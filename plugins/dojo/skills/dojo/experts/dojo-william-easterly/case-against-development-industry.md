# The Case Against the Development Industry

## When to Use
- When the institutional structure of development assistance is at issue.
- When evaluating specific development institutions.
- When designing reform of the development architecture.

## Fails When
- **All development institutions are treated as identical.** Real institutional variation exists; the critique applies selectively.

## Core Concept

Easterly's critique extends beyond specific aid programmes to the broader institutional architecture of development — what he calls (somewhat polemically) the "development industry". The argument:

**The major institutions have structural problems.**

**The World Bank.** Combines lending, policy advice, and research. The lending creates incentives to lend (the institution's revenue depends partly on loan flows); the policy advice is constrained by political-economic considerations of major shareholders; the research has often been deployed to support institutional priorities rather than independent inquiry. Reform efforts have been periodic but limited.

**The IMF.** Structural-adjustment programmes have repeatedly imposed standardised conditionality on diverse contexts. The political-economy of the IMF — board structure, voting weights, leadership selection — has historically privileged advanced-economy interests over developing-country agency. Reform has been gradual and partial.

**Bilateral aid agencies (USAID, DfID/FCDO, GIZ, etc.).** Subject to domestic political pressures that often distort programming. Accountable primarily to home-country taxpayers and politicians rather than to recipient populations. Subject to political-cycle priorities that change with administrations.

**UN agencies.** Multiple agencies with overlapping mandates; substantial coordination problems; bureaucratic structures; subject to member-state political pressures. Resource limits constrain delivery.

**Large international NGOs.** Often funded primarily by Western donors, structurally subject to donor priorities, with operations across many countries that limit deep local engagement. Some excellent specific work; the institutional structure has limits.

**The collective incentive problem.** The development industry's institutions interact with each other in ways that reproduce the dominant approach. Donors fund evaluators; evaluators publish in industry journals; journals reflect industry consensus; consensus shapes funding. The system reinforces its own existing patterns regardless of development outcomes.

**The professional class.** Development economists, evaluators, programme managers, technical specialists — substantial professional class whose careers depend on the development industry. The professional class has its own interests; the institutional pattern serves them regardless of whether it serves the development outcomes.

**The reform agenda.** The critique is not "abolish all development institutions" — some functions are necessary (humanitarian, technical cooperation, global public goods). The reform agenda includes:
- Reduce structural aid flows in favour of trade and investment.
- Restructure governance to give recipient countries genuine voice.
- Reduce conditionality on aid that remains.
- Support local institutions and civil society in recipient countries.
- Accept that much "development" work the industry does is counterproductive.

**The South African dimension.** South Africa is engaged with the development industry as recipient (legacy donor relationships), provider (African development assistance through SADC, AU, AfCFTA, BRICS), and host (substantial international NGO and UN presence). The framework supports critical engagement — supporting useful functions while pushing back against institutional patterns that reproduce paternalism.

## How to Apply

1. **Distinguish specific institutions** rather than treating "development industry" as undifferentiated.
2. **Apply structural analysis** to institutional incentive patterns.
3. **Support reform** while engaging useful functions.
4. **Resist reproducing institutional patterns** in South Africa's own development engagement.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African civil-society organisation considers accepting substantial funding from a major international donor.

**Application:** The Easterly approach: identify the structural relationship that the funding will create — donor priorities shaping CSO work; donor reporting requirements distorting CSO focus; CSO accountability shifting toward donor and away from constituency. The funding may still be worth taking, but with eyes open. Mitigation: build alternative funding sources; resist conditionality that distorts mission; protect organisational autonomy.

**Result:** A CSO decision-making process that takes the structural relationship seriously.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the critique as personal attack on individuals.
**Why:** The argument is structural. Many individuals in the development industry do excellent work despite the structural pattern.

**Don't:** Use the critique to refuse all institutional engagement.
**Why:** Some functions are necessary; useful engagement is possible.
