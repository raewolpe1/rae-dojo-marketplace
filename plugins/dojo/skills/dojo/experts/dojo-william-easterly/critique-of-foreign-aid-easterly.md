# Critique of Foreign Aid (Easterly Version)

## When to Use
- When aid effectiveness is being debated.
- When designing or evaluating aid programmes.
- When the Sachs-Easterly debate is in play.

## Fails When
- **The critique is read as opposition to all assistance.** Easterly supports humanitarian aid and specific technical cooperation.

## Core Concept

Easterly's critique of foreign aid runs through his career — from the structural-adjustment-evaluation work at the World Bank, through *The Elusive Quest for Growth*, *The White Man's Burden*, and *The Tyranny of Experts*. The argument:

**Aid has not delivered the development it promised.** Decades of aid, hundreds of billions of dollars, comprehensive planning, and yet the African development record is bleak; the conditional-aid record across countries is bleak; the "aid works under good governance" claims (Burnside-Dollar 2000) didn't replicate when Easterly re-examined the data.

**Aid changes the political relationship.** When governments are funded by donors, they are accountable to donors. The citizen-state accountability that supports inclusive institutional development is weakened. Aid props up bad governments.

**Aid produces parallel structures that weaken the state.** Donor-funded projects with their own implementation systems, recruitment of state talent into donor projects, parallel reporting systems — all weaken state capability rather than strengthening it.

**The aid industry's incentives reproduce failure.** Donors need to show impact; recipients need to absorb aid; evaluators are funded by donors; the institutional incentives align toward continued aid flows regardless of outcomes. Reform of specific programmes doesn't fix the structural incentive problem.

**The poverty-trap argument is empirically weak.** Sachs argues that poor countries are stuck in poverty traps that aid can break. The empirical evidence for stable poverty traps is limited. The countries that escaped poverty mostly did so without the big-push aid. The countries that received the big-push aid have mostly disappointed.

**The MDGs/SDGs intensify the problem.** Global targets imposed on diverse countries create reporting burdens, distort priorities, and ignore the local-knowledge requirements of real development. The architecture is a continuation of the planner mentality.

**The role of the aid industry in failure.** Pre-Easterly aid evaluation typically treated "aid effectiveness" as a question for the recipient. Easterly turned the lens on the donor — the aid industry's institutional incentives, its bureaucratic processes, its political-economy. The result is a substantially different diagnostic: the aid industry is part of the problem, not just a neutral mechanism for solving it.

**The exception of humanitarian aid.** In acute crises (famine, displacement, disease outbreak), aid can save lives that would otherwise be lost. The political-economy concerns are less acute. Easterly supports humanitarian aid robustly.

**The exception of specific technical cooperation.** Knowledge transfer, skills training, specific institutional partnership without large financial flows — these can be valuable without the structural-aid distortions.

**The alternative.** Reduce structural aid flows. Build conditions for local searchers. Support political rights and accountability. Engage in trade openness that allows poor countries to engage the global economy on equal terms. Maintain humanitarian aid as humanitarian aid.

**For South Africa:** the aid debate is now mostly historical — the country shifted from aid recipient to net contributor over the post-1994 period. But the lessons inform the country's engagement with African development assistance and the continuing presence of donor-funded development work within South Africa (sectoral programmes, technical cooperation, civil-society funding).

## How to Apply

1. **Apply political-economy analysis to any aid programme.**
2. **Distinguish humanitarian from structural aid** and treat them differently.
3. **Support local searchers** rather than substituting donor projects for them.
4. **Be sceptical of strong claims of aid effectiveness** without rigorous evidence.

## Examples

**Situation:** A donor proposes substantial sector budget support to a SADC partner government.

**Application:** The Easterly approach: the political-economy effect of the support — strengthened donor accountability, weakened citizen-state accountability — is likely larger than the project-level effect. Alternatives that work better in the long run: technical cooperation, support for civil-society accountability institutions, support for specific local searchers, trade-and-investment engagement rather than aid. Even if the budget support produces measurable short-run effects, the long-run political-economy effect is likely net negative.

**Result:** A donor decision-making process that takes the political-economy seriously.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Apply the critique to oppose all international cooperation.
**Why:** Humanitarian and technical cooperation can be valuable.

**Don't:** Treat the aid critique as right-wing politics.
**Why:** The critique is empirical and political-economy-based, drawing on heterodox-development as well as classical-liberal traditions.
