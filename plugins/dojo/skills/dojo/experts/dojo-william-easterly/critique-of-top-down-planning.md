# Critique of Top-Down Planning

## When to Use
- When comprehensive development plans are being designed or evaluated.
- When the appropriate scope of state planning is at issue.
- When evaluating national strategic frameworks.

## Fails When
- **All planning is rejected.** Some planning is necessary; the critique is about comprehensive top-down design as the dominant mode.

## Core Concept

Easterly's critique of top-down planning runs across his career. *The Elusive Quest for Growth* documented the failure of grand growth strategies. *The White Man's Burden* developed the planners-vs-searchers framing. The argument:

**The historical record of top-down development planning is poor.** Soviet planning, Latin American ISI, African post-colonial state-led development, structural adjustment, MDGs, SDGs — each generation of comprehensive top-down approach has produced disappointing results. The pattern is consistent enough to support a structural rather than accidental explanation.

**Why top-down planning fails:**

**Information problem.** Comprehensive plans require comprehensive information that planners do not have. The local knowledge that real problem-solving requires is dispersed across millions of actors. Aggregating it into planning documents necessarily loses what matters.

**Implementation drift.** Even well-designed plans face implementation drift — the people implementing differ from the people designing, contexts vary, local actors adapt or resist. The implemented outcome bears limited resemblance to the design.

**Political-economy capture.** Comprehensive plans create rent-allocation opportunities. The political process around the plan often diverts resources from the intended purpose to politically connected actors.

**Adaptive failure.** Comprehensive plans designed once cannot adapt fast enough to changing conditions. By the time the plan is implemented, the conditions it was designed for have changed.

**Crowding-out of searchers.** When comprehensive plans dominate, the space for decentralised problem-solving narrows. Searchers cannot operate when planners have allocated the resources, defined the targets, and structured the institutional environment.

**The development-industry preference.** Donors and large institutions prefer plans because they can be funded, reported on, and evaluated. The institutional preference perpetuates the approach despite its track record.

**The contemporary expressions.** National Development Plans, sector master plans, MDG/SDG localisation frameworks, results-based management systems — these are the contemporary descendants of mid-20th-century planning. The form has evolved; the structural problems persist.

**The South African case.** The country has produced multiple comprehensive plans — RDP (1994), GEAR (1996), ASGISA (2005), NDP 2030 (2012), various sector master plans. The plans have varying technical quality. The implementation pattern has been consistent: substantial gap between plan ambition and actual outcomes; substantial drift from original design; capture by political-economy interests. The pattern is the planning pattern, not specific to South Africa.

**The alternative.** Not no planning — some functions require planning. But:
- Modest scope (don't try to plan everything).
- Substantial local discretion (let implementers adapt).
- Real feedback (be able to course-correct).
- Time-limited (don't lock in long-term commitments that can't adapt).
- Searcher support (build conditions for decentralised problem-solving alongside any plans).

**For practitioners.** Be sceptical when asked to produce or implement a comprehensive plan. Push for modest scope. Build in adaptation. Maintain space for the searchers whose work is what actually delivers development.

## How to Apply

1. **Apply structural scepticism** to comprehensive planning proposals.
2. **Push for modest scope and adaptation mechanisms** in any plan you must engage.
3. **Preserve space for searchers** alongside any planning work.
4. **Be honest about implementation patterns** rather than over-promising.

## Examples

**Situation:** South African NDP 2030 implementation review.

**Application:** The Easterly lens: the NDP is an ambitious comprehensive plan. Implementation has been substantially disappointing, with substantial drift from the original design and partial achievement at best on most indicators. The standard response — better planning, stronger M&E, more comprehensive coordination — would deepen the planning approach. The Easterly response: accept the structural limits of comprehensive planning; reduce scope; build in adaptation; support the decentralised problem-solving that's actually delivering what improvement exists.

**Result:** A more honest planning practice that accepts structural limits.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Refuse all planning.
**Why:** Some planning is essential. The critique is about comprehensive top-down planning as default mode.

**Don't:** Confuse "planning" with "any thinking ahead".
**Why:** Strategic thinking, scenario analysis, and modest sectoral planning are not the targets. Comprehensive design-and-rollout is.
