# Bottom-Up vs Top-Down

## When to Use
- When evaluating community-driven development approaches.
- When debating the appropriate scale of development design.
- When framing the alternative to comprehensive top-down approaches.

## Fails When
- **Bottom-up is romanticised as always superior.** The framework is about fit between mechanism and problem.

## Core Concept

The bottom-up vs top-down framing extends the planners-vs-searchers distinction. Where the planners-searchers framing focuses on the mode of problem-solving, the bottom-up-vs-top-down framing focuses on the level of design and decision-making.

**Top-down characteristics:**
- Design at high level (national, donor, expert).
- Authority flows from centre to periphery.
- Uniform implementation across diverse contexts.
- Reporting flows back to the centre.
- Accountability is toward the designing authority.

**Bottom-up characteristics:**
- Design at low level (community, household, local organisation).
- Authority is local or distributed.
- Variation across contexts is expected.
- Reporting is internal to local participants or absent.
- Accountability is among local participants.

**The structural advantages of bottom-up:**

**Local knowledge.** People in specific contexts know their context. Aggregating their decisions captures more knowledge than any central planner can possess.

**Adaptive learning.** Local actors can adjust to changing conditions and unexpected results in ways that central planners cannot.

**Skin in the game.** Local actors bear the consequences of their decisions. They have incentives to get it right.

**Accountability.** Local accountability mechanisms tend to be more reliable than long-distance accountability through bureaucratic chains.

**The structural disadvantages of bottom-up:**

**Coordination.** Some problems require coordination across actors that bottom-up approaches struggle to achieve — large infrastructure, externalities management, public-good provision at scale.

**Scale.** Some interventions are inherently large-scale (vaccination programmes, monetary policy, criminal justice) and don't have meaningful bottom-up versions.

**Local elite capture.** Bottom-up approaches can be captured by local elites just as top-down ones can be captured by national elites. Local doesn't automatically mean inclusive.

**Knowledge gaps.** Some problems require knowledge that local actors don't have (epidemiological, technical, global-scale environmental).

**The framework's principle:** match the mechanism to the problem. Most development problems are better solved by bottom-up than the development industry typically employs; some are better solved by top-down than the bottom-up advocates acknowledge. The error is using top-down by default.

**Examples of well-fitted bottom-up:**
- Smallholder agricultural practice (each farmer's land is local-specific).
- Small-business growth (each market is local-specific).
- Community-based healthcare for non-epidemic conditions.
- Local urban planning (within national frameworks).
- Skills development (matched to local labour markets).

**Examples of well-fitted top-down:**
- Vaccination campaigns.
- Macroeconomic policy.
- Trade policy.
- Criminal justice.
- Major infrastructure planning.

**The integration.** Real development typically involves layered work — top-down provides frameworks and large-scale conditions; bottom-up does the specific problem-solving within those frameworks. The development-industry mistake is to do too much top-down and crowd out the bottom-up work that should accompany it.

**For South African context.** The country's policy machinery has substantial top-down preference. National plans, sector master plans, comprehensive frameworks. The bottom-up work — community organisations, local government innovation, SME ecosystem development — is often under-resourced and under-emphasised. Rebalancing toward bottom-up where appropriate would likely improve development outcomes.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose the appropriate level of design** for each specific problem.
2. **Default to bottom-up** unless the problem clearly requires top-down.
3. **Resist comprehensive top-down design** that crowds out bottom-up work.
4. **Build top-down frameworks that support bottom-up work**, rather than substituting for it.

## Examples

**Situation:** South African basic-education reform.

**Application:** The framework: classroom and school-level quality requires substantial local discretion — teacher adaptation, school-level innovation, parent-community engagement. The national-level top-down framework can support this with curriculum standards, resource provision, teacher development, accountability mechanisms. But the work of educational quality happens at the school level. Top-down approaches that micro-manage classrooms (highly prescriptive curriculum delivery, standardised lesson plans, restrictive testing regimes) tend to crowd out the school-level work that actually improves outcomes. The reform should set frameworks and build conditions, then trust schools and teachers to do the work.

**Result:** An education-reform approach that uses appropriate levels of design.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Romanticise bottom-up as always superior.
**Why:** Some problems require top-down. The framework is about fit.

**Don't:** Treat bottom-up as automatically inclusive.
**Why:** Local elites can capture bottom-up processes just as national elites can capture top-down ones.
