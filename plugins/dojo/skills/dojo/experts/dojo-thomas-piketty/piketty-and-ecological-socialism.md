# Piketty and Ecological Socialism

## When to Use
- When the inequality crisis and the ecological crisis need to be connected — arguing that addressing one without the other is impossible.
- When *Time for Socialism* (2021) and *Equality Is a Struggle* (2025) provide the framework for ecological socialism.
- When fiscal instruments for the ecological transition need to address distributional justice simultaneously.

## Core Concept
In *Time for Socialism* and *Equality Is a Struggle*, Piketty argues that the inequality crisis and the ecological crisis are inseparable. The wealthy produce disproportionate emissions (through consumption, travel, and investment in carbon-intensive industries), while the poor suffer disproportionately from climate change (through exposure to extreme weather, food insecurity, and displacement). Addressing climate change without addressing inequality is both unjust and politically unfeasible — because ecological transition policies that burden the poor while leaving the wealthy's consumption unconstrained will be politically rejected (as the French gilets jaunes demonstrated).

Piketty's ecological socialism combines progressive taxation (carbon taxes that are progressive — falling more heavily on high emitters — rather than flat), wealth redistribution (using carbon tax revenue to fund transfers to low-income households and investment in clean energy), and democratic governance of the transition (ensuring that affected workers and communities participate in designing the transition rather than being subjected to it).

The global dimension is critical: the wealthy countries that produced most historical emissions have obligations to the poor countries that are suffering the consequences. Climate finance, technology transfer, and trade terms that account for carbon content are institutional expressions of this obligation.

## How to Apply
1. **Connect inequality and climate.** "Who produces the emissions? Who suffers the consequences? The distributional analysis connects inequality to climate — and demonstrates that addressing one without the other is impossible."
2. **Design progressive carbon pricing.** "Carbon taxes should be progressive — falling more heavily on high emitters (the wealthy) than on low emitters (the poor). Revenue should fund transfers to low-income households and clean energy investment."
3. **Democratise the transition.** "Workers in carbon-intensive industries and communities dependent on fossil fuel economies must participate in designing the transition — not be subjected to it."
4. **Apply the global obligation.** "Wealthy countries have obligations to poor countries on climate: historical responsibility for emissions, technology transfer, climate finance, and trade terms that reflect carbon content."

## Examples
**Situation:** A government proposes a flat carbon tax to fund the ecological transition. Critics argue it will disproportionately burden the poor, who spend a higher proportion of their income on energy and transport.
**Application:** "The critics are right. A flat carbon tax is regressive — it takes a larger share of the poor's income than the rich's. The wealthy person who flies frequently and drives a large vehicle pays more in absolute terms but less as a share of income. The poor person who heats their home and takes public transport pays less in absolute terms but more as a share of income. The result: the poor fund the ecological transition while the wealthy's lifestyle is barely affected. Piketty's alternative: a progressive carbon tax, with rates that increase with the individual's total carbon footprint, combined with a carbon dividend — redistribution of carbon tax revenue to low-income households. The result: the wealthy pay more (because their carbon footprint is larger), the poor receive a net transfer (because the dividend exceeds their carbon tax payment), and the ecological transition is funded progressively. Additionally: a wealth tax on carbon-intensive assets (fossil fuel investments, large property, aviation) directly targets the wealth that produces the highest emissions."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Separate inequality from climate.
**Why:** The two crises are produced by the same structures — concentrated wealth, unconstrained capital, and political systems captured by the wealthy. Addressing them separately is analytically wrong and politically futile.

**Don't:** Propose ecological transition without distributive justice.
**Why:** An ecological transition that burdens the poor will be politically rejected — and rightly so. Justice requires that the transition's costs fall on those who produced the crisis, not on those who suffer from it.
