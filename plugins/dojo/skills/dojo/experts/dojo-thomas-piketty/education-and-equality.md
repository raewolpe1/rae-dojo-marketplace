# Education and Equality

## When to Use
- When education is being promoted as the solution to inequality without examining whether the education system itself reproduces the inequality it is supposed to address.
- When the long-run relationship between educational investment and income distribution needs to be articulated.
- When educational inequality — differential quality by parental income — needs to be diagnosed and addressed.

## Core Concept
Piketty argues that education is the central long-run equaliser — the investment that determines whether economic growth benefits the many or the few. When educational quality is genuinely equal across income levels, the next generation's income distribution is more equal than the current generation's. When educational quality varies with parental income — better schools for the rich, worse schools for the poor — the education system reproduces inequality across generations.

The historical evidence is clear. The expansion of mass education in the twentieth century — public schools, universal access, publicly funded universities — was a primary driver of the reduction in inequality between 1910 and 1980. The subsequent underfunding of public education and the expansion of private educational alternatives since 1980 has contributed to the return of inequality. The correlation is not coincidental: education determines the distribution of human capital, which determines the distribution of labour income, which determines the overall distribution of income (alongside the capital share).

The implication for policy: the single most important long-run investment for equality is educational equalisation — ensuring that per-pupil expenditure, teacher quality, and educational infrastructure are genuinely equal across income levels. This requires not merely access (everyone can attend school) but quality equalisation (the school in the poorest district is as good as the school in the wealthiest).

## How to Apply
1. **Audit educational quality by income.** "What is the per-pupil expenditure in the poorest districts versus the wealthiest? What is the teacher quality differential? What are the learning outcomes by parental income? If these vary significantly, the education system is reproducing inequality."
2. **Demand quality equalisation.** "Access to education is necessary but insufficient. The question is quality: is the public education system providing the same quality of instruction, facilities, and resources to children from poor families as to children from wealthy families?"
3. **Connect to long-run distribution.** "Today's educational inequality is tomorrow's income inequality. Investment in educational equalisation now produces distributional gains for the next generation."
4. **Invest at the bottom.** "The highest return on educational investment — in both equality and growth terms — comes from investing at the bottom: early childhood education, primary schooling, and technical education for disadvantaged populations."

## Examples
**Situation:** A country has achieved universal primary education — 98% enrolment. But learning assessments show vast differences in learning outcomes by parental income: children from the top income quintile score three to four grade levels above children from the bottom quintile. The government reports the 98% enrolment as an education success.
**Application:** "Universal enrolment is an access achievement, not an equality achievement. The three-to-four grade level gap by income reveals that the education system is reproducing inequality rather than equalising it. Children from wealthy families attend better-resourced schools, with better-trained teachers, in better facilities — and they learn more. Children from poor families attend under-resourced schools with undertrained teachers — and they learn less. The enrolment is equal; the education is not. Over the next generation, the learning gap will translate into an income gap: children who learned more will earn more. The education system is functioning as a reproduction mechanism — laundering family income inequality into educational inequality into labour market inequality. The remedy: equalise per-pupil expenditure across districts (the poorest districts should receive more, not less, than the wealthiest); deploy the best teachers to the most disadvantaged schools (through compensation incentives and career pathways); and invest in early childhood education for the bottom quintile (where the learning gap originates)."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat education as a silver bullet.
**Why:** Educational equalisation is necessary but insufficient. Without fiscal policy to constrain capital concentration (r > g operates regardless of education), educational equality reduces only the labour-income dimension of inequality.

**Don't:** Present access as equality.
**Why:** Universal enrolment with unequal quality is not educational equality. The equality must be in quality — outcomes, not just access.
