# r > g

## When to Use
- When inequality is deepening and the mechanism — capital returns exceeding economic growth — needs to be identified and explained.
- When the claim that "growth will solve inequality" needs to be challenged with the r > g dynamic.
- When designing policy to counteract the automatic concentration of wealth.

## Core Concept
Piketty's central finding, the structuring insight of *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2014) and revisited in *Capital in the 21st Century, Ten Years Later* (2025), is that when the rate of return on capital (r) exceeds the rate of economic growth (g), wealth concentrates automatically. The rich get richer not because they are more productive, more talented, or more deserving — but because their capital generates returns faster than the economy as a whole is growing.

The mechanism is mathematical. If capital earns 5% per year (r = 5%) and the economy grows at 2% per year (g = 2%), then capital's share of national income rises over time. The capital-holder's wealth grows at 5% while the average person's income grows at 2%. Over decades, the gap compounds — producing the extreme wealth concentration visible in the data.

This is not a moral argument — it is an arithmetic observation. r > g describes what happens when capital is not taxed, when inheritance is not constrained, and when political arrangements permit capital to compound unchecked. The remedy is equally straightforward in principle (though politically difficult in practice): progressive taxation that reduces the after-tax r below g, ensuring that capital does not concentrate faster than the economy grows.

The historical record confirms the dynamic. In the nineteenth century and the Belle Époque (1870–1914), r > g prevailed and wealth concentration was extreme. The period 1914–1980 saw r reduced below g through the destruction of capital in two world wars, progressive taxation, and the expansion of the welfare state — producing the most egalitarian period in modern history. Since 1980, the reduction of top tax rates and the deregulation of capital markets have restored r > g — and wealth concentration has returned to pre-World War I levels.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose the dynamic.** "What is the approximate rate of return on capital in this context? What is the rate of economic growth? If r > g, wealth is concentrating. This is the first-order diagnostic."
2. **Identify the policy failure.** "If r > g is operating, the fiscal system is failing to constrain capital concentration. Effective tax rates on capital income and wealth are too low. The remedy is fiscal: increase the effective tax on capital returns so that after-tax r ≤ g."
3. **Challenge the growth narrative.** "When growth is proposed as the solution to inequality, ask: who captures the growth? If r > g, growth disproportionately benefits capital-holders. Growth without progressive fiscal policy deepens inequality."
4. **Design for after-tax r ≤ g.** "The policy target is clear: the after-tax rate of return on capital should not exceed the rate of economic growth. This requires: wealth taxes, progressive income taxes on capital income, and inheritance taxes that prevent dynastic accumulation."

## Examples
**Situation:** A country has experienced strong economic growth (5% GDP growth) over a decade, but income inequality has worsened. The government is puzzled — growth was supposed to reduce inequality.
**Application:** "Growth has deepened inequality because r > g. The economy grew at 5%, but the return on capital — including real estate, financial assets, and business ownership — averaged 8–10%. The top 10%, who own most of the capital, saw their wealth grow at 8–10% while average incomes grew at 5%. The bottom 50%, who own essentially no capital, benefited only from labour income growth — which was below 5% because the capital share of national income was rising. The growth produced a larger pie, but the capital-owning class took a growing share of each slice. The remedy: progressive taxation that captures part of the capital returns and redistributes them through public investment — in education, health, and social protection — that benefits the bottom 50%. Without this fiscal redistribution, continued growth will continue to deepen inequality. Growth is not the solution. Growth plus progressive redistribution is the solution."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present r > g as an iron law.
**Why:** r > g is a tendency, not a necessity. Policy can reduce r (through taxation) and increase g (through investment in education and infrastructure). The dynamic is real but counteractable.

**Don't:** Reduce the analysis to a formula.
**Why:** r > g describes a mathematical relationship, but its effects are lived by real people — the family whose rent rises faster than their wages, the community whose land values enrich distant owners, the country whose resources compound in metropolitan bank accounts.
