# The Capital Share

## When to Use
- When the functional distribution of income — the split between capital income (profits, rents, interest, dividends) and labour income (wages, salaries) — is shifting in ways that deepen inequality.
- When the rising capital-income ratio (the ratio of total capital to national income) needs to be connected to distributional outcomes.
- When the claim that "growth benefits everyone" needs to be tested against the capital share data.

## Core Concept
Piketty's analysis of the capital share — the proportion of national income that accrues to capital rather than labour — is foundational. Capital income is distributed far more unequally than labour income: the top 10% own most of the capital, so a rising capital share automatically increases inequality even if total income grows. When the capital share rises from 25% to 35% of national income, the additional 10% flows overwhelmingly to those who own capital — the top 10% and especially the top 1%.

The capital-income ratio (β) — the ratio of total capital stock to annual national income — has returned in many countries to nineteenth-century levels: 600–700% of national income. This means that inherited wealth and accumulated capital dominate the economy, and capital income represents a growing share of total income. The implications for inequality are stark: in a world where capital earns 4–5% and labour income grows at 1–2%, the capital-owning class pulls away from the rest.

## How to Apply
1. **Track the capital share.** "What proportion of national income accrues to capital? Is it rising? A rising capital share indicates that economic growth is disproportionately benefiting capital owners."
2. **Disaggregate by ownership.** "Who owns the capital? If the top 10% own 60–80% of capital, a rising capital share means that 60–80% of the additional income flows to the top 10%."
3. **Connect to development.** "When a development programme promotes economic growth without addressing the capital share, ask: who will capture the growth? If the capital share is rising, the growth will disproportionately benefit those who already own capital."
4. **Design fiscal responses.** "A rising capital share requires fiscal intervention: taxes on capital income, wealth taxes, and inheritance taxes that prevent capital concentration from translating into permanent income inequality."

## Examples
**Situation:** A country reports strong GDP growth and declining absolute poverty. But the Gini coefficient is rising. The government is puzzled — growth is reducing poverty but increasing inequality.
**Application:** "The puzzle dissolves when you examine the capital share. GDP is growing, but the capital share of national income is rising — from 28% to 36% over a decade. The growth is real, and it reduces absolute poverty because total income is larger. But the growth's distribution is skewed: 36% of every additional unit of income goes to capital owners (the top 10–20%), while 64% goes to labour (the broader population). Since capital ownership is concentrated, the top decile captures a disproportionate share of growth. The bottom 50% benefit from growth — their incomes rise — but they benefit less than the top 10%, so relative inequality widens. The solution: fiscal policy that captures part of the rising capital share and redirects it to the bottom 50% through public services, social protection, and educational investment. Without this fiscal redistribution, continued growth will continue to reduce absolute poverty while deepening relative inequality."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the capital share as fixed.
**Why:** The capital share varies across time and across countries. It is shaped by policy — taxation, regulation, labour protections, institutional design. It can be changed.

**Don't:** Disconnect from distribution.
**Why:** A rising capital share is technically interesting but morally significant only when connected to the distribution of capital ownership. The distributional implication — that a rising capital share enriches the already-wealthy — is the point.
