# Piketty and Development

## When to Use
- When development strategy needs to address inequality as a structural driver — not merely as a symptom to be managed through social protection.
- When applying Piketty's framework to the specific conditions of developing countries: colonial legacies, extractive economies, weak fiscal systems, and dual economies.
- When designing development programmes that build fiscal capacity, educational equality, and redistributive institutions.

## Core Concept
Piketty's framework applies to developing countries with particular force — and with specific complications. The r > g dynamic operates in developing countries often more intensely than in developed ones: capital returns in resource-rich economies can be extremely high while economic growth for the majority remains low. The inequality regime analysis reveals that many developing countries operate hybrid regimes: post-colonial proprietarianism layered over colonial extraction structures, with hyper-capitalist elements imported through structural adjustment and globalisation.

The specific conditions of developing countries add dimensions. Weak fiscal systems: many developing countries collect less than 15% of GDP in tax revenue, compared to 30–45% in developed countries. This fiscal gap prevents the redistributive taxation that Piketty prescribes. Colonial legacies: property distributions established during colonialism persist and compound through r > g. Dual economies: a modern formal sector (integrated into global capital markets) coexists with a large informal sector (excluded from formal fiscal and financial systems). Resource dependence: natural resource revenues create opportunities for redistribution but also for elite capture.

The development application: build the fiscal capacity to implement progressive taxation; invest in educational equalisation as the long-run mechanism; trace and address colonial capital legacies; and design resource revenue management that prevents concentration.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose the inequality regime.** "What type of inequality regime characterises this country? What are the colonial legacies? What ideologies sustain the current distribution?"
2. **Build fiscal capacity.** "If the tax-to-GDP ratio is below 15%, building the fiscal system is the first priority. Without fiscal capacity, progressive taxation is impossible."
3. **Design for the dual economy.** "Fiscal policy must reach beyond the formal sector. Property taxes (including land), consumption taxes (progressive, not flat), and resource revenue taxation can capture income that income taxes miss."
4. **Invest in educational equalisation.** "The highest-return investment for long-run equality is educational quality equalisation. This is the structural intervention that changes the next generation's income distribution."

## Examples
**Situation:** A resource-rich developing country has high GDP growth driven by mining, but inequality is extreme. The government proposes a development strategy focused on "economic diversification" to reduce resource dependence.
**Application:** "Economic diversification is necessary but insufficient without fiscal redistribution. The mining sector generates high returns to capital (r) — but these returns accrue to the mining companies (largely foreign-owned) and the domestic elite. The majority of the population works in agriculture and the informal sector, where incomes grow at the general rate (g). r >> g in this economy: the mining sector enriches its owners while the rest of the economy grows slowly. Diversification would reduce dependence on mining — but unless the diversified economy's growth is distributed more equally, it will reproduce the inequality in different sectors. The development strategy needs three additions. First, fiscal: a mining fiscal regime that captures 50–70% of resource revenues through royalties, profit taxes, and windfall levies. Second, redistribution: a sovereign wealth fund that converts resource revenues into permanent public assets — invested in education, health, and infrastructure. Third, equalisation: per-pupil education expenditure equalised across regions, with the resource-producing regions' revenues funding national educational investment. Without these fiscal mechanisms, diversification produces a diversified economy with the same inequality — which is not development."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Apply the framework mechanically.
**Why:** Developing countries face specific conditions — weak fiscal systems, colonial legacies, dual economies — that require adapted analysis, not mechanical application of the developed-country framework.

**Don't:** Present fiscal reform as the only intervention needed.
**Why:** Fiscal policy is essential but must be accompanied by institutional reform, educational investment, and political change. The fiscal system operates within a political economy that must also be addressed.
