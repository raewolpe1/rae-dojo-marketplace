# Inequality Regimes

## When to Use
- When classifying the political-economic arrangement of a specific country or period — what type of inequality regime is it?
- When demonstrating that different regimes have produced vastly different levels of inequality, proving that alternatives are possible.
- When *Capital and Ideology* (2020) provides the analytical framework.

## Core Concept
In *Capital and Ideology* (2020), Piketty argues that every society is an "inequality regime" — a set of institutional arrangements that produce a specific distribution of income and wealth, sustained by an ideology that justifies that distribution. The ideology is essential: without it, the inequality would be experienced as arbitrary and would be politically unstable. The ideology makes the inequality appear natural, just, or necessary.

Piketty identifies several major inequality regimes across history. Trifunctional societies: pre-modern societies organised into three orders — clergy (who pray), nobility (who fight), and commoners (who work) — each with a specific role and a corresponding justification. Proprietarian societies: post-revolutionary societies built on the sanctity of private property — the regime that dominated the nineteenth century, with property rights as the ideological foundation. Social-democratic societies: the post-World War II arrangements that combined private ownership with progressive taxation, social insurance, and public services — producing the most egalitarian period in modern history. Hyper-capitalist societies: the post-1980 arrangements that reduced taxation, deregulated capital markets, and weakened labour protections — producing the return to pre-war inequality levels. Communist societies: the Soviet and Maoist experiments that abolished private property but concentrated political power — producing equality of deprivation.

The key insight is that these regimes are not natural stages of development. They are political choices — and different choices produce different outcomes. The social-democratic regime demonstrated that high equality and high growth are compatible. The hyper-capitalist regime's claim that inequality is the price of growth is empirically false.

## How to Apply
1. **Classify the regime.** "What type of inequality regime characterises this country or context? Proprietarian? Social-democratic? Hyper-capitalist? Hybrid? The classification reveals the ideological foundations and suggests the pressure points."
2. **Identify the ideology.** "What ideology sustains this regime? Meritocracy? Property rights? Free-market fundamentalism? The ideology is the regime's vulnerability — once named, it can be challenged."
3. **Compare historically.** "What level of inequality did other regimes produce? The social-democratic period (1950–1980) achieved low inequality with high growth. This proves that the current level of inequality is not necessary."
4. **Propose regime change.** "If the current regime produces unjustifiable inequality, propose the institutional changes that would move toward a more egalitarian regime. Be specific: what taxes, what regulations, what institutional reforms."

## Examples
**Situation:** A country in Southern Africa has high and rising inequality. The government promotes a narrative of "inclusive growth" while maintaining low effective tax rates on capital, minimal wealth taxation, and a dual economy with a modern formal sector and a large informal sector.
**Application:** "This is a hybrid inequality regime: post-colonial proprietarianism layered over the colonial extraction economy. The proprietarian element: property rights established during the colonial period remain largely intact — land, mineral rights, and financial capital are concentrated in the hands of those who benefited from the colonial distribution. The hyper-capitalist element: low effective taxation on capital, deregulation, and the integration of the formal economy into global capital markets. The colonial residue: a dual economy in which the formal sector operates on metropolitan terms while the informal sector absorbs the population that the formal sector excludes. The 'inclusive growth' narrative is the regime's ideology — it acknowledges inequality while proposing growth as the remedy, avoiding the structural question of who owns the capital that growth will enrich. The historical comparison is devastating: the social-democratic regimes of 1950–1980 achieved lower inequality with comparable or higher growth rates — through progressive taxation, public investment in education, and labour market protections. These instruments are available to this country. They are politically difficult — because the ideology of the current regime makes them appear unnecessary or dangerous. The task is to challenge the ideology with data."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the typology as rigid.
**Why:** Real-world regimes are complex hybrids. Most countries combine elements of multiple regime types. The typology is an analytical tool, not a rigid classification system.

**Don't:** Present any single regime as inevitable.
**Why:** Piketty's central message is that inequality regimes are political choices. None is natural or inevitable. The current regime can be changed through political action.
