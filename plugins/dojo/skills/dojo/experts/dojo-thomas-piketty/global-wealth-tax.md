# Global Wealth Tax

## When to Use
- When capital concentration needs to be constrained directly — through taxation of the wealth stock, not merely the income flow.
- When capital flight and tax competition between jurisdictions undermine national progressive taxation.
- When fiscal policy design needs a specific, technically detailed proposal.

## Core Concept
Piketty's proposal for a global progressive wealth tax is his most specific and most controversial policy recommendation. The logic is direct: if r > g drives wealth concentration, then taxing wealth reduces after-tax r, directly constraining concentration. An annual tax of 1–2% on large fortunes reduces the rate at which wealth compounds — slowing the r > g dynamic without eliminating the incentive to invest.

The global dimension addresses the capital flight problem. If one country imposes a wealth tax, capital moves to jurisdictions without one. A coordinated global tax — or at minimum a regional tax (EU-wide, African Union-wide) — prevents this flight. The administrative requirement is a global wealth registry: a database of who owns what, where. This registry is technically feasible — most financial assets are already tracked — and would also serve anti-corruption, anti-money-laundering, and tax transparency purposes.

Piketty's proposed rates are modest: 0% on wealth below a high threshold, 1% on moderate fortunes, 2% on large fortunes, rising to 5–10% on extreme wealth (billionaires). The revenue would fund universal capital endowments, education, and climate transition. The rates are designed to constrain concentration, not to eliminate wealth — the after-tax return would remain positive, preserving the incentive to invest.

## How to Apply
1. **Propose the mechanism.** "A progressive annual wealth tax directly constrains the r > g dynamic. The tax reduces after-tax r, slowing the rate at which wealth compounds."
2. **Address capital flight.** "Capital flight is the standard objection. Address it with: regional tax coordination, automatic exchange of financial information, and a wealth registry that makes hidden wealth visible."
3. **Be specific.** "Propose specific rates: 0% below $1 million, 1% on $1–10 million, 2% on $10–100 million, 5% above $100 million. Revenue allocation: 50% to universal capital endowment, 30% to education equalisation, 20% to climate transition."
4. **Present as historically normal.** "Wealth taxation is not radical — property taxes exist in most countries and are a form of wealth tax. The proposal is to extend the principle from real estate to all forms of wealth, and to make it progressive."

## Examples
**Situation:** A country has extreme wealth concentration — the top 1% owns 50% of national wealth — and the government wants to fund expanded social protection. The finance ministry proposes increasing the VAT, which is regressive.
**Application:** "The VAT increase will fund social protection by taxing consumption — which falls disproportionately on the poor. The wealthy spend a smaller proportion of their income on consumption, so the VAT's effective rate on the wealthy is lower than on the poor. This is regressive: the poor fund their own social protection. The alternative: a progressive wealth tax. If the top 1% owns 50% of national wealth, a 1% annual tax on wealth above a high threshold would generate significant revenue — far more than the VAT increase — while being borne by those who can most afford it. The revenue funds the same social protection but through a progressive rather than regressive mechanism. The objection will be capital flight. The response: automatic exchange of financial information (which exists through CRS), a national wealth registry (which can be built on existing asset data), and regional coordination with neighbouring countries to prevent jurisdictional arbitrage."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present a global wealth tax as immediately achievable.
**Why:** Global coordination is a long-term project. But national and regional wealth taxes are feasible now — and the advocacy for global coordination should continue.

**Don't:** Dismiss administrative challenges.
**Why:** Wealth taxation faces genuine administrative challenges — valuation of non-liquid assets, enforcement, compliance. These challenges are real but not insuperable — and they should not be used as excuses to avoid the policy.
