# Piketty and Colonialism

## When to Use
- When the colonial roots of contemporary global inequality need to be traced — how capital extracted through colonialism continues to compound in the metropole.
- When colonial debt, reparations, or the ongoing economic effects of colonialism are under discussion.
- When *Capital and Ideology* and *Nature, Culture, and Inequality* (2024) provide the framework.

## Core Concept
Piketty demonstrates in *Capital and Ideology* that colonial extraction was a massive transfer of capital from the periphery to the metropole — a transfer whose effects continue through the r > g dynamic. Capital extracted through colonial labour, resource extraction, and unequal trade was invested in the metropolitan economy, where it has compounded for centuries. The wealth of the former colonial powers is partly the accumulated return on colonial capital.

The specific case of Haiti illustrates the mechanism: after gaining independence in 1804, Haiti was forced to pay France an indemnity of 150 million francs (later reduced to 90 million) for the "loss" of colonial property — including enslaved persons. This debt, serviced over more than a century, constituted a reverse flow of capital from the formerly enslaved to the former slaveholders. The capital thus extracted has compounded in France while Haiti has remained impoverished. The r > g dynamic applied to colonial capital produces contemporary global inequality.

Piketty argues for colonial accounting: the systematic estimation of colonial capital extraction and its compounding effects, providing the empirical basis for reparative justice. This is not merely a moral argument — it is an empirical one. The data can show how much capital was extracted, what return it has generated, and what the accumulated debt is.

## How to Apply
1. **Trace the colonial capital flow.** "How much capital was extracted from this country during the colonial period? Through what mechanisms — labour, resources, trade, taxation, indemnities? What would that capital be worth today, compounded at historical returns?"
2. **Connect to contemporary inequality.** "Is the current economic structure continuous with the colonial structure? Does capital still flow from the periphery to the metropole — through resource extraction, profit repatriation, debt service, or terms of trade?"
3. **Propose colonial accounting.** "Commission a systematic accounting of colonial capital extraction and its compounding returns. The accounting provides the empirical basis for claims of reparative justice."
4. **Design reparative mechanisms.** "Reparations can take many forms: debt cancellation, preferential trade terms, technology transfer, educational investment funded by the former colonial power, and structural reform of international financial institutions."

## Examples
**Situation:** A former colonial power provides development assistance to a former colony. The assistance is presented as generosity. A critic argues it is partial reparation for colonial extraction.
**Application:** "The data can settle the argument. Estimate the colonial capital extraction: the value of resources extracted, labour coerced, trade surplus captured, and any indemnities paid. Compound this capital at historical rates of return (4–5% annually over 100–200 years). Compare the accumulated colonial debt with the cumulative development assistance provided since independence. In most cases, the colonial debt dwarfs the assistance — by orders of magnitude. The development assistance is not generosity — it is a fraction of what is owed. This is not a moral claim but an arithmetic one. The r > g dynamic, applied to colonial capital, produces a debt that current assistance levels do not approach. The policy implication: development assistance should be reframed as partial repayment of colonial debt, funded at levels that reflect the actual magnitude of the extraction, and structured to build the productive capacity that colonialism destroyed."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce all contemporary inequality to colonialism.
**Why:** Post-colonial domestic factors — governance, policy choices, institutional design — also shape inequality. Colonial extraction is a major cause but not the only one.

**Don't:** Present reparations as a simple solution.
**Why:** The forms, mechanisms, and politics of reparative justice are complex. The colonial accounting provides the empirical foundation; the political design requires negotiation and institutional innovation.
