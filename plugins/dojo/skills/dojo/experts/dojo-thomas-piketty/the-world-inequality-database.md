# The World Inequality Database

## When to Use
- When inequality analysis requires data — income shares, wealth shares, capital-income ratios, effective tax rates — and the data infrastructure needs to be established or strengthened.
- When the absence of inequality data is itself a political fact that serves those who benefit from inequality remaining unmeasured.
- When the World Inequality Database (WID.world) provides the model for data systems.

## Core Concept
The World Inequality Database, co-directed by Piketty, is the most comprehensive data infrastructure for inequality analysis. It provides historical and contemporary data on income and wealth distribution for over 100 countries, drawing on tax records, national accounts, household surveys, and wealth estimates. The database makes inequality visible — and visibility is the precondition for political action.

Piketty's methodological contribution is the combination of national accounts data (which captures total income accurately but not its distribution) with tax data (which captures distribution among top incomes accurately but misses those below the tax threshold) and survey data (which captures the distribution across the population but underestimates top incomes). The triangulation of these sources produces the most accurate available picture of inequality — including the shares of the top 1%, the top 10%, the middle 40%, and the bottom 50%.

For development practice, the data infrastructure lesson is clear: without data on the distribution of income and wealth, inequality cannot be measured, diagnosed, or addressed. Many developing countries lack the administrative data (tax records) and the statistical capacity (wealth surveys) required for comprehensive inequality analysis. Building this capacity — through improved tax administration, household surveys, and administrative data systems — is a prerequisite for evidence-based equality policy.

## How to Apply
1. **Deploy the data.** "Access the World Inequality Database (WID.world) for the country in question. What are the income shares of the top 1%, top 10%, middle 40%, bottom 50%? What is the capital-income ratio? How have these changed over time? The data provides the empirical foundation."
2. **Build data capacity.** "If the data does not exist, building the capacity to collect it is the first priority. Improved tax administration produces tax data. Household surveys produce distribution data. Wealth registries produce wealth data. The data infrastructure is the foundation."
3. **Name the data gap as political.** "The absence of inequality data is not merely an administrative deficiency — it serves those who benefit from inequality remaining unmeasured. The wealthy have an interest in their wealth being invisible."
4. **Use the data politically.** "The data makes inequality visible. Visible inequality is politically actionable. The data is not merely an analytical tool — it is a political weapon."

## Examples
**Situation:** A country does not have reliable data on wealth distribution. Policy discussions about wealth taxation are stalled because "we don't know who owns what."
**Application:** "The absence of wealth data is not a technical problem — it is a political achievement by those who benefit from their wealth remaining invisible. Building the wealth data infrastructure is therefore a political act, not merely an administrative one. Three steps: first, a national wealth registry — building on existing land registries, company registries, and financial asset records to create a comprehensive picture of who owns what. Second, wealth surveys — supplementing administrative data with household wealth surveys that capture assets across the distribution. Third, transparency requirements — mandatory disclosure of beneficial ownership for companies, trusts, and financial accounts. These measures are administratively feasible — most countries already have the building blocks. What they lack is the political will to make wealth visible. The data infrastructure is not neutral — it makes visible what power keeps hidden."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present data as sufficient.
**Why:** Data without analysis, political mobilisation, and institutional reform is inert. The data makes inequality visible; political action makes it changeable.

**Don't:** Ignore the politics of data.
**Why:** Data collection is not neutral. Who collects data, what data is collected, and who has access are political decisions that shape what can be known and therefore what can be changed.
