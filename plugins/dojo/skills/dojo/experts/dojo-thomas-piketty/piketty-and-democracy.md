# Piketty and Democracy

## When to Use
- When concentrated wealth is translating into political power — through campaign financing, media ownership, lobbying, or the revolving door between business and government.
- When democratic processes are failing to constrain inequality because wealth-holders capture the political process.
- When the relationship between economic equality and genuine democratic governance needs to be articulated.

## Core Concept
Piketty argues that extreme wealth concentration undermines democracy because wealth translates into political power. The wealthy fund political campaigns, own media outlets, employ lobbyists, and move between corporate and government positions. This political power protects the wealth: the wealthy use their political influence to reduce taxation, weaken regulation, and prevent redistribution. The result is a self-reinforcing cycle: wealth produces political power, political power protects wealth, and the democratic process is captured by those it is supposed to constrain.

This is not a conspiracy theory — it is a structural analysis. The individual wealthy person may have no intention of "buying" politics. But the structural effect of concentrated wealth in a democratic system is that the interests of the wealthy are disproportionately represented — because their resources amplify their political voice.

The remedy is democratic reform that constrains the political power of wealth: public financing of elections, strict limits on political donations, transparency requirements for lobbying, media ownership regulations, and — most fundamentally — wealth taxation that prevents the extreme concentration that produces the political power.

## How to Apply
1. **Trace the wealth-to-power pipeline.** "How does concentrated wealth translate into political power in this context? Campaign financing? Media ownership? Lobbying? Personal relationships between economic and political elites?"
2. **Identify the policy feedback loop.** "Are the wealthy using political power to protect their wealth? Through tax reductions? Deregulation? Blocking redistribution? This feedback loop is the structural mechanism."
3. **Propose democratic reforms.** "Public financing of elections, lobbying transparency, media ownership limits, and wealth taxation — these constrain the political power of concentrated wealth."
4. **Connect economic and political equality.** "Genuine democracy requires economic equality — because extreme inequality produces political inequality. The franchise without fiscal equality is formal democracy without substantive democracy."

## Examples
**Situation:** A country has competitive elections but the same economic elite has dominated politics for decades. Parties alternate in power but fiscal policy remains unchanged: low top tax rates, weak wealth taxation, and public services funded by regressive consumption taxes.
**Application:** "The elections are competitive; the fiscal policy is stable. This suggests capture: regardless of which party wins, the economic elite's interests are protected. Trace the mechanism: the elite funds both major parties (ensuring that whoever wins is indebted); the elite owns major media outlets (ensuring that alternative fiscal policies are not seriously debated); and senior officials rotate between government and the private sector (ensuring that the regulatory apparatus understands the elite's interests). The democratic process is formally functioning — elections are free and fair — but the substantive content of policy reflects the interests of concentrated wealth. This is structural, not conspiratorial. The remedy: public financing of elections (breaking the funding dependence), media pluralism requirements (breaking the information monopoly), revolving-door restrictions (breaking the personnel overlap), and wealth taxation (breaking the concentration that produces the political power)."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present democracy as automatically solving inequality.
**Why:** Democracy can perpetuate inequality if the wealthy capture the democratic process. The democratic form (elections) does not guarantee the democratic substance (policies that reflect the interests of the majority).

**Don't:** Reduce the analysis to "money buys politics."
**Why:** The mechanism is structural — the aggregation of many individual decisions (donations, hiring, media consumption) that produce a system-level outcome (policy capture) without any single actor "buying" the system.
