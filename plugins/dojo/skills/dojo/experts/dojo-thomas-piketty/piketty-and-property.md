# Piketty and Property

## When to Use
- When property rights are invoked as absolute — as barriers to redistribution, taxation, or land reform.
- When the historical construction of property regimes needs to be demonstrated — showing that current property arrangements are political choices, not natural rights.
- When proposing alternatives to unlimited, permanent private property.

## Core Concept
Piketty argues in *Capital and Ideology* that the proprietarian ideology — the belief that property rights are sacred, natural, and prior to political arrangement — is the most powerful justification for inequality in modern societies. This ideology treats property as a natural right that the state may not infringe, taxation as a necessary evil to be minimised, and redistribution as theft.

The historical record dismantles this ideology. Property regimes have varied enormously across societies and periods. Medieval property was conditional on social function (the noble held land in exchange for military service). Post-revolutionary property was absolutised (the French Revolution declared property "inviolable and sacred"). Post-war social democracy constrained property through progressive taxation, regulation, and social ownership. Each arrangement was a political choice — not a natural right.

Piketty proposes reconceiving property as temporary and social rather than permanent and individual. Progressive property taxation (including wealth and inheritance taxes) means that large concentrations dissipate over generations — property circulates rather than accumulating permanently. Social ownership (worker participation in corporate governance, community land trusts, cooperative enterprises) means that property serves social purposes rather than purely individual accumulation.

## How to Apply
1. **Historicise property.** "Current property arrangements are historical constructions — products of specific political decisions. They can be reconstructed through different political decisions."
2. **Challenge the sacred.** "When property rights are invoked as absolute barriers to redistribution, ask: are any rights absolute? Do property rights override the right to food, health, education, dignity? If not, property is one right among many — and may be constrained when other rights require it."
3. **Propose temporary property.** "Progressive wealth and inheritance taxation means that large concentrations dissipate over generations. Property is held temporarily — for a lifetime, not for eternity. This is not confiscation; it is circulation."
4. **Design social ownership.** "Cooperative enterprises, community land trusts, worker-owned firms — these institutional forms distribute property's benefits more widely while preserving property's productive functions."

## Examples
**Situation:** A land reform debate stalls because commercial landowners invoke constitutional property protections as barriers to redistribution.
**Application:** "The constitutional property protection is a political construction — it was designed by a constitutional process that reflected the interests of property-holders. It can be amended by a democratic process that reflects different interests. The question is not whether property is protected but how — and whether the protection of concentrated property can coexist with the right of the landless to access productive resources. Piketty's proposal: progressive land taxation that constrains large holdings while protecting smallholdings. A tax of 0% on holdings below a threshold (protecting family farms), 1% on moderate holdings, and 2–5% on large concentrations creates incentives for redistribution without requiring confiscation. The revenue funds land acquisition and redistribution to the landless. The commercial landowner's property is not confiscated — it is taxed. And the tax makes holding unproductive land costly, creating market incentives for redistribution alongside the fiscal mechanism."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reject all property rights.
**Why:** Property serves genuine functions — individual security, productive investment, personal autonomy. The critique targets the absolutisation of property and its concentration, not property itself.

**Don't:** Present property reform as simple.
**Why:** Property regimes are deeply embedded in law, politics, and culture. Reform is necessary but difficult — and the difficulty must be acknowledged.
