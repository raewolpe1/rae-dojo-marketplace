# The IFS Model

## When to Use
- When someone wants to understand what Internal Family Systems is and how it works.
- When someone needs the foundational concepts before going deeper into specific aspects.
- When someone is comparing IFS to other therapeutic frameworks.

## Core Concept
Internal Family Systems (IFS) is a therapeutic model developed by Richard Schwartz in the 1980s that treats the mind as naturally multiple — composed of distinct sub-personalities called parts, each with its own feelings, beliefs, and agenda. This multiplicity is not pathological. It is the normal architecture of the human psyche.

Schwartz discovered the model accidentally. Working as a family therapist with eating disorder patients, he noticed that his clients spoke about their inner experience in multiplistic terms — "part of me wants to stop but another part won't let me." Rather than treating this as confused thinking, he began to listen. He found that when he addressed these parts directly — as entities with their own perspectives and histories — the therapeutic work became remarkably more effective.

The model identifies three categories of parts. Exiles are the young, wounded parts that carry the pain of past experiences — shame, terror, abandonment, worthlessness. Because their pain is overwhelming, the system organises to keep them out of awareness. Managers are the proactive protectors — the planner, the inner critic, the caretaker, the perfectionist — whose job is to prevent the exiles' pain from surfacing by maintaining control over the person's life. Firefighters are the reactive protectors — addiction, rage, dissociation, bingeing, self-harm — that activate when an exile is triggered and its pain threatens to flood the system. Both managers and firefighters are protectors; they differ in strategy, not intention.

At the centre of this system is the Self — not a part but the core of the person, characterised by calm, curiosity, compassion, clarity, confidence, courage, creativity, and connectedness (the eight Cs). The Self is inherently capable of healing. It is undamaged even in the most traumatised person. The therapeutic work of IFS is not to fix the person but to help them access their Self so that it can relate to their parts with the qualities of a good parent — listening, understanding, and helping each part release the burdens it carries.

## How to Apply
1. **Begin by noticing parts.** When you feel a strong emotion, a familiar pattern, or an inner conflict, ask: which part of me is feeling this? The act of noticing creates separation between you and the part — and that separation is the beginning of Self.
2. **Approach parts with curiosity, not judgment.** Every part, no matter how destructive its behaviour, has a positive intention. The addictive part is managing pain. The critical part is preventing failure. Ask the part: what are you trying to do for me?
3. **Map the system.** Identify the exiles (wounded parts), the managers (proactive protectors), and the firefighters (reactive protectors). Notice how they interact — which parts trigger which, which parts are in conflict, which parts polarise each other.
4. **Work with protectors before exiles.** Never go directly to the wounded parts. Ask the protectors for permission. Address their concerns. Let them know the Self is present and capable.

## Examples
**Situation:** A woman is caught in a cycle of rigid dieting (manager) followed by binge eating (firefighter). She experiences both as "me" — she is either disciplined or out of control, and she cannot understand why she cannot simply choose discipline.
**Application:** IFS would identify two parts in a polarised relationship. The dieting part (a manager) is trying to maintain control and prevent the shame an exile carries about her body. The bingeing part (a firefighter) activates when the control becomes too oppressive, seeking relief and comfort. Both are protectors. Neither is the enemy. The therapeutic work begins by developing a relationship with each part — understanding what each fears, what each protects — and gradually accessing the exile whose pain drives the entire cycle. When the exile is unburdened, both protectors can relax into non-extreme roles.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat IFS as a technique for getting rid of unwanted parts.
**Why:** The model's foundational principle is "all parts are welcome." Trying to eliminate a part reproduces the dynamic that created the problem — the rejection of aspects of the self.

**Don't:** Rush to the exiles without protector permission.
**Why:** The protective system exists for a reason. Bypassing it destabilises the system and can produce re-traumatisation rather than healing.
