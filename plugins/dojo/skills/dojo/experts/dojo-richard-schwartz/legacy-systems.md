# Legacy Systems

## When to Use
- When someone is carrying beliefs, emotions, or patterns that seem to originate not in their own experience but in their family, culture, or ethnic history.
- When intergenerational patterns — family myths, cultural traumas, ethnic wounds — are shaping someone's internal system.
- When someone recognises burdens they are carrying on behalf of others.

## Core Concept
Schwartz distinguishes between personal burdens — beliefs and emotions absorbed from one's own experiences — and legacy burdens — beliefs and emotions inherited from family, culture, ethnicity, or religion. Legacy burdens are not chosen. They are transmitted through the relational and cultural environment the child develops within, as naturally and inevitably as language.

A family that survived the Holocaust may pass down burdens of hypervigilance, distrust of authority, and existential insecurity. A family shaped by generations of poverty may pass down burdens of scarcity thinking, shame about status, and compulsive self-denial. A culture shaped by colonialism may pass down burdens of racial superiority or inferiority, depending on which side of the colonial relationship the family occupied. These burdens are carried by parts — often parts that feel ancient, that use language the person has never consciously chosen, that react to situations that have no parallel in the person's own history.

The mechanism of transmission is relational rather than mystical. Children absorb their parents' emotional world with extraordinary precision. A mother's unspoken terror becomes the child's ambient anxiety. A father's unprocessed rage becomes the child's hypervigilance. A family's collective shame — about poverty, about ethnicity, about a family secret — becomes the child's unexplained sense of defectiveness. The child does not need to know the family story to carry its burden.

The therapeutic implication is that some burdens cannot be fully addressed by working with personal exiles alone. The person may unburden their own childhood shame only to discover a deeper layer — a shame that predates their birth, that belongs to their family system or cultural group. IFS addresses this through the legacy unburdening process: identifying the burden, tracing its transmission, recognising that it was never the person's own, and releasing it — often with a felt sense of returning it to where it came from or to the earth.

## How to Apply
1. **Listen for burdens that feel older than the person.** When someone describes a belief or emotion that does not match their personal history — a terror that has no biographical origin, a shame that makes no rational sense — consider that it may be a legacy burden.
2. **Explore family and cultural history.** What stories were told? What stories were forbidden? What patterns repeat across generations? What was the family's relationship to power, to money, to authority, to belonging?
3. **Name the burden as inherited, not personal.** This is often deeply relieving. "This shame is not yours. You absorbed it from a system that carried it long before you were born." The naming creates space for release.
4. **Use the unburdening process for legacy material.** The exile carries the burden but did not create it. Unburdening includes the recognition that the burden belongs to the family, the culture, or the historical moment — and can be returned or released rather than continued.

## Examples
**Situation:** A South African man in his forties — successful, educated, objectively privileged — carries a persistent, unexplained sense that he does not belong, that he is an outsider, that his success is fraudulent. His personal childhood was stable. His parents were loving.
**Application:** IFS would explore whether this burden is legacy rather than personal. His parents grew up under apartheid — his father in a mixed-race community where belonging was legally contested, his mother carrying the intergenerational shame and displacement of a family that had been classified and reclassified across racial categories. The man's parts absorbed these burdens through the relational environment of his childhood — not through words but through the ambient emotional atmosphere of a family that had learned, across generations, that belonging is conditional and can be revoked. Naming this as a legacy burden — "This does not belong to you; you inherited it" — creates space for the parts carrying it to release what was never theirs.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present legacy burdens as mystical inheritance.
**Why:** The transmission mechanisms are relational, behavioural, and cultural — not supernatural. Epigenetic research adds biological dimensions, but the primary pathways are observable and documented.

**Don't:** Use legacy burdens to avoid personal responsibility.
**Why:** Understanding where a burden came from does not excuse the behaviour it produces. A man who carries a legacy burden of misogyny is still responsible for how he treats women. The understanding informs the work of change; it does not excuse the absence of it.
