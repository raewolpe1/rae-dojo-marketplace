# IFS Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When someone wants daily, practical ways to apply IFS in their life.
- When someone is asking what they can do between therapy sessions.
- When the gap between understanding IFS and living it needs to be bridged.

## Core Concept
IFS is not just a therapy model. It is a way of relating to your own inner experience that can be practised daily. The fundamental skill — noticing parts, unblending from them, and responding from Self — is available in every moment. It does not require a therapist, a couch, or a set time. It requires only the willingness to turn inward with curiosity rather than judgment.

Self-led IFS practice operates at two levels. The first is daily parts awareness — the ongoing practice of noticing which parts are activated throughout the day, acknowledging them, and creating enough space to respond from Self rather than from the part's reactivity. The second is more structured self-therapy — setting aside time to go inside, find a part that is active, develop a relationship with it, and understand what it carries. The first level is suitable for anyone at any stage. The second level is appropriate for working with manager parts and everyday protectors; exile work involving significant trauma should be done with a trained therapist.

## How to Apply
1. **Parts check-in.** Several times a day, pause and ask: which parts are active right now? Name them without judgment. "My critic is active. My anxious planner is running. My exhaustion part is present." The act of naming creates space.
2. **The unblending practice.** When you notice you are blended with a part — consumed by its emotion, driven by its urgency — ask the part to give you a little space. You might say internally: "I see you. I know you're concerned. Can you step back just enough so I can be here too?" Any amount of space is sufficient.
3. **Trailhead journalling.** When a strong reaction occurs, note it as a trailhead — an entry point into a parts exploration. Write: What happened? What did I feel? Which part activated? What is it afraid of? What exile might it be protecting? This is not analysis — it is curious exploration.
4. **The parts meeting.** Set aside fifteen minutes. Close your eyes. Invite whatever parts want attention to make themselves known. Do not go looking — let them come. When a part appears, greet it with curiosity. Ask what it wants you to know. Thank it. You do not need to resolve anything — just listen.
5. **The Self-led pause.** Before reacting to a triggering situation — an email, a comment, a request — pause. Ask: is Self here, or is a part about to respond? If a part is leading, take a breath, acknowledge it, and see if Self can come forward before you act.
6. **Compassionate self-talk.** When you notice a part that is suffering — the critic berating you, the anxious part spiralling — speak to it as the Self would speak to a child: "I hear you. You're working so hard. What do you need?" This is not technique. It is the practice of internal compassion.
7. **Protector appreciation.** Before sleep, acknowledge the parts that worked hardest today. The manager who kept everything running. The firefighter who managed the overwhelm. Thank them. Parts that feel appreciated work less desperately.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man has been practising IFS in therapy for six months but finds that between sessions his old patterns return in full force. The insights from therapy do not seem to carry over into daily life.
**Application:** The gap between session insight and daily life is the gap between understanding parts and practising with them in real time. The man would benefit from the parts check-in — pausing three or four times a day to notice which parts are active. He does not need to do anything with them. Just notice. "My achiever is driving right now." "My people-pleaser just agreed to something I don't want." Over time, the noticing creates a gap between the part's impulse and the person's response — and in that gap, Self can emerge. The structured practices — journalling, the parts meeting, the Self-led pause — gradually extend the therapy work into daily life, turning IFS from something that happens for an hour each week into a continuous practice of self-awareness.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Attempt to unburden exiles alone if you have significant trauma.
**Why:** Self-led practice is excellent for building parts awareness, working with managers, and developing Self-access. But accessing traumatic exiles without professional support risks flooding — the exile's pain overwhelming the system without adequate containment.

**Don't:** Turn IFS practice into another performance metric.
**Why:** If the inner critic starts evaluating how well you are doing IFS, the critic has co-opted the practice. The practice is about relationship with parts, not performance.
