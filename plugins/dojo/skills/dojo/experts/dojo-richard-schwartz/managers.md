# Managers

## When to Use
- When someone is struggling with perfectionism, chronic self-criticism, people-pleasing, overwork, or compulsive control.
- When someone wants to understand why they cannot relax, why they are always planning or monitoring.
- When the inner critic is a central feature of someone's suffering.

## Core Concept
Managers are the proactive protectors of the IFS system. Their job is to prevent the exiles' pain from surfacing by maintaining control over the person's inner and outer life. They operate preemptively — anticipating threats, enforcing standards, monitoring for danger — so that the exiles are never triggered in the first place.

Common manager parts include the inner critic (which enforces impossibly high standards to prevent failure and the shame the exile carries about it), the caretaker (which focuses on others' needs to prevent the exile's experience of rejection or abandonment), the planner (which tries to control the future to prevent the exile's terror of uncertainty), the perfectionist (which demands flawless performance to prevent the exile's belief that it is fundamentally defective), and the people-pleaser (which ensures everyone is happy to prevent the exile's experience of being unwanted).

Managers are often the most socially rewarded parts. The culture celebrates the driven achiever, the selfless caretaker, the meticulous perfectionist. This makes them harder to recognise as protectors in extreme roles — the person and everyone around them may genuinely believe that the manager's relentless standards are simply "who they are." But the telltale sign is the absence of choice. A healthy capacity for achievement is flexible — the person can work hard and also rest, can care for others and also care for themselves. A manager in an extreme role is rigid — the person cannot stop, cannot rest, cannot tolerate imperfection, because the manager believes that any relaxation of vigilance will expose the exile's pain.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the manager.** When someone describes patterns of control, self-criticism, or compulsive accommodation, name the part. "That sounds like a part of you that works very hard to keep you safe. Can we get to know it?"
2. **Appreciate the manager's work before asking it to change.** Managers respond poorly to being told they are wrong or unnecessary. They respond well to being seen and appreciated. "You've been doing this for a long time. What are you afraid would happen if you stopped?"
3. **Discover what the manager protects.** Every manager guards an exile. The inner critic protects the child who was shamed. The caretaker protects the child who was abandoned when it had needs. The perfectionist protects the child who was only loved conditionally.
4. **Help the manager trust the Self.** Managers step back not when they are forced but when they trust that the Self is present and capable of handling what they have been managing. This trust develops gradually, through demonstrated competence.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man in his fifties works seventy-hour weeks, never takes holidays, and is admired as a leader. He is also exhausted, his marriage is hollow, and he has chronic insomnia. He does not understand why he cannot simply work less.
**Application:** IFS would identify a powerful manager — the achiever/worker — that has been in an extreme role for decades. This part believes that the man's worth depends entirely on his productivity. It is protecting an exile — perhaps a boy who learned that he was only valued when he performed, whose father was emotionally present only when the boy succeeded, whose sense of being lovable was conditional on being useful. The manager cannot relax because it believes that any reduction in output will expose the exile's core belief: that without achievement, he is nothing. The work is not to force the man to work less. It is to understand the manager's fear, develop a relationship with the exile it protects, and help the exile release the burden of conditional worth.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Try to silence or override the inner critic.
**Why:** The inner critic is a protector. Fighting it activates other protectors and confirms its belief that vigilance is necessary. Understanding it is far more effective than combating it.

**Don't:** Assume that someone's productive, organised, or caring exterior means they are well.
**Why:** Managers create a surface of functionality that can mask profound inner pain. The most accomplished, most selfless people are sometimes the most burdened.
