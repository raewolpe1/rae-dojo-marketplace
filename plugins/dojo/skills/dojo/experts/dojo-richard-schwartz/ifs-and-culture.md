# IFS and Culture

## When to Use
- When someone is interested in how IFS extends beyond individual therapy into social, cultural, and systemic work.
- When someone wants to understand how parts dynamics operate at collective levels.
- When the intersection of inner work and outer change is relevant.

## Core Concept
Schwartz has increasingly extended IFS beyond individual therapy into what he calls the broader application of the model to families, organisations, communities, and cultures. The core insight is that the same dynamics that operate within individuals — protective parts, exiled vulnerabilities, polarisation, and the possibility of Self-leadership — operate at every level of human organisation.

In families, parts of different family members interact to create systemic patterns — the child whose manager part caretakes the parent, the parent whose firefighter part rages when their exile is triggered. In organisations, parts dynamics produce corporate cultures — the manager-dominated culture of relentless productivity, the firefighter-dominated culture of crisis and blame. In societies, parts dynamics produce ideology — the exile's terror projected onto out-groups, the manager's control expressed as authoritarianism, the firefighter's violence enacted as war.

The concept of legacy burdens (explored more fully in the legacy-systems file) is central to this extension. Legacy burdens are beliefs, emotions, and energies that are passed down through families, cultures, and ethnic groups across generations. Racism, for instance, can be understood as a legacy burden — a set of beliefs and reactive patterns absorbed from the culture and carried by parts that did not create them. These parts can be identified and unburdened just as personal parts can, though the process is more complex because the burdens are reinforced by ongoing cultural systems.

Schwartz's most ambitious claim is that Self-leadership could transform not just individuals but organisations and societies. If leaders could govern from Self rather than from their protective parts — with calm, curiosity, and compassion rather than fear, control, and reactivity — the systems they create would reflect those qualities. This is aspirational rather than demonstrated, but it represents the direction in which Schwartz is taking the model.

## How to Apply
1. **Notice your parts' reactions to cultural and political issues.** When you feel intense reactivity about a social issue, ask: which part of me is activated? What exile is it protecting? This does not mean the issue is not real — it means your response to it is shaped by your parts.
2. **Identify cultural parts.** Groups develop collective protectors — the part of an organisation that maintains a culture of niceness to avoid conflict, the part of a community that scapegoats outsiders to manage collective anxiety.
3. **Apply Self-leadership to group settings.** In meetings, in families, in community discussions — practise responding from Self rather than from reactive parts. Self-energy is contagious; one Self-led person can shift a group dynamic.
4. **Recognise that inner work and outer work are complementary.** Unburdening your personal racism parts does not excuse you from working for structural change. Understanding your parts does not replace political action. Both are necessary.

## Examples
**Situation:** A team leader notices that his team avoids all conflict — meetings are pleasant but nothing difficult is ever discussed. Important issues fester until they become crises.
**Application:** IFS would identify a collective manager — a part of the team's culture that maintains harmony to prevent the collective exile's fear of rupture or abandonment. Individual team members' own managers collude with this cultural part — each person's conflict-avoidant protector reinforces the group norm. The leader can address this by modelling Self-leadership: naming the dynamic directly, with curiosity rather than blame, and creating safety for the team's parts to bring difficult material forward. "I notice we tend to avoid hard conversations. I think there's a part of our culture that is afraid of conflict. What would it take for us to bring the harder topics into the room?"

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce systemic oppression to a parts issue.
**Why:** Racism, poverty, and injustice are structural realities that require structural responses. IFS can help individuals and groups process their internal experience of these realities, but parts work is not a substitute for policy change, institutional reform, or collective action.

**Don't:** Use IFS to bypass political engagement.
**Why:** "I've done my inner work on racism" is a manager's move if it substitutes for actual engagement with racial justice. Inner work and outer work are parallel, not sequential.
