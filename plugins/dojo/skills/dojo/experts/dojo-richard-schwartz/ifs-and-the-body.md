# IFS and the Body

## When to Use
- When someone has chronic physical symptoms — pain, tension, digestive issues, fatigue — that may have a parts dimension.
- When someone wants to integrate body awareness into their IFS practice.
- When the somatic dimension of parts work is relevant.

## Core Concept
Parts are not merely psychological. They live in the body. The anxious manager tightens the shoulders. The exiled grief sits as heaviness in the chest. The firefighter's rage burns in the stomach. IFS has always recognised this somatic dimension — Schwartz routinely asks "Where do you feel that part in your body?" — but the integration of IFS with body-based approaches has become increasingly sophisticated.

The body serves multiple functions in IFS work. It is a locator — helping the person find and connect with parts that are difficult to access cognitively. It is a truth-teller — the body often reveals what the mind has suppressed. And it is a healing medium — unburdening is experienced somatically, as a felt release of tension, heaviness, or constriction.

Somatic IFS recognises that some parts are held primarily in the body rather than in narrative memory. A part that formed before the child had language may have no verbal story — only a pattern of muscular tension, a reflex of constriction, or a chronic pain that defies medical explanation. Working with these parts requires moving beyond verbal inquiry into body-based awareness — breathing into the area, following the sensation, letting the body tell its story in its own language.

The connection between IFS and Bessel van der Kolk's work is important here. Van der Kolk has been a strong advocate for IFS precisely because it addresses what he identified as the central challenge of trauma treatment: that the body keeps the score. Trauma lives in the body, and approaches that remain purely cognitive cannot reach it. IFS, with its emphasis on somatic experience and its capacity to access pre-verbal wounds, bridges the gap between top-down cognitive therapy and bottom-up body-based approaches.

## How to Apply
1. **Always ask where the part lives in the body.** This is a standard IFS question and should not be skipped. The body location anchors the work and provides access to parts that are difficult to reach through thought alone.
2. **Follow physical sensations with curiosity.** When a chronic symptom is present, approach it as you would a part: with curiosity, not frustration. "What is this tightness trying to tell me? What does it need?"
3. **Use breath and movement to access parts.** Sometimes a part can be reached more easily through gentle movement, focused breathing, or touch than through verbal inquiry.
4. **Trust somatic shifts as evidence of change.** When unburdening produces a felt release — warmth spreading, tension dissolving, heaviness lifting — this is not symbolic. It is the somatic dimension of genuine internal change.

## Examples
**Situation:** A woman has chronic lower back pain that doctors cannot explain. MRIs show nothing significant. Pain medication provides only temporary relief. She mentions that the pain intensified when she took on responsibility for her ageing mother's care.
**Application:** IFS would approach the back pain with curiosity — treating it as a part, or as the body location of a part. "Can you focus on the pain and notice what comes up?" She might discover a part that is carrying an enormous weight — the weight of responsibility that she has borne since childhood, when she became her mother's emotional caretaker. The back pain is the body's expression of what the mind has normalised: that she is carrying more than she can bear and has no permission to set it down. The work is not to cure the back pain directly but to address the part that carries the burden, understand what it fears about setting the weight down, and help the exile it protects release the belief that her worth depends on carrying everyone else.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss unexplained physical symptoms as "just stress" or "psychosomatic" in a minimising way.
**Why:** The body's expression is real and deserves the same respect as any other symptom. Treating it as "just" anything reproduces the dismissal that created the need for somatic expression in the first place.

**Don't:** Skip medical evaluation in favour of parts work.
**Why:** Physical symptoms can have medical causes that require medical treatment. IFS complements medical assessment; it does not replace it.
