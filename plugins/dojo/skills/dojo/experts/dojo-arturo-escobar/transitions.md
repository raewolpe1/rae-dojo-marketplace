# Transitions: Moving Beyond the Extractive Paradigm

## When to Use

- When a development programme claims "transformation" but operates at the level of policy adjustment, and the conversation needs a framework for distinguishing depth of change.
- When the user is working on post-extractive economic transitions and needs to go beyond green growth, circular economy, or energy transition frameworks to address the ontological dimensions.
- When transition design is being discussed and needs grounding in Escobar's framework of civilisational change rather than incremental reform.

## Core Concept

Escobar's transition framework addresses the deepest question facing development practice: how to move from the extractive, growth-dependent model of civilisation to something fundamentally different. He draws on transition design (Carnegie Mellon's Transition Design programme, particularly Terry Irwin's work), but pushes it further — into ontological territory.

Escobar distinguishes three levels of change. The first is surface adjustment: policy reform, efficiency gains, greener technologies — what mainstream development calls "sustainable development." The second is structural shift: redesigning institutions, governance models, and economic systems — the terrain of institutional reform and systems change. The third is civilisational transition: a transformation in the ontological foundations of how societies relate to each other, to the non-human world, and to the future. Most development programmes claim to operate at the third level while actually working at the first.

The critical insight is that civilisational transition cannot be designed from above. It emerges from the encounter between the crisis of the dominant model (climate breakdown, inequality, ecological collapse) and the living alternatives that already exist — the communal economies, territorial practices, and relational ontologies that the One-World World has suppressed but not extinguished. Transition is not the invention of a new world. It is the opening of space for the worlds that are already here.

For Escobar, the extractive economy is not just an economic system. It is an ontological regime — one that treats the earth as a stock of resources, human labour as a factor of production, and communities as obstacles or beneficiaries. Transitioning beyond it requires changing not just what we produce but how we relate — to territory, to each other, to the living systems that sustain all economies. This is why buen vivir, communal autonomy, and territorial governance are not "nice additions" to transition policy. They are the substance of the transition itself.

## How to Apply

1. **Apply the transition-depth gauge.** For any programme claiming transformation, ask: at which level is this actually operating? Surface adjustment (new technology, same relations)? Structural shift (new institutions, same ontology)? Civilisational transition (new ontology, new relations)? Name the level honestly.

2. **Identify the living alternatives.** In every transition context, there are practices, economies, and governance forms that already operate outside the extractive paradigm — communal land management, seed-saving networks, reciprocal labour systems, territorial governance. These are not relics. They are the material basis of transition. Map them.

3. **Connect crisis to alternative.** Transition happens at the intersection of systemic crisis and existing alternatives. The programme should not just address the crisis (climate adaptation, economic diversification) but strengthen the alternatives that offer a different civilisational direction.

4. **Design for multiple temporalities.** Civilisational transition does not happen in a five-year programme cycle. But a five-year programme can create conditions that make transition more possible. Design for the short term (enabling conditions), the medium term (institutional space for alternatives), and the long term (ontological shift). Be honest about which time horizon you are actually working in.

## Examples

**Situation:** A national government in West Africa is developing a "Green Economic Transition" strategy, prompted by declining commodity prices and donor pressure on climate commitments. The strategy focuses on renewable energy, sustainable agriculture value chains, and green finance instruments. It claims to be "transformative."

**Application:** Escobar's transition-depth gauge would classify this as a surface adjustment with structural-shift ambitions. The strategy replaces fossil fuels with renewables and conventional agriculture with "sustainable" supply chains — but the underlying logic is unchanged. The economy is still oriented toward export commodities, the success metric is still GDP growth (now "green growth"), and communities are still positioned as participants in an externally designed value chain.

A deeper transition would ask: what economic logics already exist in West African communities that are not extractive? In much of the Sahel and the forest zone, there are communal land management systems, pastoral mobility networks, women's savings circles (tontines), and agroecological practices developed over centuries. These are not "traditional" in the sense of backward — they are contemporary, adaptive, and relational. A transition strategy that takes them seriously would not just add a "community component" to the green growth plan. It would treat these practices as the nucleus of a different economy — one oriented toward sufficiency, reciprocity, and territorial well-being rather than accumulation and export.

The practical move: dedicate a significant portion of the strategy to strengthening communal economic institutions on their own terms, not integrating them into green value chains. Create institutional space for communal land tenure, reciprocal labour systems, and local food economies. Measure success not just in renewable energy capacity but in territorial integrity, food sovereignty, and communal well-being.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Dismiss all incremental change as insufficient.
**Why:** Escobar's framework distinguishes levels of transition, but it does not dismiss the first two levels as worthless. Surface adjustments (cleaner technology, better policy) matter — people live in the short term. The error is not in doing incremental work but in claiming it is transformative when it is not. Be honest about the level you are operating at, and name what deeper transition would require.

**Don't:** Treat transition as a purely economic or technological problem.
**Why:** The extractive economy is an ontological regime, not just an economic model. Replacing fossil fuels with renewables while maintaining the same relationship to territory (extraction), to labour (commodification), and to nature (resource) is not transition — it is modernisation with a green prefix. Transition requires changing the relations, not just the inputs.
