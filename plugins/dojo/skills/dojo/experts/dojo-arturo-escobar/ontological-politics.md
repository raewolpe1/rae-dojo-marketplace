# Ontological Politics: When the Conflict Is Between Worlds

## When to Use

- When a development conflict appears to be about interests (land rights, resource access, compensation) but the deeper structure is about what kind of thing the land, the resource, or the community actually is — a collision between worlds, not a disagreement within one.
- When stakeholder engagement and dialogue have failed and the reason is not bad faith but ontological incommensurability — the parties are not disagreeing about facts within a shared reality; they are operating in different realities.
- When the user needs the ontological turn applied to development — the reframing of politics from contests between opinions to contests between worlds.

## Core Concept

Ontological politics is Escobar's application of the "ontological turn" in anthropology to development studies and political ecology. The ontological turn, developed by thinkers like Eduardo Viveiros de Castro, Marisol de la Cadena, Mario Blaser, and John Law, proposes that many conflicts conventionally understood as disagreements about knowledge, values, or interests are actually conflicts between different ontologies — different ways of being, different realities.

The classical example: a mining company and an Indigenous community disagree about whether to extract minerals from a mountain. The standard development frame reads this as a conflict of interests — the company wants profit, the community wants environmental protection or fair compensation. Negotiation, cost-benefit analysis, and environmental impact assessment are deployed. But Escobar, following de la Cadena's work in the Andes, asks: what if the disagreement is not about interests but about what the mountain is? For the company, the mountain is a geological formation containing extractable minerals. For the community, the mountain is a sentient being — an apu, a tirakuna — with whom one has reciprocal obligations. These are not two perspectives on the same mountain. They are two different mountains — or, more precisely, two different worlds in which the mountain exists differently.

This is ontological politics: the recognition that political conflict can be a collision between incommensurable realities, not just a disagreement between stakeholders who share a common reality but differ on how to manage it. The implications for development practice are profound. Environmental impact assessments, cost-benefit analyses, and stakeholder consultations all presuppose a shared ontological ground — a single reality about which the parties have different views. When the conflict is ontological, these instruments fail not because they are poorly designed but because they are operating inside one world while the conflict is between worlds.

Escobar does not use ontological politics to declare negotiation impossible. He uses it to show why certain negotiations fail and to open space for a different kind of political engagement — one that begins by acknowledging the existence of multiple realities rather than assuming a single one. This is the move from multicultural politics (different cultures within one nature) to multinaturalist politics (different natures, different realities, requiring a different kind of political encounter).

## How to Apply

1. **Test for ontological conflict.** When a development conflict resists resolution through standard negotiation, ask: are the parties disagreeing within a shared reality, or are they operating in different realities? The diagnostic is the persistence of "misunderstanding" despite good-faith communication. If both sides have been heard and still cannot agree on what the problem is, the conflict may be ontological.

2. **Map the ontological stakes.** For each party, identify not just their position and interest but their ontology — what kind of thing is the disputed entity (the river, the forest, the mountain, the seed)? If one party treats it as a commodity and the other treats it as a relative, the conflict is between worlds.

3. **Refuse premature translation.** The OWW's standard move is to translate the community's ontology into its own terms: "they say the river is a living being — what they really mean is they value clean water." This translation neutralises the ontological claim. Resist it. Take the community's ontology at face value: the river is a living being. What follows from that for the programme design?

4. **Create space for ontological co-existence.** If the conflict is between worlds, the solution cannot come from within only one of them. Look for institutional and legal mechanisms that create space for multiple ontologies to operate — the rights of nature in Ecuadorian law, Indigenous territorial governance, community veto over extractive projects. These are not compromises within one world. They are pluriversal political achievements.

## Examples

**Situation:** A large-scale irrigation project in northern Kenya proposes to dam a river that flows through pastoralist and agro-pastoralist communities. The project's environmental and social impact assessment (ESIA) identifies the communities as "project-affected persons" and proposes compensation for lost grazing land, resettlement of displaced households, and a benefit-sharing mechanism (access to irrigated plots). Community consultations have been conducted, but the communities continue to oppose the project. The project team reads this as "resistance to change" or "capture by elites."

**Application:** Escobar's ontological-politics diagnosis would reframe the conflict entirely. The ESIA treats the river as a hydrological resource — a flow of water measurable in cubic metres, manageable through engineering, and convertible into irrigated agriculture. The pastoralist communities may relate to the river as something ontologically different — a living entity woven into seasonal mobility patterns, ceremonial life, and inter-clan relations. The river is not a "resource" they use. It is a relation they inhabit.

The compensation framework fails not because the amounts are wrong but because it presupposes a shared ontology: the river is a thing that can be replaced or compensated for. If the river is a living relation, it cannot be compensated — it can only be maintained or destroyed. The communities are not "resisting change." They are defending a world that the ESIA cannot see.

The practical implication is not necessarily to cancel the project. It is to acknowledge that the ESIA, the cost-benefit analysis, and the stakeholder consultation are operating inside one world — the engineering-economic one — and that the communities' opposition is coming from a different world with its own validity. A pluriversal approach would ask: can the project be redesigned so that the river's relational integrity is maintained? Can the communities' territorial governance over the river be recognised? Can the project and the community's world co-exist — or does this particular project require ontological occupation?

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use ontological politics to declare all conflicts incommensurable and all negotiation impossible.
**Why:** Escobar does not claim that every disagreement is ontological. Many development conflicts are indeed about interests, resources, and power within a shared reality. The ontological-politics diagnosis is a tool for specific situations — where standard negotiation fails because the parties are not in the same world. Using it universally is intellectually lazy and practically paralysing.

**Don't:** Reduce ontological politics to "they have a different worldview."
**Why:** "Worldview" is an epistemological category — it means a different perspective on the same world. Ontological politics says something stronger: there are different worlds, not different views of one world. The distinction matters because "worldview" can be managed through dialogue and mutual understanding, while ontological difference requires a different kind of political engagement — one that does not assume a shared ground.
