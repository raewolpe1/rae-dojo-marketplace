# Autonomous Design: Designing With, Not For

## When to Use

- When a programme design process concentrates design authority in external agencies — donors, consultants, government ministries — and needs to be reoriented toward community self-determination.
- When "participatory design" has reached its limits: the community participates, but the framework, categories, and success criteria remain externally defined.
- When the user needs a design philosophy rooted in place, relationality, and radical interdependence rather than universalising user-centred design.

## Core Concept

Autonomous design is Escobar's answer to the question: what does design look like from the pluriverse? It is developed most fully in Designs for the Pluriverse (2018), where Escobar argues that all design is ontological — every designed object, system, or programme enacts a particular world. The modern design tradition, from industrial design to human-centred design, enacts the world of the autonomous individual, the commodity, and the market. Autonomous design enacts a different world — one of communal relations, territorial integrity, and radical interdependence.

The key distinction is between "designing for" and enabling design capacity. Conventional development design operates in the "for" mode: experts design interventions for communities, who are positioned as users, beneficiaries, or (at best) participants. Autonomous design shifts the question entirely. It asks: how can a community's own capacity to design its world be strengthened? The design authority — the power to define the problem, the good life, and the criteria of success — stays with the community.

Autonomy here is not sovereign independence. Escobar draws on the political thought of Latin American social movements, particularly Afro-Colombian community councils and the Zapatistas, where autonomy means the capacity to define the terms of one's existence in relation to others. It is relational autonomy — you determine your own path, but always in connection with the territory, the community, the non-human world. This is what Escobar calls radical interdependence: not the interdependence of globalised supply chains but the interdependence of beings who share a world.

For development practitioners, this reframes everything. The programme is not the design — the community's life-project is the design. The programme is, at best, an enabling condition. The theory of change is not yours to write.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask the design-authority question.** In every programme, identify who holds the power to define the problem, the solution, and the criteria of success. If the answer is the donor, the consultant, or the ministry, the design is not autonomous. Trace the chain of design authority and identify where it can be redistributed.

2. **Start from the community's own territorial or life plan.** Many Indigenous and Afro-descendent communities in Latin America and Africa have developed their own plans — planes de vida (life plans), planes de manejo territorial (territorial management plans), or equivalent instruments. These are autonomous-design documents. Begin there, not with the donor's country strategy.

3. **Replace user-centred design with place-centred design.** The "user" is an abstraction of the modern individual. Place-centred design begins with the territory — the meshwork of human, non-human, ecological, and spiritual relations that constitute a community's world. The design must serve the place, not just the individuals in it.

4. **Design for relational well-being, not individual outcomes.** The indicators of success in autonomous design are communal: territorial integrity, food sovereignty, cultural continuity, ecological health, intergenerational knowledge transmission. These are not reducible to individual outcome metrics. Design the M&E framework to capture relational well-being.

## Examples

**Situation:** An international NGO is designing a "livelihoods programme" for Afro-Colombian communities on the Pacific coast. The programme offers training in entrepreneurship, access to microfinance, and support for market integration. The design was developed at headquarters and validated through "community consultations."

**Application:** Escobar, who has worked with these communities for decades, would reframe from the ground up. These communities have their own economic logic — what he calls "economies of difference." They practise communal land tenure through collectively titled territories (titulacion colectiva). They manage mangrove ecosystems through ancestral knowledge. They have reciprocal labour systems (minga, mano cambiada) that the microfinance model cannot see.

The autonomous-design approach would begin with the community council's (consejo comunitario) own territorial plan. What economic activities does the community identify as strengthening its territorial autonomy? Perhaps artisanal fishing with traditional techniques, cacao cultivation under forest canopy, or ethno-tourism on the community's own terms. The programme becomes an enabling infrastructure for the community's own design — providing resources, connections, and protection without imposing the entrepreneurship frame. The minga is not a "coping mechanism" to be replaced by microfinance. It is an economic institution of a different world.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Export autonomous design as a universal methodology.
**Why:** The irony would be fatal. Autonomous design is rooted in specific struggles — Afro-Colombian territorial movements, Zapatista autonomy, Andean communal governance. To extract it into a toolkit deployable anywhere is to reproduce the universalising logic it was created to resist. The principles travel; the methodology must be reinvented in each place.

**Don't:** Confuse autonomy with isolation or autarky.
**Why:** Escobar's concept of autonomy is explicitly relational. Communities do not design in isolation — they design in connection with territory, with other communities, with non-human beings, and yes, with markets and states, but on their own terms. Autonomous design that becomes a justification for abandoning communities to their own resources is a perversion of the concept.
