# The One-World World: Modernity's Claim to Be the Only Reality

## When to Use

- When a programme, strategy, or institutional frame needs its hidden ontological assumption exposed — the assumption that there is only one valid reality and all others are deficient versions of it.
- When even progressive development language (inclusion, participation, empowerment) is reproducing the structure it claims to overcome.
- When the conversation needs to move from "this policy is flawed" to "this policy presupposes a world that erases other worlds."

## Core Concept

The One-World World (OWW) is Escobar's name for modernity's deepest operation: not just claiming to be the best world, but claiming to be the only world. Every other way of being — Indigenous territorial governance, Andean sumak kawsay, African ubuntu, peasant subsistence economies — is positioned not as an alternative world but as an underdeveloped version of the modern one. The OWW does not argue with other worlds. It converts them into problems to be solved.

This concept draws on the work of John Law, who described how Euro-American modernity enacts a "one-world world" — a single reality composed of objective facts, knowable through science, manageable through policy. Escobar extends this into development studies: the entire apparatus of development — from the World Bank to the SDGs — presupposes the OWW. It assumes that all societies are on a single trajectory, that the destination is known (industrial modernity, liberal democracy, market integration), and that the only question is how to get there faster and more equitably.

The OWW is not a conspiracy. It is an ontological habit — so deeply embedded that it feels like common sense. When a donor writes "evidence-based policy," they mean evidence legible within the modern scientific frame. When a government writes "inclusive growth," it means growth that includes more people in the existing economy, not growth redefined by another economy entirely. When an NGO writes "community voice," it means the community may now speak within the project's categories — not that the project's categories might be wrong.

Escobar's crucial move is to show that this is not a methodological problem fixable by better data or more participation. It is an ontological problem: the development apparatus cannot see other worlds because it has already decided there is only one. The solution is not inclusion. It is ontological opening — making space for multiple worlds to co-exist.

## How to Apply

1. **Run the one-world test.** Take the theory of change, the programme document, or the strategy and ask: can this be fully stated without reference to any world other than the modern liberal-capitalist one? If yes, it is inside the OWW. Name what it cannot see.

2. **Trace the universalising move.** Identify the moment where a culturally specific concept (GDP, resilience, empowerment, governance) is deployed as if it were universal. This is the OWW at work — converting one world's concepts into the measure for all worlds.

3. **Show what the frame erases.** For every category the programme uses, ask: what does this look like from inside another world? "Productivity" in a communal economy means something different from "productivity" in an industrial one. "Land tenure" in an Indigenous ontology means something different from "land tenure" in a property-rights framework. The OWW erases these differences by treating its own categories as the only valid ones.

4. **Distinguish reform from refusal.** The OWW can absorb enormous amounts of reform — participatory methods, gender mainstreaming, climate adaptation — without changing its ontological structure. The question is not whether the programme is reformed but whether it has opened space for another world to operate on its own terms.

## Examples

**Situation:** A bilateral donor is funding a "governance strengthening" programme in Mozambique. The programme aims to build capacity in district-level government, improve public financial management, and strengthen accountability mechanisms. The theory of change assumes that effective governance means transparent, rules-based, bureaucratic administration on the liberal-democratic model.

**Application:** Escobar's OWW diagnosis would proceed in layers. First, note that the programme's concept of "governance" is culturally specific — it is the Weberian bureaucratic model exported globally since the colonial era. Mozambique's communities have governance traditions — lineage-based authority, communal land management, spiritual leadership — that the programme cannot see because they do not fit the "governance" category as defined. They are not "weak governance." They are a different world's governance.

Second, note that "accountability" in the programme means upward accountability to the state and the donor, legible through financial reports and audit trails. But in many Mozambican communities, accountability runs laterally — to kin, to ancestors, to the land. This is not absence of accountability. It is accountability within a relational ontology that the liberal frame does not recognise.

The OWW move is to acknowledge these existing systems and ask: what if the programme were designed to strengthen the community's own governance rather than to replace it with the liberal model? This does not mean romanticising traditional authority. It means granting it ontological standing — treating it as a valid governance world, not as a deficiency to be corrected.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use OWW detection as a blanket dismissal of all modern institutions.
**Why:** Escobar is not anti-modern in a simple sense. He is against modernity's claim to be the only world. Modern medicine, for instance, is a powerful practice — but it becomes part of the OWW when it denies ontological standing to other healing traditions. The diagnostic is about the universalising claim, not about modernity per se.

**Don't:** Confuse the OWW with Western culture.
**Why:** The OWW is an ontological structure, not a cultural identity. It has been adopted by elites across the Global South as thoroughly as by those in the North. The critique is not "Western bad, non-Western good." It is: any single world that claims to be the only world is engaged in ontological occupation, regardless of its geographic origin.
