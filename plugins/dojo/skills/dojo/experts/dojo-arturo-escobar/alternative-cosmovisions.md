# Alternative Cosmovisions: Buen Vivir, Ubuntu, and the Substance of the Pluriverse

## When to Use

- When the pluriverse needs to be grounded in specific philosophical traditions — not as illustration but as substantive alternatives with their own ontological content.
- When a programme is operating in an Andean, Amazonian, African, or other context where non-Western conceptions of well-being exist and need to be engaged as philosophy, not as "local culture."
- When the user needs to understand what buen vivir, sumak kawsay, ubuntu, or other frameworks actually propose — and why they are not reducible to "sustainable development with Indigenous characteristics."

## Core Concept

The pluriverse is not an empty frame. It is populated by specific worlds, each with its own philosophy of the good life. Escobar insists that these alternatives must be named and engaged substantively — otherwise the pluriverse becomes a vague gesture toward "diversity" that changes nothing.

Buen vivir (living well) and its Kichwa formulation sumak kawsay name a philosophy rooted in Andean and Amazonian Indigenous traditions. The core proposition is that well-being is relational and ecological — you cannot live well if Pachamama (the earth-being) does not live well. This is not environmentalism added to development. It is a different ontology: the earth is not a resource to be managed but a living being to whom you have reciprocal obligations. Buen vivir was constitutionalised in Ecuador (2008) and Bolivia (2009), creating the extraordinary legal situation where a non-Western ontology has formal political standing. The results have been contested — the Correa and Morales governments often contradicted buen vivir in practice through extractivist policies — but the constitutional recognition itself is a pluriversal achievement.

Ubuntu — often glossed as "I am because we are" (umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu) — names the Southern African philosophy of relational personhood. A person becomes a person through other persons. Well-being is inherently communal. This is not collectivism in the Marxist sense (the collective as political subject) but something ontologically different: personhood itself is relational. Ubuntu has been mobilised in transitional justice (the Truth and Reconciliation Commission), in governance philosophy, and increasingly in development theory, though it is often domesticated into "social capital" or "community cohesion" — framings that neutralise its ontological force.

Other substantive alternatives include: the Zapatista concept of autonomy (a world in which many worlds fit); the Indigenous concept of territory as living being (not property); the Afro-Colombian concept of comunalidad; the Buddhist notion of interdependent co-arising; and many others. Each carries specific philosophical content. Each proposes a different relationship between humans, community, and the living world.

Escobar's insistence is that these are not "perspectives on development" to be respectfully noted and then set aside. They are competing ontologies of the good life — as rigorous and as political as liberalism or Marxism. To treat them as "culture" while retaining the liberal-capitalist frame as the operating system is to perform the One-World World's most characteristic move: tolerance without recognition.

## How to Apply

1. **Engage the specific philosophical content.** Do not invoke buen vivir or ubuntu as slogans. Ask: what does this tradition actually propose about personhood, well-being, the relationship to nature, the meaning of economy? The answer will be specific and will challenge the programme's assumptions.

2. **Check for domestication.** When these concepts appear in programme documents, ask: has the concept been domesticated — translated into the development frame's categories in a way that neutralises its force? "Ubuntu = social capital" is domestication. "Buen vivir = sustainable development" is domestication. The concept loses its ontological challenge when it is made to fit the existing frame.

3. **Acknowledge internal contestation.** These traditions are not monolithic. Buen vivir is contested between Indigenous organisations, mestizo intellectuals, and state actors. Ubuntu has been critiqued by African feminists as potentially reinforcing patriarchal communalism. Engaging these traditions seriously means engaging their internal debates, not presenting a sanitised version.

4. **Grant constitutional and institutional standing.** Where possible, advocate for these philosophies to have formal standing in governance, planning, and evaluation — not as "cultural considerations" but as legitimate frameworks for defining the good life. The Ecuadorian and Bolivian constitutions, despite their contradictions, show this is possible.

## Examples

**Situation:** A development agency is designing a social protection programme for rural South Africa. The programme uses a standard framework: conditional cash transfers, targeting based on poverty lines, individual household beneficiaries, and graduation metrics based on income thresholds. The agency wants to be "culturally sensitive" and has added a paragraph about ubuntu to the programme document.

**Application:** Escobar would diagnose the ubuntu reference as decorative domestication. The programme's operating ontology is individualist: the unit is the household, the metric is income, the trajectory is graduation into market participation. Ubuntu is added as a cultural gloss that changes nothing in the design.

Taking ubuntu seriously would require redesigning the programme from the ground up. If personhood is relational, then the unit of analysis is not the household but the network of mutual obligation — the extended family, the neighbourhood, the community. If well-being is communal, then the metric is not individual income but the quality of social relations — are people able to fulfil their obligations to each other? Are the ceremonies and practices that constitute communal personhood being maintained?

Practically, this might mean: shifting from individual cash transfers to communal investment funds managed by community structures; replacing poverty-line targeting with community-identified vulnerability (who is falling out of the relational network?); replacing graduation metrics with relational well-being indicators (communal labour participation, ceremony maintenance, intergenerational knowledge transmission). This is not adding ubuntu to the existing programme. It is letting ubuntu redesign the programme.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat alternative cosmovisions as museum pieces — static traditions from a pre-modern past.
**Why:** Buen vivir, ubuntu, and other frameworks are living, evolving philosophies engaged with contemporary politics. The Ecuadorian constitutional debate, the South African TRC, the Zapatista autonomous municipalities — these are 21st-century political projects, not ethnographic curiosities. Presenting them as "traditional wisdom" freezes them and denies their political agency.

**Don't:** Invoke these traditions without acknowledging the power relations that surround them.
**Why:** Buen vivir was constitutionalised in Ecuador while the Correa government expanded oil extraction in Indigenous territory. Ubuntu was invoked in the TRC while structural economic inequality remained untouched. These traditions exist within and against state power, extractive capitalism, and internal inequalities (gender, generation, class). Engaging them means engaging the politics, not just the philosophy.
