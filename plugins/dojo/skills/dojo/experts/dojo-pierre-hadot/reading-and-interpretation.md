# Reading and Interpretation

## When to Use
- When the question is how to read and interpret texts — whether ancient philosophy or modern organisational documents — as practical exercises rather than abstract theory.
- When helping practitioners engage with texts as instructions for action rather than as information to be stored.
- When the hermeneutic question matters: what did this text mean to its original audience, and what practice did it serve?

## Core Concept
Hadot argued that ancient philosophical texts have been systematically misread by modern scholars who treat them as theoretical treatises — systematic expositions of doctrine. In fact, most ancient philosophical texts were exercises addressed to specific audiences in specific situations. Marcus Aurelius's Meditations were not a philosophical treatise but a personal practice journal — notes written to himself as part of the exercise of self-examination. Epictetus's Discourses were not lectures but therapeutic conversations with students facing specific life challenges. Even Plato's dialogues are exercises in thinking, not expositions of a system.

This hermeneutic insight — that texts are exercises, not doctrines — transforms how one reads. Instead of asking "what does this text argue?" one asks "what does this text do? What exercise does it perform? What transformation does it seek in its reader?" This is reading as spiritual exercise: the text changes the reader, not merely informs them.

The same hermeneutic can be applied to modern professional documents. A programme strategy is not merely an exposition of what the programme will do — read as an exercise, it is a set of instructions for how programme staff should think, decide, and act. An evaluation report is not merely a presentation of findings — read as an exercise, it is an invitation to change practice in light of evidence.

## How to Apply
1. **Read for the exercise.** "Before asking 'what does this text argue?' ask 'what does this text ask me to do? How does it want to change how I see, think, or act?'"
2. **Attend to the audience.** "Every text was written for someone. Who? What was their situation? What exercise did the author prescribe for that situation? This context transforms interpretation."
3. **Apply to professional documents.** "Read your organisation's strategy as Hadot reads the Meditations — not as theory but as practice. What exercises does this strategy prescribe? If none, the strategy is discourse without life."
4. **Practise re-reading.** "The ancients re-read their foundational texts repeatedly — not to extract new information but to renew the exercise. What professional texts deserve re-reading as exercises rather than one-time information consumption?"

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme team has produced a comprehensive lessons-learned document from a five-year governance programme. The document is well-written and substantive. It has been shared widely. Nobody's practice has changed as a result.
**Application:** "The lessons-learned document has been read as information — a set of propositions about what worked and what did not. It has not been read as an exercise. Read as an exercise, each lesson is an instruction: 'next time, do this differently.' But the document prescribes no practice through which these instructions become habitual. Hadot would diagnose the problem precisely: the document is philosophical discourse without spiritual exercise. The lessons are stated but not practised. The remedy: take the three most important lessons and design an exercise for each. If the lesson is 'community engagement must begin earlier,' the exercise is: at every programme inception, the team spends week one in the community before writing anything. Not a recommendation — an exercise, built into the programme calendar, repeated in every new programme. If the lesson is 'M&E frameworks should be co-designed with government,' the exercise is: the first M&E workshop in every new programme includes government counterparts. The lessons-learned document becomes a rule of life — a set of exercises that the institution practises — rather than a repository of wisdom that nobody lives."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Claim that all reading should be practical.
**Why:** Theoretical reading has its place. Understanding an argument, analysing a system, criticising a position — these are legitimate intellectual activities. Hadot's point is that ancient philosophical texts were primarily practical, and that modern reading has lost this dimension. Not that all reading should be practical.

**Don't:** Ignore historical context when reading ancient texts.
**Why:** Hadot was a meticulous historian. Understanding texts as exercises requires understanding the historical situation in which they were written. Decontextualised reading produces anachronism, not insight.
