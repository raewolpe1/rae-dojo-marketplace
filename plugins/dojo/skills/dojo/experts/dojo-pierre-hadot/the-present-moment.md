# The Present Moment

## When to Use
- When a practitioner is paralysed by regret over past decisions or anxiety about future outcomes.
- When the question of what can be done now has been lost beneath speculation about what might happen or recrimination about what did happen.
- When establishing the present moment as the only site of genuine action and agency.

## Core Concept
Both Stoics and Epicureans converged on the centrality of the present moment, though from different philosophical foundations. The Stoics saw the present as the moment of moral choice — the only moment in which one can exercise virtue. The past is gone; the future is not yet; only the present is real and within one's power. Marcus Aurelius returned to this insight constantly: delimit the present, attend to what is before you now, act according to reason in this moment.

The Epicureans similarly valued the present, but for different reasons. Epicurus taught that pleasure is available only in the present — the remembered past and anticipated future are mental constructs. The Epicurean exercise of attention to present pleasure — the taste of bread, the warmth of friendship, the calm of a garden — was a practice of gratitude and sufficiency.

Hadot saw in both traditions a common therapeutic function. The unreflective mind oscillates between past and future, generating regret and anxiety that consume the energy available for present action. The spiritual exercise of attending to the present interrupts this oscillation and returns the practitioner to the only moment in which they can actually do anything.

This is not anti-planning. Strategic thinking about the future is an activity performed in the present. The exercise targets the unproductive rumination that substitutes for action — the anxiety loop that keeps the practitioner circling without acting.

## How to Apply
1. **Redirect to action.** "What can you do now? Not next quarter, not once the funding is approved — now. The present is the only moment of action."
2. **Interrupt the anxiety loop.** "You are rehearsing a future that may not arrive. Return to what is before you. What is the next concrete step?"
3. **Practise present attention.** "In your next meeting, practise attending to what is actually being said rather than preparing your response. In your next field visit, practise seeing what is actually there rather than confirming what you expected."
4. **Distinguish planning from worrying.** "Planning is a present activity with future reference — it is action. Worrying is a present activity with no productive output — it consumes energy without producing anything. Which are you doing?"

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme director has learned that a major donor is considering withdrawing funding from a flagship programme. No decision has been made, but the director has spent three weeks in escalating anxiety — drafting contingency plans, conducting scenario analyses, writing memos to senior leadership — while the programme's current operations receive diminishing attention.
**Application:** "Three weeks of anxiety about a decision that has not been made. In those three weeks, the programme you are anxious about losing has received less attention than the anxiety itself. The Stoics would identify the judgment behind the anxiety: 'if this funding is lost, the programme will fail, and my professional reputation will suffer.' Examine this judgment. Is the funding decision within your control? No. Is your response to the decision within your control? Yes. Then attend to what is within your control. What can you do now — today — for the programme? Run it excellently. The best response to a potential funding withdrawal is a programme that is evidently working. If the donor visits and finds excellent implementation, rigorous data, genuine community engagement — that is the strongest case for continued funding. If the funding is nevertheless withdrawn, an excellently run programme is easier to transition to another donor than a neglected one. Either way, present attention to the programme serves you better than three weeks of anxiety about a decision you cannot influence. Make the contingency plan — that is a present action with a productive output — and then return to the work."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss future planning as incompatible with present-moment awareness.
**Why:** Planning is a present activity. The exercise targets unproductive rumination, not strategic thinking. A practitioner who refuses to plan on philosophical grounds has misunderstood the exercise.

**Don't:** Use present-moment awareness to avoid learning from the past.
**Why:** Reflection on past experience — examination of conscience — is itself a spiritual exercise. The target is regret that paralyses, not reflection that instructs.
