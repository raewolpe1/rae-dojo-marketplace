# The Figure of the Sage

## When to Use
- When exploring what it means to have aspirational standards — ideals of excellent practice — that orient without becoming sources of paralysis or self-condemnation.
- When a practitioner is either crushed by impossible standards or lacks any standard at all.
- When the function of exemplary figures — mentors, role models, idealised practitioners — in professional formation is at stake.

## Core Concept
Every ancient philosophical school held up the figure of the sage — the wise person who has achieved the philosophical life completely. For the Stoics, the sage is the person who lives entirely according to nature and reason, free from destructive passions, perfectly virtuous. For the Epicureans, the sage is the person who has achieved ataraxia — freedom from disturbance — through the correct understanding of nature and the practice of friendship and simple pleasure.

The crucial point, as Hadot emphasised, is that the sage is a regulative ideal — a direction of aspiration, not a description of anyone who actually exists. The Stoics explicitly acknowledged that the sage may never have existed and probably never would. This did not diminish the sage's function. The sage serves as an orienting point: not "be this" but "move toward this." The practitioner is always on the way, never arrived. The philosophical life is the practice of progressing (prokoptein), not the achievement of perfection.

This understanding of the regulative ideal has profound implications for professional practice. Every profession has its implicit sage — the ideal practitioner. The danger is twofold: if the ideal is too high, it crushes practitioners with impossibility; if there is no ideal, practice has no direction. The ancient solution — the ideal as regulative, always orienting, never achieved — navigates between these dangers.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the implicit sage.** "What does the ideal practitioner look like in your field? Not the superstar but the person who embodies excellent practice? Name them — and examine what qualities they represent."
2. **Use the sage as direction, not standard.** "The sage orients: I am not this person, but I am moving toward what they represent. Today, what one thing can I do that moves me in that direction?"
3. **Acknowledge the gap without despair.** "The gap between where you are and where the sage is — that gap is not failure. It is the space of practice. Practise within it rather than collapsing under it."
4. **Create institutional sages.** "Organisations also need regulative ideals — visions of what excellent institutional practice looks like. Not benchmarks to be met but directions to be pursued."

## Examples
**Situation:** A young evaluator feels overwhelmed by the gap between the evaluators she admires — experienced, nuanced, politically astute, methodologically rigorous — and her own early-career practice, which feels clumsy and uncertain.
**Application:** "The evaluators you admire function as your sages — regulative ideals of evaluative practice. The Stoics would tell you: you are not failing because you are not them. You are practising — progressing toward the kind of evaluator they represent. The sage is not a standard to be met but a direction to be followed. Today, the exercise is not to be the ideal evaluator but to practise one element of what they represent. If you admire their ability to listen to stakeholders without prejudging, practise that today — in one interview, attend to what the person actually says rather than confirming what you expected. If you admire their methodological flexibility, practise that — in one analysis, set aside the standard template and ask what this specific data actually reveals. Each practice is a step — prokopē, progress — toward the sage. The gap between where you are and where they are is not a judgment. It is the space of your formation. Every sage was once a beginner. The difference is that they practised."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present the sage as a person who actually achieves perfection.
**Why:** The sage is regulative, not descriptive. Presenting perfection as achievable either crushes the practitioner or creates impostor syndrome when the inevitable imperfections appear.

**Don't:** Dismiss the sage as impractical because nobody achieves it.
**Why:** The fact that the ideal is never fully achieved does not diminish its practical function. The North Star is never reached; it nevertheless orients every journey.
