# Hadot Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When translating Hadot's philosophical framework into specific, daily exercises for practitioners working within development institutions.
- When designing a personal practice regime or a team formation process grounded in the ancient tradition.
- When the question is: how do I begin practising philosophy within my professional life?

## Core Concept
Applying Hadot to professional practice means establishing specific, repeated exercises that transform habitual orientation over time. Not principles to be endorsed but practices to be performed — daily, weekly, at each decision point — until they become second nature.

The exercises are drawn from across the ancient schools but adapted to institutional life. They do not require withdrawal from the institution; they require philosophical engagement within it. The practitioner who practises does not leave their organisation — they transform how they operate within it.

Five exercises form the core of Hadot-informed professional practice.

First, the morning preparation. Before the workday begins, spend five to ten minutes rehearsing: what will I face today? What situations will test my equanimity, my judgment, my integrity? How will I respond? This is Marcus Aurelius's praemeditatio — not pessimism but philosophical readiness.

Second, the attention practice. Throughout the day, interrupt the automatic flow at regular intervals — before each meeting, before each decision, before each important email — and ask: am I attending to this, or am I processing it on autopilot? This is prosoche applied to institutional life.

Third, the view from above. When overwhelmed by institutional minutiae, deliberately widen the frame: see this situation within the programme's five-year arc, within the community's history, within the larger human endeavour. Return with proportion restored.

Fourth, the evening review. At the end of each working day, review briefly: where did I practise well? Where did I fall short of my own standards? What one thing will I do differently tomorrow? This is Seneca's examination of conscience — calm, non-judgmental, practical.

Fifth, the weekly reading. Each week, read one passage from a philosophical text — not for information but as an exercise. What does this passage ask me to do? How does it illuminate my current situation? What practice does it prescribe?

## How to Apply
1. **Start with one exercise.** "Do not attempt all five simultaneously. Choose the one that addresses your most pressing need: if attention is the problem, begin with prosoche. If perspective is the problem, begin with the view from above. Practise one exercise for a month before adding another."
2. **Build community.** "Find one colleague who will practise with you — share your evening reviews, discuss your weekly readings, hold each other accountable. The philosophical schools functioned as communities of practice; individual practice alone is difficult to sustain."
3. **Connect exercise to vision.** "Each exercise serves a vision of the good practitioner. Articulate that vision: what kind of practitioner do you aspire to be? The exercises are the means; the vision is the end."
4. **Accept the difficulty.** "These exercises will feel awkward, artificial, and sometimes pointless for the first weeks. The ancients knew this: philosophical practice requires persistence before it becomes natural. Continue through the discomfort."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant who has been reading Hadot wants to establish a philosophical practice within her development consulting work but does not know where to begin. She travels frequently, works for multiple clients, and has no consistent daily routine.
**Application:** "Begin with the one exercise that travels with you: the evening review. Every evening, wherever you are — in a hotel room in Nairobi, in a guesthouse in Dhaka, at home in between assignments — spend ten minutes reviewing the day. Not a diary entry, not a to-do list — an examination: where did I attend today? Where was I on autopilot? Where did I exercise genuine judgment and where did I apply methodology mechanically? Where did I listen and where did I merely process? Write one sentence capturing what you learned. Over a month, these sentences will reveal patterns — the situations where you practise well and the situations where you fall short. From these patterns, you can identify the next exercise. If the pattern reveals that you lose attention in long institutional meetings, add the prosoche interruption: at each meeting, pause once and ask yourself what is actually being decided. If the pattern reveals that you lose perspective when overwhelmed by deliverables, add the view from above at the start of each new assignment. Build the practice gradually. The ancient schools did not produce philosophers in a week; they formed them over years. Your practice is a lifetime commitment, not a productivity hack."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present these exercises as a productivity system.
**Why:** The exercises transform the practitioner's orientation toward their work — they make the practitioner more attentive, more reflective, more honest. They may or may not make the practitioner more productive. That is not their purpose.

**Don't:** Abandon the exercises when they feel ineffective.
**Why:** Philosophical practice, like physical training, produces results slowly. The first month may feel pointless. The ancient schools warned their students about exactly this — the temptation to abandon practice before the practice has had time to work.
