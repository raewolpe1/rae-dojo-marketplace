# Hadot and the Examined Life

## When to Use
- When establishing practices of self-examination — examination of conscience, reflective practice, honest self-assessment — within professional life.
- When the Socratic dictum that the unexamined life is not worth living is relevant to the practitioner's situation.
- When connecting ancient practices of self-examination to modern concepts of reflective practice and professional development.

## Core Concept
The examination of conscience was practised across ancient philosophical schools. The Pythagoreans reviewed each evening: what did I do? What did I fail to do? What should I have done differently? The Stoics — Seneca, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius — practised similar reviews, examining their judgments, their emotional responses, and their actions against the standard of their philosophical commitments. This was not guilt-driven self-flagellation but a practical exercise in self-knowledge and continuous improvement.

Hadot emphasised that this examination was neither narcissistic nor abstract. It was tied to specific philosophical commitments and oriented toward future practice. Seneca examined his day not to wallow in failure but to identify where his practice fell short and to prepare to do better tomorrow. The examination was forward-looking — a practice of self-correction, not self-punishment.

The examined life in Hadot's interpretation has three dimensions. First, attention to one's own judgments, desires, and actions — the prosoche that monitors the inner life. Second, honest assessment against a standard — the sage, the philosophical principles, the commitments one has made. Third, practical adjustment — changing tomorrow's practice in light of today's examination. Without the third dimension, self-examination becomes self-indulgent rumination.

## How to Apply
1. **Institute the evening review.** "At the end of each working day, spend ten minutes reviewing: where did I practise well today? Where did I fall short of my own standards? What will I do differently tomorrow? This is Seneca's practice, adapted to professional life."
2. **Examine judgments, not just actions.** "Actions follow judgments. When you acted poorly, examine the judgment behind the action: what did I believe about the situation that led me to respond this way? Was that judgment accurate?"
3. **Connect to a standard.** "Self-examination without a standard is directionless. What standard do you examine yourself against? The professional code? The practitioner you aspire to be? Name it."
4. **Keep it practical.** "The examination should produce one specific adjustment for tomorrow, not a comprehensive self-critique. Seneca did not redesign his life each evening; he corrected one tendency."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme manager has been told by multiple team members that her management style is micromanaging and that staff feel unable to exercise initiative. She is surprised — she sees herself as supportive and hands-on.
**Application:** "The gap between how you see yourself and how others experience you is precisely what self-examination addresses. The Stoic exercise is to examine not your intentions but your actions and their effects. This evening, review today: in each interaction with staff, what did you actually do? Did you ask for their input or did you prescribe? Did you delegate with trust or delegate with conditions that constrained? Did you create space for initiative or fill the space with your own direction? Examine the judgment behind your actions: what do you believe about your staff's competence? If the judgment is 'they need guidance,' examine it — is it accurate for each person, or is it a generalised assumption that prevents you from seeing their capability? Tomorrow, practise one specific exercise: in the first decision that arises, ask a team member what they recommend — and then follow their recommendation unless there is a compelling reason not to. This is not abdication; it is the exercise of trust. Practise it daily for a month, reviewing each evening: where did I trust and what happened? Where did I control and why? Over time, the examination will reveal whether your micromanagement is a response to genuine staff limitations or a habit that produces the limitations it assumes."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Make self-examination a source of paralysing self-criticism.
**Why:** The ancient practitioners examined themselves to improve, not to condemn. Seneca approached his evening review with the calm of a judge, not the severity of a prosecutor. Self-examination that produces shame rather than adjustment has become pathological.

**Don't:** Practise self-examination in isolation from community.
**Why:** The ancient schools practised examination within community — philosophical friends who supported and challenged each other. Individual self-examination without external perspective risks self-deception. Build in feedback from trusted colleagues.
