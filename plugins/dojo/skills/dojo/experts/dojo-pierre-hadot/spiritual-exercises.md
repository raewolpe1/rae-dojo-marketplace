# Spiritual Exercises

## When to Use
- When identifying the specific practices that sustain transformation — in philosophical life, in professional development, or in organisational culture.
- When designing formation processes that produce lasting change rather than temporary enthusiasm.
- When the question is: what do people actually do to become different, not merely to know different things?

## Core Concept
Hadot identified a repertoire of spiritual exercises (askēsis) practised across the ancient philosophical schools. These were not theoretical but practical — repeated, intentional activities designed to transform the practitioner's habitual orientation. The major exercises include: attention to the present moment (prosoche), the view from above (seeing one's situation within the cosmic whole), examination of conscience (reviewing one's actions and judgments), meditation on death (memento mori), premeditation of adversity (praemeditatio malorum), reading and study as practice, physical discipline, and dialogue with others.

What makes these spiritual exercises — as opposed to mere techniques — is their relationship to a comprehensive way of life. Each exercise is embedded within a philosophical school's vision of the good: the Stoic exercises serve the Stoic understanding of nature and virtue; the Epicurean exercises serve the Epicurean understanding of pleasure and friendship. Divorced from this larger vision, the exercises become self-help techniques — useful perhaps, but stripped of the philosophical depth that gives them transformative power.

The exercises share a common structure: they interrupt the habitual, unreflective flow of life and redirect attention toward what matters. They are practices of dis-identification — stepping back from one's immediate reactions, desires, and anxieties to see them within a larger frame. This interruption is the beginning of philosophical life.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the exercise.** "For any practice you want to sustain — adaptive management, reflective evaluation, participatory governance — name the specific exercise. What does the practitioner do, how often, in what form?"
2. **Embed the exercise in a vision.** "An exercise without a vision is a technique. What vision of good practice does this exercise serve? The exercise of weekly reflection serves the vision of learning from experience. Name the connection."
3. **Design for repetition.** "Exercises work through repetition, not through one-off events. Design the exercise to be repeated — daily, weekly, at every decision point — until it becomes habitual."
4. **Distinguish exercise from performance.** "An exercise transforms the practitioner. A performance demonstrates competence to others. Most institutional practices are performances. Redesign them as exercises."

## Examples
**Situation:** A monitoring and evaluation unit wants to move from compliance-oriented reporting to genuine learning. They have read about developmental evaluation and utilisation-focused evaluation. They understand the theory. They continue to produce compliance reports.
**Application:** "The M&E unit needs exercises, not more theory. Three exercises, practised regularly, could begin the transformation. First, the weekly question: every Friday, each M&E officer spends thirty minutes writing an answer to one question — 'what did I learn this week that should change how this programme operates?' Not a report, not a log — a genuine reflection on one insight. Second, the utilisation check: before producing any report, the officer asks one intended user: 'what question do you need answered?' and designs the analysis to answer that question. This is an exercise in attention — attending to the user rather than to the template. Third, the monthly dialogue: the M&E team meets not to review outputs but to discuss what they are learning — what patterns they see across programmes, what surprises them, what they do not understand. This is the ancient exercise of philosophical dialogue, adapted to institutional practice. Each exercise is specific, repeatable, and oriented toward the vision of M&E as learning. Without such exercises, developmental evaluation remains a discourse — an aspiration that everyone endorses and nobody practises."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present exercises as quick fixes or productivity hacks.
**Why:** Spiritual exercises are not techniques for optimisation. They are practices of self-transformation embedded in a vision of the good life. Reducing them to hacks strips them of their philosophical depth and their transformative power.

**Don't:** Prescribe exercises without explaining what vision of life they serve.
**Why:** An exercise divorced from its philosophical context becomes arbitrary. The practitioner needs to understand not just what to do but why — what way of life the exercise sustains and what kind of person it forms.
