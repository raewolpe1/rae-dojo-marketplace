# Hadot and Stoicism

## When to Use
- When applying Stoic philosophy — specifically, Hadot's interpretation of Stoicism as a way of life — to professional challenges.
- When the Stoic discipline of distinguishing what is within one's control from what is not provides the relevant framework.
- When building genuine resilience through philosophical practice rather than through motivational slogans.

## Core Concept
Hadot's interpretation of Stoicism recovered the tradition as a complete way of life, not merely a set of ethical maxims. For the Stoics, philosophy comprised three inseparable parts: physics (understanding nature), logic (disciplining thought), and ethics (living well). These were not academic subjects but dimensions of a single practice. The Stoic understood nature in order to live according to it, disciplined thought in order to judge correctly, and acted virtuously because virtue was the natural expression of a rational being aligned with the cosmic order.

The Stoic spiritual exercises follow from this comprehensive vision. The discipline of assent (not accepting impressions uncritically), the discipline of desire (wanting only what is within one's control), and the discipline of impulse (acting rationally and for the common good) — these are daily practices, not theoretical commitments. Epictetus's fundamental distinction — between what is "up to us" (our judgments, desires, and impulses) and what is "not up to us" (everything external) — is not a doctrine to be believed but an exercise to be practised moment by moment.

Hadot insisted that this Stoicism is radically different from the popular caricature of emotional suppression. The Stoic does not suppress emotion; the Stoic examines the judgments that produce emotion and corrects false judgments. If I am anxious about a funding decision, the Stoic exercise asks: what judgment produces this anxiety? The judgment that the funding decision is within my control and that my wellbeing depends on its outcome. Correcting these judgments — the decision is not within my control; my wellbeing depends on my virtue, not on external outcomes — does not suppress anxiety but dissolves its cognitive foundation.

## How to Apply
1. **Practise the discipline of assent.** "Before accepting your initial reaction to any situation, pause. Is your impression accurate? Are you adding judgments to facts? The fact is: the donor has questioned the methodology. The added judgment — 'they are undermining us' — may or may not be accurate. Separate fact from judgment."
2. **Apply the control distinction.** "What in this situation is within your control? Your effort, your integrity, your preparation. What is not? The donor's decision, the government's response, the outcome. Direct energy toward what you control."
3. **Practise for the common good.** "Stoic ethics is not individualistic. The discipline of impulse requires acting for the common good — the community, the team, the programme's participants. Self-interest is natural but must be governed by reason and directed toward the common."
4. **Connect to nature.** "Stoic resilience is not willpower; it is alignment with reality. The practitioner who accepts what cannot be changed and acts on what can is not resigned but realistic — and realism is the foundation of effective action."

## Examples
**Situation:** A team leader has invested six months preparing a programme proposal that has just been rejected by the donor. The team is demoralised. The team leader feels personally responsible and is considering whether development work is worth continuing.
**Application:** "The Stoic exercise begins with the discipline of assent. Examine your impressions. The fact: the proposal was rejected. The judgments you are adding: 'the work was wasted,' 'I failed my team,' 'development work is futile.' Are these judgments accurate? The work was not wasted — the analysis, the relationships built, the understanding gained remain. You did not fail your team — you led a competent effort toward an uncertain outcome. Development work is not futile — one rejection does not negate the field. Now apply the control distinction. The donor's decision was not within your control. Your preparation, your effort, your integrity — these were, and they were exercised well. Your demoralisation comes from treating the outcome as though it were within your control and its failure as though it were your failure. It was neither. The Stoic exercise for the team: examine what was within your control and what was not. Identify what you learned that strengthens the next proposal. Direct energy toward the next action rather than toward rumination on the past. This is not positive thinking — it is rigorous cognitive discipline applied to a specific situation."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce Stoicism to "don't worry about what you can't control."
**Why:** The Stoic framework is comprehensive: physics, logic, ethics. The control distinction is one exercise within a complete way of life. Isolated from its philosophical context, it becomes a platitude.

**Don't:** Present Stoicism as emotional suppression.
**Why:** Hadot was emphatic that Stoic exercises do not suppress emotions — they examine and correct the false judgments that produce destructive emotions. Joy, love, and appropriate concern are central to Stoic ethics.
