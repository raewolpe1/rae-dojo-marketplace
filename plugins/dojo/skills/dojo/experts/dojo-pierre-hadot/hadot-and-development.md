# Hadot and Development

## When to Use
- When examining why the development industry's massive investment in training, capacity building, and knowledge management produces so little lasting change in practice.
- When diagnosing the gap between what practitioners know and how they act — the discourse-life gap applied to development.
- When considering what genuine formation — not merely information transfer — would require.

## Core Concept
Hadot did not write about development. But his analysis of the separation between philosophical discourse and the philosophical life describes the development industry's central learning paradox with striking accuracy.

The development industry invests enormously in knowledge: training workshops, technical assistance, knowledge management platforms, communities of practice, lessons-learned databases, best-practice guides. The quantity of development knowledge is unprecedented. Yet evaluations consistently find that practice changes slowly, that lessons are rarely applied, that training produces short-term enthusiasm and long-term stagnation. The industry has abundant discourse and minimal transformation.

Hadot's framework diagnoses this precisely: the industry provides information without formation. Training communicates knowledge without prescribing the exercises that would transform knowledge into habitual practice. Lessons-learned documents state what should be done differently without establishing the daily practices that would make the doing habitual. Communities of practice share information without functioning as philosophical schools — communities of practitioners who hold each other accountable to a shared standard of practice.

The fundamental error is treating knowledge as sufficient for change. The ancient schools knew better: knowing what virtue requires does not make one virtuous. Only the practice of specific exercises — daily, repeated, embedded in community — transforms knowledge into character. Development's capacity building provides the knowledge of what good practice looks like. It does not provide the askēsis that would make good practice habitual.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose the discourse-life gap.** "In this organisation or programme, what is the gap between what people know they should do and what they actually do? Name the gap specifically — not as 'implementation failure' but as 'discourse without exercise.'"
2. **Replace training with formation.** "Training transfers information. Formation transforms the practitioner. What would formation look like in this context? Not a five-day workshop but a year-long practice: weekly exercises, regular examination, community accountability."
3. **Design exercises, not curricula.** "For every competency the programme needs to build, design a specific exercise — a repeated, intentional practice — rather than a training module. If the competency is adaptive management, the exercise might be: every Friday, each team writes one adjustment they made this week and why."
4. **Build schools, not training programmes.** "The ancient philosophical school was a community of practitioners who lived the philosophy together. What would a 'school' look like in this programme context? A group of practitioners who meet regularly, practise together, hold each other accountable, and share a vision of excellent practice."

## Examples
**Situation:** A government ministry has received extensive technical assistance in results-based management over five years. Staff have attended multiple trainings, study tours, and coaching sessions. The ministry's planning documents now use results-based language fluently. But actual management practice remains input-focused — budgets are allocated by activity, monitoring tracks expenditure rather than results, and decision-making is driven by political considerations rather than evidence.
**Application:** "Five years of technical assistance have produced discourse without practice. The ministry speaks results-based management fluently — the discourse is excellent. But nobody practises it. There are no exercises. No one reviews results data weekly and asks: are we achieving what we intended? No one examines their management decisions monthly and asks: which of these were evidence-based and which were political? No one practises the discipline of adjusting plans based on monitoring data — because the monitoring data arrives too late to inform decisions and is designed for upward reporting rather than management use. Hadot would prescribe: stop providing more training. Start prescribing exercises. The simplest: every Monday, each department head reviews one indicator and answers one question — is this moving in the right direction? If not, what will I do this week to change it? This is the examination of conscience applied to management. Over months, practised weekly, it transforms the orientation from input-based to results-based — not through knowledge (which the ministry already has) but through practice (which it does not). The technical assistance should function as a philosophical school — not delivering knowledge but supervising practice."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Conclude that all training is useless.
**Why:** Information is necessary — practitioners need to know what good practice looks like. But information is not sufficient. The point is not to abandon training but to supplement it with exercises that make the training habitual.

**Don't:** Ignore structural constraints.
**Why:** Hadot's framework addresses the discourse-life gap at the level of practice. But structural constraints — political incentives, resource limitations, institutional cultures — also prevent knowledge from translating into action. Formation is necessary but not sufficient; structural conditions must also be addressed.
