# Adorno and Science

## When to Use
- When evaluation methodologies claim objectivity while embedding specific assumptions about what counts as evidence, what constitutes rigour, and what questions deserve investigation.
- When the choice of method determines the findings — when what is measured shapes what is known — and the method is treated as a neutral tool.
- When debates about qualitative versus quantitative methods, or about the validity of different evidence types, reach an impasse.

## Core Concept
The positivism dispute (Positivismusstreit) in German sociology, in which Adorno and Karl Popper were central figures, was not merely a methodological disagreement. It was a confrontation over the relationship between knowledge and the society that produces it. Adorno's position was that the positivist demand for value-free social science — the insistence that social inquiry should emulate the methods of natural science, producing objective, verifiable, generalizable knowledge — is itself a value position that serves the existing social order. By refusing to examine the conditions that produce the inquirer, the assumptions built into the method, and the interests served by the questions asked, value-free social science presents the existing order as natural rather than historical, as given rather than produced.

The critique is not that empirical methods are wrong but that they are insufficient and not self-aware. The survey that measures attitudes does not examine the social conditions that produced those attitudes. The randomised controlled trial that isolates a causal effect does not examine the system within which that effect occurs. The cost-benefit analysis that calculates efficiency does not question the values embedded in the calculation. Each method captures a dimension of reality while making other dimensions invisible — and then presents its capture as the whole truth.

For M&E practice, this means that methodology is never merely technical. The choice between RCTs and participatory evaluation, between quantitative indicators and qualitative narratives, between theory-based evaluation and experimental design — these are not neutral choices. Each embeds assumptions about what counts as knowledge, whose perspective matters, and what the evaluation is for. The practitioner who chooses a method without reflecting on what the method includes and excludes, whose interests it serves and whose it marginalises, has made a political decision while believing they have made a technical one.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask what the method assumes.** "Every method assumes a relationship between the researcher and the researched, between the question and the answer, between the evidence and the conclusion. Name these assumptions. They are not neutral."
2. **Examine the excluded questions.** "The method determines not only how questions are answered but which questions are asked. What questions does your methodology make it impossible to ask? Those excluded questions may be the important ones."
3. **Refuse the neutrality claim.** "When a methodology is presented as objective, value-free, or scientifically rigorous, ask: objective from whose perspective? Free of whose values? Rigorous by whose standards? The claim of neutrality is itself a position — one that serves the interests it refuses to examine."
4. **Include reflexivity.** "The evaluation that does not examine its own conditions of production — who commissioned it, what interests it serves, what assumptions it carries, what questions it cannot ask — has already falsified its object by presenting a positioned perspective as a neutral one."

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor commissions a randomised controlled trial to evaluate the impact of a livelihood programme in a rural province. The evaluation finds statistically significant but modest effects on household income.
**Application:** "The RCT has produced a specific kind of knowledge — the average treatment effect on a measurable outcome within a controlled design. This knowledge is real, but it is radically incomplete, and the methodology presents its incompleteness as completeness. The trial cannot ask why some households benefited more than others in ways that require understanding their specific histories and circumstances. It cannot examine the structural conditions — land tenure, market access, political economy — within which the livelihood programme operates. It cannot capture the effects the programme had that were not measured — changes in dignity, in hope, in community relationships, in the capacity to imagine a different future. The modest effect size may reflect not the programme's inadequacy but the methodology's incapacity to see what the programme actually did. The donor, however, will read the modest effect size as evidence that the programme is not 'working' — because the RCT has defined 'working' as producing statistically significant changes in household income, and this definition is treated as objective rather than as one choice among many. The methodology has not neutrally evaluated the programme. It has defined what evaluation means in terms that may be inadequate to what the programme does."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use the critique of positivism to reject evidence, data, or empirical research.
**Why:** Adorno's position is not anti-empirical. It is anti-positivist — opposed to the claim that empirical methods exhaust what can be known. The critique demands that empirical methods be supplemented with critical reflection on their conditions, assumptions, and limits, not that they be abandoned.

**Don't:** Treat the critique of value-free social science as a licence for politically motivated research.
**Why:** The critique does not argue that research should be value-laden in the sense of producing predetermined conclusions. It argues that the pretence of value-freedom conceals the values already embedded in the methodology. Honest research acknowledges its values rather than either denying them or imposing them.
