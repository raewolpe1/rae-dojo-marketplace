# Identity Thinking and Its Critique

## When to Use
- When programme design requires standardised categories — beneficiary types, vulnerability levels, intervention modalities — and the question arises whether these categories adequately represent the people they classify.
- When a framework, typology, or classification system is being applied and the practitioner senses that significant dimensions of reality are being lost in the application.
- When the gap between the category and the categorised is producing distortions in programme design or evaluation.

## Core Concept
Identity thinking (Identitatsdenken) is Adorno's term for the foundational operation of Western conceptual thought: the assumption that the concept is identical to the object, that the category captures the thing, that the universal subsumes the particular without remainder. When we classify, measure, or categorise, we assume — usually without reflection — that the classification captures what matters about the classified object. The child is "stunted." The household is "food insecure." The community is "vulnerable." The programme is "effective." Identity thinking treats these classifications as adequate representations of reality.

Adorno's critique is not that classifications are inaccurate — they may be perfectly accurate on their own terms. The critique is that they claim an adequacy they cannot possess. Every classification includes by excluding. The child classified as "stunted" is stunted — but they are also everything the category "stunted" cannot contain. The household classified as "food insecure" may be food insecure — but the category erases the specific history, relationships, strategies, and meanings that constitute this household's particular experience of hunger. The violence of identity thinking is not inaccuracy. It is the claim of adequacy — the presentation of a partial capture as a complete representation.

For development practice, identity thinking is not an occasional error. It is the structural logic of programme design. Programmes require categories — beneficiary classifications, vulnerability assessments, intervention typologies. These categories must treat different people, communities, and situations as instances of the same type. The particular must be subsumed under the universal for the programme to function at scale. Adorno's critique does not require abandoning this logic — it requires acknowledging what it costs. Every time a community is classified, something about that community is consumed by the classification. The consumed dimension — the nonidentical — does not disappear. It persists as the gap between the programme's understanding and the community's reality, between the indicator's report and the lived experience, between the category and the categorised.

## How to Apply
1. **Audit the categories.** "Take each category your programme uses — beneficiary type, vulnerability level, intervention modality — and ask: what does this category exclude? What dimension of this person's or community's reality cannot survive the classification process?"
2. **Name the cost.** "Do not merely note that categories are imperfect. Specify what each category costs. The category 'female-headed household' captures a demographic fact but may erase the specific strength, strategy, and meaning-making of the household it classifies. Name the erasure."
3. **Resist the aggregation.** "When individual cases are aggregated into categories for reporting, something is destroyed in the aggregation. The aggregate is not a summary of the individuals — it is a replacement. Can you hold, alongside the aggregate, specific cases that show what the aggregate conceals?"
4. **Use categories self-consciously.** "You will use categories — the institutional conditions require it. But you can use them knowing what they do. A category used self-consciously — with awareness of what it excludes — is less violent than a category used as though it captures reality without remainder."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme targeting "youth at risk" in Cape Town classifies young people using a risk assessment tool that scores factors including school dropout, substance use, criminal justice contact, and household poverty. The classification determines the intervention each young person receives.
**Application:** "The risk assessment tool performs identity thinking with particular precision. It reduces each young person — with their specific history, relationships, aspirations, fears, talents, and ways of being in the world — to a numerical score that places them in a category. The category determines the intervention. The young person who scores high on criminal justice contact and low on substance use receives a different programme from the one who scores the reverse. But neither programme is designed for this young person. Both are designed for the category. The individual — with their irreducible particularity — has been consumed by the classification and replaced by a type. The programme then intervenes on the type, not the person. What the risk assessment cannot capture — why this young person dropped out (perhaps to care for a sibling), what their criminal justice contact actually involved (perhaps being in the wrong place), what their actual needs are (perhaps an adult who listens) — is not merely unrecorded. It is structurally invisible. The assessment tool was not designed to see it. The programme was not designed to respond to it. The young person learns to present themselves in the terms the assessment can process, and the gap between who they are and how they are classified becomes one more dimension of the damage."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reject all categorisation as inherently violent and refuse to classify.
**Why:** This is practically impossible and theoretically naive. The critique of identity thinking does not propose an alternative form of thought that would capture reality without remainder. It proposes that thought acknowledge what it costs. Categories are necessary. But they should be used with awareness of their violence, not with the assumption of their adequacy.

**Don't:** Treat the critique of identity thinking as a call for "more nuanced" categories.
**Why:** More nuanced categories are still categories. They still subsume the particular under the universal. The critique is not that the categories are insufficiently fine-grained — it is that the operation of categorisation as such involves a structural loss. Better categories reduce the loss but do not eliminate it.
