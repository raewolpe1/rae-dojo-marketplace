# Heterodox Economics

## When to Use
- When mainstream economic methodology is producing bad policy advice and the methodology itself needs to be put on the table.
- When introducing the user to the existence of multiple legitimate schools of economic thought.
- When responding to claims that "economics" has settled the question, where "economics" means "the neoclassical mainstream."
- When defending the use of historical, institutional, or political-economy analysis against the charge that it is "not economics."

## Fails When
- **You reduce the argument to school identity.** "I'm heterodox, you're neoclassical, end of debate" is not engagement.
- **You straw-man neoclassical economics.** It has its strengths; address its strongest version, not its weakest.
- **The audience is already familiar with the heterodox tradition** — shorthand will do.

## Core Concept

Economics is not one discipline with one methodology and one set of conclusions. It is a contested terrain with multiple traditions — classical, neoclassical, Keynesian, Marxist, Austrian, institutionalist, evolutionary, behavioural, feminist, ecological, post-Keynesian, structuralist. Each has different assumptions about human behaviour, institutional context, dynamics, and what counts as evidence. Each can be more or less useful for particular questions. Treating economics as if "economics" means "neoclassical microfoundations" is to mistake one tradition for the field.

Heterodox economics is the umbrella term for the non-neoclassical traditions. It is heterogeneous: post-Keynesians, institutionalists (in the Veblen-Commons-Galbraith tradition, not the property-rights tradition), Marxists, structuralists, ecological economists, feminist economists. What unites them is not a positive agreement but a shared rejection of certain neoclassical commitments — the methodological individualism, the assumption of optimising rationality, the treatment of institutions as exogenous, the focus on equilibrium analysis, the formalism that requires abstraction from history and politics.

The case for heterodox economics is partly methodological and partly substantive.

**Methodological.** Different economic questions require different methods. Forecasting next quarter's inflation may be well-served by macroeconomic modelling that abstracts from institutional detail. Understanding why Korea succeeded and Argentina did not requires historical, institutional, and political-economy analysis that the standard macroeconomic toolkit cannot deliver. Insisting on a uniform method across all questions is methodological monism that wastes available analytical resources. Heterodox economics is, in the first instance, a defence of methodological pluralism.

**Substantive.** Many of the most important questions in development economics — what role does the state play in successful catch-up? what causes productive capability to accumulate? how do trade regimes affect industrial structure? — have been better answered by heterodox traditions than by the neoclassical mainstream. The historical record on industrial policy, the role of institutions, and the dynamics of catch-up is more accurately described by the heterodox literature. The randomista turn (Banerjee-Duflo-Kremer) within mainstream development economics has produced rigorous answers to important micro-questions while leaving the structural questions unanswered.

The relevant heterodox traditions for development questions include:

**Institutionalist / evolutionary.** From Veblen and Commons through Schumpeter, Nelson, Winter, and Dosi: focus on technological change, institutional evolution, path dependence, learning, and innovation systems. The intellectual home for industrial policy analysis and capability accumulation.

**Structuralist development economics.** The ECLA tradition (Prebisch, Furtado, Sunkel), the Indian planning tradition (Mahalanobis), the African structuralists. Focus on the centre-periphery dynamics of the world economy, the developmental role of the state, the structural transformation of poor economies.

**Post-Keynesian.** Robinson, Kaldor, Minsky. Focus on effective demand, financial instability, distributional dynamics. The intellectual home for macroeconomic critique of neoliberal stabilisation packages.

**Friedrich List and the German Historical School.** The 19th-century institutionalist-historical tradition that provided the intellectual framework for the developmental state.

**Schumpeter and innovation economics.** Creative destruction, the entrepreneurial firm, the role of credit in innovation. Used by Mazzucato, Pérez, and others to analyse mission-oriented industrial policy.

The use of heterodox traditions is not a partisan commitment; it is methodological seriousness. Use the tradition that best illuminates the question. Most development questions are better served by historical-institutional analysis than by neoclassical formalism. The reverse can be true for narrowly defined questions about market microstructure or specific allocative efficiency. The pragmatic position is methodological pluralism.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the methodological assumptions in the policy debate.** What is being assumed about rationality, institutions, dynamics, history? Often the assumptions are hidden in the mathematical formalism.
2. **Match the methodology to the question.** Long-run development questions need historical-institutional analysis. Short-run macro questions may use mainstream tools. Sector-specific industrial policy needs the evolutionary-institutional tradition.
3. **Surface the political content.** Neoclassical economics smuggles in political assumptions (market efficiency, the priority of property rights, the universality of preferences). Make these explicit and contestable.
4. **Use multiple traditions on the same question.** Triangulation across schools produces more robust analysis than monomethod work.
5. **Engage with the strongest mainstream version.** Heterodox arguments are stronger when they engage seriously with the best mainstream work, not the worst.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African policy debate is being conducted entirely in the framework of standard macroeconomic stabilisation — inflation targeting, fiscal consolidation, exchange rate flexibility. The structural questions about manufacturing, employment, and industrial transformation are being treated as second-order.

**Application:** The framework is constraining the conversation. The methodological assumptions of standard macroeconomics — that the economy will return to a natural equilibrium once macroeconomic distortions are removed, that supply-side responses follow automatically from stable prices — are precisely the assumptions that have failed repeatedly in middle-income countries. A heterodox framing brings the structural questions back into the centre. The relevant traditions for South Africa: post-Keynesian macro (the limits of stabilisation without demand and industrial policy), structuralist development economics (the centre-periphery dynamics constraining manufacturing-led growth), evolutionary economics (the capability accumulation logic), Friedrich List (the historical record of catch-up).

**Result:** A debate that has been narrowed to fiscal-monetary technicalities is reopened to questions about productive structure, employment, and capability accumulation. The methodological move is the political move.

**Situation:** A World Bank country diagnostic argues, on standard cross-country regression evidence, that the country's growth slowdown reflects institutional quality and policy "distortions."

**Application:** Cross-country regression as methodology is well-known to be fragile — sensitive to specification, controls, sample selection, and the operationalisation of "institutions." The substantive claims rest on a methodology that mainstream econometricians themselves treat with caution. Heterodox engagement with the same question through historical case-study analysis (what did this country actually do? what worked and what didn't?) typically produces different and more useful conclusions.

**Result:** The diagnostic's methodological foundations are exposed as weaker than they appear. The country can engage with the substantive claims rather than treating them as settled science.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use heterodox economics as a tribal identity rather than as a methodological resource.
**Why:** The point is better analysis, not team membership. Heterodox traditions are useful where they illuminate questions better than mainstream ones. Use them for that purpose.

**Don't:** Reject the entire mainstream toolkit.
**Why:** Parts of it are useful for the questions they are designed for. Be selective.

**Don't:** Imagine heterodox traditions agree with each other on positive content.
**Why:** They don't. Marxists and Schumpeterians have substantial disagreements. Post-Keynesians and institutionalists differ. The shared ground is the rejection of certain neoclassical assumptions; the positive content varies.
