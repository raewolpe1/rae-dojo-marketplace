# The Role of Institutions in Development

## When to Use
- When the "good governance" agenda is being presented as a precondition for development.
- When responding to the "institutions cause development" thesis as developed by Acemoglu, Robinson, and the property-rights school.
- When designing institutional reform sequences in developing countries.
- When evaluating donor programmes premised on institutional improvement as the development lever.

## Fails When
- **You overstate the case to deny that institutions matter at all.** They do. The question is which institutions, defined how, in what sequence.
- **You romanticise corruption or weak institutions.** The historical observation that developed countries were corrupt during their take-off does not mean corruption is good or harmless. It means the causal sequence is more complex than the orthodoxy claims.
- **You misrepresent Acemoglu and Robinson.** Their academic work is more nuanced than the policy-derivative "good governance" agenda. Engage with the actual claim.

## Core Concept

Institutions matter for development. The serious question is: which institutions, defined how, in what causal sequence with development itself?

The orthodox "good governance" agenda — promoted by the World Bank, IMF, OECD, and the major donor community — defines institutions narrowly as: strong property rights, contract enforcement, anti-corruption, transparency, the rule of law, and (often) democratic accountability. It then argues that these institutions are *causally prior* to development: countries develop because they have these institutions; countries that lack them cannot develop. The policy implication is that institutional reform should precede or accompany other development efforts, and that donors should condition support on institutional improvement.

This thesis has problems both historical and analytical.

**Historical problem.** Today's rich countries did not have sophisticated versions of these institutions during their take-off periods. Britain in the early 19th century had pervasive corruption, restricted franchise, weak property rights enforcement against the powerful, child labour, and slave trade involvement. The United States in the late 19th century had robber-baron capitalism, machine politics, judicial bias toward the rich, racial exclusion from property and contract rights, and rampant securities fraud. Korea under Park Chung-hee (the period of fastest catch-up) was authoritarian, with weak property rights protection against the state, government-directed credit, and limited transparency. Sophisticated institutions emerged *during and after* development; they were not preconditions for it.

**Analytical problem.** The "institutions cause development" claim suffers from severe endogeneity. The same political-economic processes that produce development also produce institutional improvement. Demand for sophisticated institutions emerges as economies become more complex; the resources to fund institutional development emerge from the productivity gains of catch-up. Treating institutions as the exogenous cause of development is a category error.

**Definitional problem.** "Institutions" in the orthodox agenda are defined narrowly, in ways that smuggle in particular policy preferences. Strong property rights enforcement protects existing property holders; it does not address whether the distribution of property is just or developmental. Anti-corruption is selectively applied (Western firms paying bribes to African officials is corruption; agricultural subsidies that distort global markets are not). The "good governance" framing functions partly as a way to recommend Anglo-American institutions as the universal template.

**Sequencing problem.** Even where the orthodox institutions are genuinely desirable as a destination, demanding them as a precondition for development creates an impossible bar. Poor countries lack the resources, technical capacity, and political coalitions to implement sophisticated institutional reform before they have developed; demanding they do so anyway is to demand they remain poor.

The correct framing is that development and institutional improvement co-evolve. There are minimal institutional preconditions — a functioning state with monopoly on legitimate force, basic legal predictability, some enforceable contracts — without which nothing can happen. Above this minimum, institutional sophistication grows as the economy develops. Industrial policy and capability building drive development; institutional improvement follows and supports that development; institutional perfection is the destination, not the journey.

Acemoglu and Robinson's *Why Nations Fail* is a more nuanced argument than the policy-derivative good-governance agenda, but it has its own problems. The "inclusive versus extractive institutions" dichotomy struggles to handle the East Asian developmental states (which were extractive on the orthodox criteria but produced sustained development); the historical analysis is often retrospectively selective; and the policy implications are insufficiently distinct from the orthodox agenda to justify the analytical investment.

## How to Apply

1. **Distinguish minimal institutional requirements from sophisticated institutional architectures.** The minima (functional state, basic legal predictability, enforceable contracts above some baseline) are real preconditions. Sophisticated property rights, anti-corruption infrastructure, and Western-style transparency are destinations, not preconditions.
2. **Identify the institutions actually needed for the development strategy being pursued.** If the strategy is industrial policy through targeted protection and state-led investment, the relevant institutions are technocratic ministries with industry expertise and political backing — not the World Bank governance index.
3. **Sequence institutional reform alongside development, not before it.** Build state capacity in the areas that the development strategy actually exercises. Improve courts and contract enforcement as commercial activity grows. Strengthen anti-corruption where commerce and state interact directly.
4. **Resist the use of institutional conditionality to delay or block development support.** When donors or external partners attach institutional preconditions, ask whether the preconditions are actually causal for the desired outcome, or are policy preferences smuggled in as technical requirements.

## Examples

**Situation:** Donor-funded review of an African country's governance recommends suspending budget support until anti-corruption and property rights reforms reach a specified threshold.

**Application:** The conditionality assumes that the institutional reforms are causally prior to the outcomes the budget support is meant to enable. The historical record does not support that assumption. The conditionality has the practical effect of withdrawing development support from a country that has not completed institutional reforms — which it cannot complete without the development the support is meant to enable. This is the institutional version of kicking away the ladder.

**Result:** Engage the donors on which specific institutional improvements are causally necessary for the specific outcomes being funded — not on a generic institutional improvement programme.

**Situation:** South African policy debate frames state capture, weakness at SOEs, and procurement corruption as the reason industrial policy "cannot work" in the current institutional environment.

**Application:** The framing is partly right and partly self-defeating. State capture and procurement corruption are real problems that have undermined specific industrial policy efforts. But the response cannot be to defer industrial policy until institutions are fixed; the institutional weaknesses are partly themselves consequences of the absence of a coherent developmental project. Building capable, insulated technical institutions in the areas the industrial strategy actually exercises (the IDC, the dtic, specific SEZ administrations, sector-specific procurement frameworks) is the way to build institutional capability — not waiting until generic governance scores improve.

**Result:** A practical sequencing: build the institutional architecture for the industrial strategy in parallel with rolling out the strategy, with specific institutional investments tied to specific strategic priorities. Resist the framing that all institutional reform must precede all developmental action.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use the argument to deny that institutions matter.
**Why:** They do, in specific ways, for specific purposes. The argument is about definition, sequencing, and the use of "institutions" as a policy stick — not about whether the underlying concept has any validity.

**Don't:** Argue that countries can have any institutions and still develop.
**Why:** There are minima. A country with no functioning state, no basic legal predictability, no enforceable contracts, will not develop. The minima matter; the sophistication of the additional architecture follows development rather than preceding it.

**Don't:** Misrepresent the academic literature to score a polemical point.
**Why:** Acemoglu and Robinson, North, and others are serious scholars whose work deserves serious engagement. The polemical target is the policy-derivative "good governance" agenda, not the academic institutionalist tradition as such.
