# Critique of Neoliberal Globalisation

## When to Use
- When evaluating IMF, World Bank, or major bilateral donor policy prescriptions.
- When the Washington Consensus (or its successors — the "post-Washington Consensus," the "good governance" agenda) is being invoked as settled policy wisdom.
- When debating the terms and obligations of WTO membership or bilateral/regional trade agreements.
- When reviewing structural adjustment programmes or their contemporary successors (IMF country programmes, World Bank Country Partnership Frameworks).

## Fails When
- **You reduce the critique to anti-globalisation rhetoric.** Chang is not anti-globalisation; he is against a specific configuration of globalisation that systematically disadvantages developing countries. The critique is configurational, not categorical.
- **You ignore legitimate elements in the orthodox framework.** Macroeconomic stabilisation, basic fiscal discipline, transparency in some forms — these are not Washington Consensus excesses; they are general policy hygiene.
- **The question is about technical policy detail** within an agreed framework, where the broad framing critique is not the productive contribution.

## Core Concept

The Washington Consensus — the term coined by John Williamson in 1989 to describe the convergent policy advice of the IMF, World Bank, and US Treasury — set out ten policy prescriptions: fiscal discipline, public expenditure reorientation, tax reform, financial liberalisation, exchange rate competitiveness, trade liberalisation, FDI liberalisation, privatisation, deregulation, and property rights. Through the 1980s and 1990s, this framework was implemented across most of Latin America, Africa, the post-Communist transition economies, and parts of Asia through structural adjustment programmes and bilateral aid conditionality.

The empirical record of Washington Consensus implementation is poor. Latin American growth rates in the 1990s were lower than in the pre-Consensus 1970s and 1980s. Sub-Saharan African growth stagnated for two decades. The Russian economic transition collapsed output by 40 per cent. Indonesia and Thailand were precipitated into the Asian financial crisis partly through premature capital account liberalisation. The successful catch-up cases of the period — China, Vietnam, Korea (which had largely completed its catch-up before being subjected to the Consensus during the 1997 crisis), India (which liberalised on its own terms, slowly) — did not follow the Consensus template.

The intellectual response to the failure has been the "post-Washington Consensus" or the "good governance" agenda — supplementing the original prescriptions with institutional improvement, social protection, and country ownership rhetoric. The new framework retains the core policy commitments while softening their implementation. The Chang critique applies to both the original and the successor framework.

The **Bad Samaritans** thesis (Chang, 2007) identifies the structure of the harm. Rich countries, having developed through protection and active state action, now press developing countries to adopt policies that the rich countries did not adopt during their own development. The Samaritans are bad not because their advice is malicious but because it is hypocritical and historically illiterate — they are recommending policies that obstruct the catch-up they claim to want to support. The book applies the kicking-away-the-ladder framework across trade policy, foreign investment, intellectual property, privatisation, fiscal policy, and central bank independence.

The core failure modes of the neoliberal globalisation framework:

**Premature liberalisation.** Trade and capital account liberalisation is pushed before countries have built the capabilities to compete or the institutions to manage volatility. The result is deindustrialisation, financial instability, and crisis vulnerability — the precise opposite of what was promised.

**Universalist prescriptions.** Different countries with different histories, structures, and challenges are offered the same policy package. The package reflects the policy preferences of dominant shareholder countries, not country-specific analysis.

**Time inconsistency.** Policies recommended for developing countries differ from policies practised by developed countries. The pattern is consistent — the policies developed countries are currently practising are not on the menu offered to developing countries.

**Capture of conditionality.** Conditionality has been used to lock in policy preferences favourable to capital-exporting countries (capital account openness, IP protection, FDI rights) and to insulate them from democratic challenge in the recipient country.

**Failure to learn from failure.** The IMF and World Bank have repeatedly issued mea culpas about specific policy failures (capital account liberalisation timing, fiscal contraction in crisis, austerity in the Eurozone) while continuing to apply the same framework in subsequent cases. The institutional learning has been slow and partial.

The contemporary version of the framework includes the "good governance" agenda (institutional reform conditionality), the focus on FDI attraction (FDI-friendly regulatory environment as policy priority), and the elevation of "evidence-based policy" through RCT methodology (which produces actionable findings on micro-interventions while leaving the structural questions unanswered).

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the framework's assumptions.** When IMF/World Bank advice is on the table, identify which Consensus or post-Consensus prescriptions are being applied, and what assumptions about country trajectory they imply.
2. **Compare with the historical record.** Did developed countries follow this advice during their own development? Almost always no.
3. **Compare with successful recent catch-up.** Did China, Vietnam, or Korea follow this advice during their catch-up? Almost always no.
4. **Identify what the framework is asking the country to give up.** Industrial policy? Capital controls? Selective trade protection? Public investment? Each "reform" has an opportunity cost in development strategy.
5. **Engage on alternative configurations.** What policy package, designed for this country's actual position, would support catch-up? Use this as the alternative to the orthodox package.
6. **Be selective about what to accept.** Some orthodox elements (fiscal discipline, transparency, anti-corruption in specific forms) are useful and should be implemented on their merits. Others (premature liberalisation, generic privatisation) should be resisted. The selectivity is the policy art.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa is being pressed by external interlocutors (IMF Article IV reviews, OECD recommendations, ratings agency commentary) to "complete structural reforms" — meaning further trade liberalisation, faster privatisation, labour market deregulation, and fiscal consolidation.

**Application:** Each of these recommendations is examinable against the historical record and the country's specific situation. Further trade liberalisation in a context of substantial deindustrialisation will accelerate the loss of manufacturing capability — the empirical pattern across post-Washington Consensus middle-income countries. Privatisation of SOEs that perform developmental functions (Eskom, Transnet for grid and rail infrastructure) will transfer monopoly rents rather than improve allocation. Labour market deregulation in a context of structural unemployment will lower wages without raising employment significantly. Fiscal consolidation during a growth-and-employment crisis is procyclical and self-defeating. The orthodox package is the wrong configuration for South Africa's current challenges.

**Result:** A defensible alternative configuration: maintain selective trade protection and industrial policy in targeted sectors; restructure rather than privatise developmental SOEs; combine some labour market flexibility with active labour market policy and demand-side support; sequence fiscal consolidation behind growth recovery. Engage with the external interlocutors on this alternative rather than accepting the orthodox framing.

**Situation:** A donor-funded study recommends rapid trade liberalisation under AfCFTA as the path to industrial development across the continent.

**Application:** The recommendation reflects the standard post-Washington Consensus framing of trade as universally beneficial. Applied to AfCFTA, it would mean rapid tariff elimination, harmonised low external tariffs, and minimal sector-specific intervention — precisely the configuration that has produced deindustrialisation across the continent's previous trade liberalisation episodes (the 1990s SAPs). The alternative is to approach AfCFTA as a strategic industrial policy instrument — protected market for African manufacturing during the catch-up phase, selective liberalisation in non-strategic areas, performance-conditioned cross-border investment frameworks.

**Result:** AfCFTA's developmental potential depends critically on how it is configured. The orthodox configuration will reproduce past failures; the strategic configuration could anchor a continental industrialisation push.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the IMF and World Bank as monolithic.
**Why:** Both institutions contain serious heterodox economists, internal critics, and reform initiatives. The framework critique applies to the institutional consensus, not to every staff member. Engaging with the internal critics is often productive.

**Don't:** Reject every element of the orthodox framework.
**Why:** Some elements are useful. Selective acceptance and selective resistance is the policy stance. Categorical rejection makes the critique easier to dismiss.

**Don't:** Imagine that the framework will be overturned by criticism.
**Why:** The framework reflects the interests of dominant shareholder countries. It will not be overturned by argument alone. The practical task is to maintain policy space within the framework, build coalitions across countries facing similar constraints, and demonstrate alternative configurations through successful implementation.
