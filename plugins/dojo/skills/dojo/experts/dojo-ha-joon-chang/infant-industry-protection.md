# Infant Industry Protection

## When to Use
- When designing trade and industrial policy for a sector that has development potential but cannot yet compete with established global incumbents.
- When evaluating whether to maintain, modify, or wind down existing protection in a maturing sector.
- When responding to the orthodox claim that infant industry protection is theoretically discredited or empirically ineffective.
- When making the case to Treasury or external donors for temporary, conditional protection as part of a development strategy.

## Fails When
- **The sector is fundamentally non-viable.** Some industries cannot become competitive regardless of protection — typically because of geographic, resource, or scale constraints. Protection in such cases is a permanent subsidy, not a temporary leg-up.
- **Protection lacks performance conditions.** Without measurable, time-bound targets for production, exports, local content, or capability development, protection becomes a rent transfer to incumbents.
- **There is no withdrawal mechanism.** If the protected industry has political power to extend protection indefinitely, the discipline that infant industry protection depends on disappears.
- **The protected industry is held to ransom by rent-seekers** — the policy becomes the goal rather than the development it was meant to enable.

## Core Concept

Alexander Hamilton's 1791 *Report on the Subject of Manufactures* and Friedrich List's 1841 *The National System of Political Economy* established the modern infant industry argument: a developing industry, even one with strong long-run prospects, cannot survive immediate exposure to mature foreign competitors that already possess decades of accumulated scale economies, technological learning, supplier networks, and skilled labour. Without temporary protection, the infant dies before reaching maturity.

The argument has three essential components. First, the protection is *temporary* — it is justified only during the catch-up phase. Second, it is *selective* — it applies to specific sectors with identifiable capability-development potential, not as a generalised tariff wall. Third, it is *performance-conditioned* — continued support requires verified progress toward measurable benchmarks. The combination of temporariness, selectivity, and conditionality is what distinguishes infant industry protection from generic mercantilism.

The empirical record is overwhelmingly supportive. Every successful late developer — the United States in the 19th century, Germany after 1879, Japan from the Meiji era, Korea and Taiwan after 1960, China after 1978 — used substantial infant industry protection during their catch-up phases. The historical evidence is so strong that the standard mainstream response is to argue not that protection failed in these cases, but that these cases were exceptions, special, or successful "despite" protection rather than because of it. This is the kind of argument one makes when the evidence is uncomfortable.

The serious questions about infant industry protection are not whether it works, but how to design it well:

**Duration.** Too short, and the industry has not had time to develop. Too long, and protection becomes permanent subsidy. Korean automotive protection ran roughly two decades; Japanese protection of various industries ran from one to three decades. There is no universal answer — it depends on the technological frontier the industry is climbing toward.

**Performance benchmarks.** Production volumes, export shares, local content percentages, R&D spend, productivity growth, skill development. These must be specific, measurable, and verifiable. Without them, "industrial policy" becomes "industrial spending."

**Discipline mechanisms.** Underperforming firms must lose support. This is the hardest design challenge because it is politically resisted. The Korean Economic Planning Board's discipline came partly from its political insulation under the developmental authoritarian regime; democratic developmental states must build the discipline through institutional design (independent review bodies, automatic withdrawal triggers, public disclosure).

**Sequencing of liberalisation.** As capabilities mature, protection is removed sector by sector. The sequence matters — typically, capital goods sectors are liberalised after the consumer goods sectors they supply have matured. Premature liberalisation in any sector imposes adjustment costs without strategic benefit.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the sector's development potential.** What are the technological capabilities being targeted? What is the global demand trajectory? What existing base — skills, supplier networks, related industries — can be built upon?
2. **Set time-bound performance benchmarks.** Production targets, export shares, local content percentages, R&D investment, skill development. The benchmarks should be ambitious enough to push capability accumulation, achievable enough to be credible.
3. **Specify the protection instruments and their levels.** Tariffs (set at levels that allow competition with domestic incumbents to remain meaningful — generally 20–40 per cent rather than prohibitive), quotas, subsidies, public procurement preferences. Choose instruments that match the development purpose.
4. **Build in a withdrawal schedule.** Specify the conditions under which support is reduced or removed: failure to meet benchmarks, maturity of the industry, achievement of agreed milestones. Make the schedule public.
5. **Establish disclosure and review.** Annual public reporting on performance against benchmarks. Independent technical review of whether protection should continue, be modified, or be withdrawn.
6. **Liberalise as capabilities mature.** When the industry can compete on international markets, the protection is removed. The exit is part of the policy, not an afterthought.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's automotive sector under the MIDP (1995) and APDP (2013) — a thirty-year programme of import-export complementation and tariff rebates designed to develop domestic automotive manufacturing and export capability.

**Application:** This is a textbook infant industry protection programme. The MIDP and APDP combined tariff protection on imports with rebates conditioned on local production and exports. Local content requirements pushed component manufacturing. The programme created roughly 110,000 direct jobs, a substantial component supplier base, and significant export capability — South African vehicles are now exported to Europe, the US, and elsewhere. The sector has reached a maturity at which the policy should be modified — the next decade should focus on the electric vehicle transition, higher value-add components, and R&D, with the tariff rebate component progressively replaced by R&D and capability-building subsidies.

**Result:** A maturing protection programme should not be wound down but redirected toward the next stage of capability accumulation. The lesson from Korean and Japanese automotive policy is that protection evolves into innovation policy as capabilities mature — it does not simply end.

**Situation:** Proposal to develop a domestic solar PV manufacturing industry in South Africa to support the renewable energy build-out.

**Application:** The infant industry case is strong: global solar PV manufacturing is dominated by Chinese firms with massive scale economies; South African manufacturers cannot compete on price alone; but South Africa has the renewable energy demand (the Just Energy Transition build-out), the metallurgical and chemical base for upstream inputs (silicon, glass, aluminium), and the policy instruments (public procurement, local content requirements on REIPPPP projects). A ten-to-fifteen-year protection programme combining local content requirements on public renewable energy procurement, tariff protection on imported modules, and capability-building subsidies could be justified. The performance conditions: specified production volumes, increasing local content percentages, R&D investment, export targets to African markets by year ten.

**Result:** A concrete infant industry protection design with measurable benchmarks and a withdrawal schedule, defensible against both pure free-trade critics (because of the conditions and exit) and pure protectionist excess (because of the discipline).

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Defend protection in the abstract without specifying duration, sectors, and conditions.
**Why:** "Industrial policy" and "protection" without design specifics are political slogans, not policy positions. The design is the policy.

**Don't:** Allow the protection to be set by the protected industry.
**Why:** Industries lobby for higher tariffs, longer duration, weaker conditions, and indefinite extension. If the policy is co-designed with the beneficiaries, it will be captured. Maintain technical and political distance between the policy-setters and the rent-receivers.

**Don't:** Treat infant industry protection as a permanent feature.
**Why:** It is justified by the catch-up phase. Once the industry has matured, the protection becomes a subsidy. The transition out is built into the policy from the beginning.
