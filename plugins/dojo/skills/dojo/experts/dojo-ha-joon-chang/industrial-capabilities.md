# Industrial Capabilities

## When to Use
- When defending the long-term, capability-focused approach to development against short-term GDP-focused arguments.
- When designing skills development, R&D investment, or technology absorption programmes.
- When evaluating whether economic growth is producing genuine development.
- When critiquing strategies that pursue GDP without capability accumulation.

## Fails When
- **The conversation is genuinely about short-run output management** (stabilisation, demand support during recession), where capability-building is not the relevant frame.
- **You make the argument too abstract.** Capabilities are not generic; they are specific to sectors, technologies, and production tasks.
- **You ignore the time horizon.** Capability accumulation is a decade-or-longer project. Be honest about the time scale.

## Core Concept

Economic development is the accumulation of productive capabilities, not the accumulation of GDP. This distinction is not pedantic — it determines which policies are developmental and which are merely growth-producing.

A country's productive capability is its capacity to do increasingly sophisticated things: to design and build complex products, to absorb and adapt new technologies, to organise complex production systems, to coordinate dense supplier networks, to operate at competitive scale and quality. Capability is embodied in workers' skills, firms' organisational knowledge, supplier ecosystems, R&D institutions, technical standards bodies, and the tacit knowledge that links them. Capability is built over time through learning by doing, learning by exporting, learning through technology absorption, formal training and education, and deliberate investment in research and development.

GDP can be raised without building capability. Examples include:

**Resource extraction booms.** Higher commodity prices raise GDP without changing what the country can do. When the prices fall, the country has not advanced.

**Capital inflow surges.** FDI in extractive sectors or in finance can raise GDP without embedding productive capabilities. The capital can leave; the capability has not been built.

**Tourism, BPO, and similar volume services.** These can grow output without accumulating the kind of sophistication that drives long-run productivity growth across the economy.

**Financialisation.** Asset price inflation produces measured income without producing productive transformation.

**Construction and infrastructure booms.** Useful in their own right, but if not connected to productive capability accumulation, they leave the country no more able to do sophisticated things after the construction ends.

The countries that have successfully developed have all done so through capability accumulation, almost always centred on manufacturing. The Korean trajectory from textiles (1960s) through steel and shipbuilding (1970s) through automobiles and electronics (1980s) through semiconductors (1990s) through complete information technology and biotech (2000s onwards) is the paradigm. Each stage built on the previous; each required deliberate state-led capability investment; each produced workers, firms, and supplier networks more sophisticated than the last. The result is a country whose ability to make things is now in the global top tier.

Capability accumulation has several characteristics that distinguish it from generic investment:

**Path dependence.** Capabilities accumulate sequentially. You cannot leap into advanced manufacturing without the preceding stages; the workers, suppliers, and institutional knowledge accumulated in earlier stages are the foundation for later stages.

**Tacit knowledge.** Much of productive capability is embodied in workers and firms rather than in codified knowledge. It cannot be imported through manuals, training videos, or even FDI without sustained operational engagement.

**Coordination problems.** Many capabilities are systemic — they require the simultaneous development of skills, suppliers, infrastructure, and customer demand. Markets do not solve these coordination problems automatically; the developmental state explicitly addresses them.

**Time horizon.** Capability accumulation takes decades. Korea's automotive industry was a thirty-year project; semiconductors were another twenty. Short-horizon policy cannot build capabilities.

**Specificity.** Capabilities are sectoral. A country builds automotive capability or pharmaceutical capability or semiconductor capability through sector-specific learning; there is no generic "manufacturing capability" that can be built in the abstract.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose the country's current capability base.** What can the country make? At what quality and scale? Which sectors have accumulated supplier networks, skilled workers, and operational knowledge?
2. **Identify the next-stage capabilities to build.** Where can existing capabilities be extended? Which sectors have demand trajectories and learning potential? Which capabilities are foundational for further stages?
3. **Design capability-building support.** Skills development tied to sector requirements; R&D investment in priority technologies; FDI policy that requires capability transfer; public procurement that anchors demand for capability-building investment.
4. **Resist GDP-focused short-term measures** that do not build capability. Commodity export expansion, tourism volume growth, financialisation — these can grow GDP without producing the structural transformation that constitutes development.
5. **Measure capability, not just GDP.** Track export complexity, technology adoption, skill upgrading, supplier network density, R&D investment, patenting, productivity growth in specific sectors. These are the indicators of development.

## Examples

**Situation:** South African mining beneficiation policy — the proposal to require minerals to be processed in South Africa rather than exported in raw form.

**Application:** Beneficiation as capability building can be developmental if designed correctly. The question is which minerals, at what processing depth, with what supporting capabilities. Iron ore to steel requires energy, water, infrastructure, and metallurgical capability. Platinum group metals to catalytic converters requires advanced chemical and metallurgical capability and connection to automotive demand. Lithium and rare earths to battery and electronics components requires significant supporting infrastructure and skill base. The capability lens reframes the debate from "should we beneficiate?" (yes/no) to "which beneficiation, with which supporting capabilities, on what time horizon?" Some beneficiation projects make sense; others are economically marginal without the supporting capabilities being built simultaneously.

**Result:** A capability-focused beneficiation strategy: identify the value chains with strong demand trajectories (battery materials for EVs, platinum catalysts for hydrogen economy, rare earths for electronics), invest in the supporting capabilities (energy, water, technical skills, R&D), design FDI partnerships that require capability transfer, build over decades. Resist beneficiation rhetoric that requires processing without capability building — that just adds cost without producing capability.

**Situation:** A consultancy proposes that South Africa should focus on tourism and high-end services to drive growth, given the country's natural endowments and existing service base.

**Application:** Tourism and high-end services can grow GDP without building productive capability. The country can grow as a destination without becoming more able to do sophisticated things; the workers, firms, and institutional knowledge in tourism do not build into manufacturing, technology, or knowledge-intensive sectors in the way that manufacturing capability builds across sectors. The proposal mistakes a current activity for a development strategy.

**Result:** Tourism has its place in the economy, but it is not a development strategy. The development strategy needs to centre on capability accumulation in manufacturing and connected services (engineering, design, R&D) that drives sophistication across the economy. Tourism can grow alongside, not instead of.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Talk about "building capability" without specifying which capabilities.
**Why:** Capabilities are sectoral. Generic capability rhetoric is content-free. The specifics — automotive capability, pharmaceutical capability, semiconductor capability — are what matter.

**Don't:** Imagine capability can be imported through FDI without sustained domestic engagement.
**Why:** FDI can transfer some capability under the right conditions (performance requirements, local content, R&D partnerships). It cannot substitute for domestic capability accumulation. Countries that rely entirely on FDI for sophistication remain dependent.

**Don't:** Confuse short-run productivity gains with capability accumulation.
**Why:** Cost reduction and operational improvement can raise short-run productivity without building capability for the next stage. Capability is forward-looking; productivity is current. Both matter, but they are not the same.
