# State-Owned Enterprises

## When to Use
- When debating privatisation, restructuring, or expansion of state-owned enterprises.
- When responding to the orthodox claim that SOEs are inherently inefficient and should be privatised.
- When reviewing governance and performance frameworks for existing SOEs.
- When considering the role of new public enterprise in industrial development.

## Fails When
- **The specific SOE is structurally non-viable.** Some SOEs cannot become productive regardless of governance reform; continued operation is a permanent transfer.
- **The institutional environment cannot support disciplined SOE governance.** If political interference is structurally guaranteed and cannot be insulated against, the SOE will fail regardless of business design.
- **You defend SOEs in general without addressing specific failures.** SOE defence requires engaging with the specific governance design.

## Core Concept

State-owned enterprises are not inherently inefficient. They are not inherently efficient either. They are an organisational form whose performance depends on design, governance, mandate, and institutional environment. The orthodox position that SOEs are inherently inefficient and should be privatised wherever possible is not supported by the historical record or by the contemporary cross-country evidence.

The historical record shows SOEs playing major roles in every successful catch-up development: POSCO in Korea (steel); Embraer in Brazil (aerospace); Petrobras in Brazil (oil and energy); Eskom in apartheid-era South Africa (electricity, in its productive phase); SingTel and Singapore Airlines in Singapore; Renault in postwar France; the UK's nationalised industries during postwar reconstruction; Volkswagen (substantially state-owned in postwar Germany); Statoil/Equinor in Norway; the Chinese state-owned enterprise sector throughout the reform period. The cross-country evidence shows enormous variation in SOE performance — from highly productive, innovative public enterprises (POSCO, Petrobras in its strong period, Singapore Airlines) to chronic loss-makers (Argentina's YPF under various governance arrangements, many Sub-Saharan African parastatals in the 1970s–80s, Eskom in its captured period).

The variation is explained by design and governance, not by the public-private form. The features that produce SOE success are identifiable:

**Clear, developmentally-oriented mandate.** The SOE has an explicit purpose tied to a national development strategy — supplying strategic inputs (steel, energy, transport), accumulating capabilities in priority sectors, providing infrastructure, anchoring industrial clusters. The mandate is public and reviewable.

**Hard budget constraint with developmental support.** The SOE faces real performance discipline — bankruptcy is possible, underperformance has consequences — combined with patient capital and developmental support during the catch-up phase. The discipline is what distinguishes a developmental SOE from a rent-extraction vehicle.

**Insulated, technically capable management.** Senior management is recruited on technical merit, paid competitively, given long tenures, and insulated from short-term political interference. The board structure protects strategic continuity. The Korean POSCO model is the paradigm — a public enterprise run with technical excellence and long-horizon strategic planning.

**Transparency and public accountability.** Public reporting on performance against mandate. Independent technical audit. Public disclosure of executive compensation, procurement, and major decisions. The accountability is what defends the SOE against capture.

**Discipline of failed bets.** When SOEs undertake new investments or business lines that fail, the failures are recognised, restructured, and exited. Public ownership without exit discipline becomes the soft-budget-constraint failure mode.

The contemporary South African SOE crisis (Eskom, Transnet, SAA, Denel, the Post Office, and others) is not evidence that SOEs are inherently inefficient. It is evidence of what happens when SOE governance is captured. The failure mode includes: politically rather than technically appointed senior management; soft budget constraint without performance discipline; opaque procurement and patronage networks; mandate drift from developmental purpose to rent distribution; absence of credible exit mechanisms for failed business decisions; and political interference in operational decisions. Each of these is a governance failure, not an ownership-form failure. The Korean POSCO and the South African Eskom were both publicly owned; the differences are entirely in governance and political-economy environment.

Privatisation is sometimes the right answer — for SOEs whose mandate has expired, whose markets have matured to support private competition, or whose governance cannot be reformed in the available political space. It is sometimes the wrong answer — for SOEs with developmental mandates that markets will not finance, for natural monopolies where private ownership transfers rents rather than reducing costs, for strategic sectors where developmental coordination requires public ownership. The decision is case-by-case and requires substantive analysis of mandate, market structure, and governance feasibility.

## How to Apply

1. **Assess the specific SOE on five dimensions.** Mandate (clear, developmental, public?). Governance (insulated, technically capable management?). Discipline (hard budget constraint, exit on failure?). Transparency (public reporting, independent audit?). Strategic role (still serving its developmental purpose?).
2. **Diagnose specific failures.** Where the SOE is performing badly, identify the governance, mandate, or environment failures producing the poor performance — not a generic "public ownership inefficiency."
3. **Design specific reforms.** Governance redesign (board structure, management appointment, performance discipline); mandate clarification; budget constraint hardening; transparency improvements; exit mechanisms for failed business decisions.
4. **Decide on privatisation on its merits.** Where mandate has expired and markets are competitive, privatisation may be appropriate. Where developmental coordination is still required and markets will not provide it, restructuring within public ownership is appropriate.
5. **Resist ideological framings.** "Privatise because SOEs are inefficient" and "do not privatise because public ownership is inherently better" are both ideological positions disguised as analysis.

## Examples

**Situation:** Eskom — South Africa's electricity utility, currently in protracted operational and financial crisis after a decade of state capture, poor procurement, and underinvestment.

**Application:** Eskom's failures are governance failures, not public-ownership failures. The required interventions are governance redesign (board insulation from political interference, technical competence in management appointments, transparent procurement, performance-conditioned executive compensation), mandate clarification (electricity supply, grid management, what role in the energy transition), financial restructuring (separate the legacy debt from the operational entity, allow the operational entity to plan investment), and discipline mechanisms (independent technical review, public performance reporting). The model is not "Eskom should be privatised because it is publicly owned and therefore inefficient" — it is "Eskom should be restructured and re-governed to perform its mandate." Privatisation of generation segments may have a place; full privatisation of the grid would transfer monopoly rents from public to private hands without solving the underlying problem.

**Result:** A reform programme that addresses the actual problem (governance and capture) rather than treating the symptom (poor performance) with a remedy (privatisation) that does not target the cause.

**Situation:** Proposal to privatise Transnet's port and rail operations on the grounds that "private operators are more efficient."

**Application:** Port and rail are network infrastructure with strong natural monopoly characteristics. Privatisation typically transfers the monopoly rent from public to private owner without changing the underlying market structure. The international evidence on rail and port privatisation is mixed at best — some succeeded (UK rail privatisation as a partial success on operational metrics, controversial on others), some failed (UK rail privatisation as a financial and reliability failure depending on metric, multiple emerging-market port concessions as rent-extraction exercises). The relevant comparison is not "private versus public ownership" but "what governance and discipline arrangements produce the desired performance." Singapore's PSA (port operations) is one of the world's most efficient port operators and is state-owned. Korean port operations are mixed public-private. The question is design, not ownership.

**Result:** Reject the "privatise because efficient" framing. Engage with what governance and operational arrangements would actually produce the desired performance — which may include some private participation in specific segments but does not require generic privatisation.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Defend SOEs in general against the orthodox critique without engaging with specific failures.
**Why:** SOE failures are real and frequent. The defence of SOEs as an organisational form requires engagement with the specific governance, mandate, and discipline design — not a generic claim that public ownership is fine.

**Don't:** Argue for privatisation on the grounds that public ownership is inherently inefficient.
**Why:** The historical record does not support this claim. Engage on the specific case — mandate, market structure, governance feasibility — not on the ideological generalisation.

**Don't:** Confuse SOE reform with privatisation.
**Why:** Most SOE reform options sit within public ownership. Restructuring, recapitalisation, governance redesign, mandate clarification, board restructuring — these are SOE reforms that do not involve privatisation. The full menu of options is wider than the orthodox "public versus private" framing allows.
