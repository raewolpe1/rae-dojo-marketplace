# Selective Trade Protection

## When to Use
- When designing or reviewing a national trade policy that needs to balance protection and openness.
- When debating tariff schedules under AfCFTA or other regional or global trade agreements.
- When responding to the orthodox claim that protection is autarkic or that openness is universally beneficial.
- When sequencing trade liberalisation across sectors as capabilities mature.

## Fails When
- **The protection is generalised across all sectors.** Generalised protection is autarky or close to it; the costs to consumers and to non-targeted sectors outweigh the benefits.
- **The protection has no link to a development strategy.** Protection unconnected to capability building is rent transfer.
- **The country lacks the institutional capacity** to administer selective protection (sector targeting, performance monitoring, sunset enforcement). In this case, simpler regimes may be more practical even at the cost of strategic loss.
- **The exit is undefined.** Indefinite protection becomes the goal rather than the means.

## Core Concept

Selective trade protection is the trade-policy expression of industrial policy. It applies tariffs, quotas, public procurement preferences, and other trade instruments to specific sectors with development purposes, while maintaining openness in sectors where openness serves the strategy. It is neither autarky nor across-the-board free trade — it is strategic, sequenced, performance-conditioned use of trade instruments.

The concept rests on three observations:

**Different sectors have different developmental properties.** Some have learning potential, dense linkages, capability-building value; others do not. Trade policy should differentiate between them rather than treating all sectors identically. The mainstream view that the trade regime should be uniform across sectors has no basis in either theory or evidence; it is a political stance disguised as a technical one.

**The sequencing matters.** Successful late developers liberalised sector by sector as capabilities matured, not all at once. The Korean automotive industry was protected for two decades and then opened up; consumer electronics followed a similar pattern; semiconductors are still receiving substantial state support. The sequence is: build the capability behind protection, then open up. Reverse sequence (open up first, hope for capability later) has a poor empirical record.

**Trade policy is one instrument among several.** Tariffs interact with subsidies, public procurement, FDI policy, exchange rate policy, and skill development. Designing tariffs in isolation produces sub-optimal policy. The trade strategy is part of the development strategy, not separate from it.

The instruments of selective trade protection include:

**Tariffs.** Set at levels that give domestic industry breathing space without insulating it from all competitive pressure — typically 20–40 per cent in catch-up phases for targeted sectors, with much lower or zero tariffs in others. Specific tariff schedules (per unit rather than ad valorem) provide more predictable protection. Tariff escalation (higher tariffs on finished goods than on inputs) supports value addition.

**Local content requirements and rebate schemes.** Conditional benefits (e.g. import rebates conditional on local production) link trade policy to capability development. The South African MIDP/APDP used this structure successfully.

**Public procurement preferences.** State purchasing power directed to domestic suppliers, conditional on price and performance benchmarks. Public procurement can anchor demand for capability-building investment.

**Strategic use of regional integration.** Regional agreements (AfCFTA, MERCOSUR, ASEAN) can be used as protected enlarged markets in which domestic industries scale before facing global competition — not as steps to generalised free trade.

**Anti-dumping and countervailing instruments.** Defending against predatory pricing by foreign competitors is consistent with selective protection and is within WTO-compliant policy space.

The WTO and bilateral trade agreements constrain the available policy space but do not eliminate it. Successful late developers in the WTO era (China, Vietnam, increasingly Ethiopia, Bangladesh) have used WTO-consistent instruments — performance requirements on FDI, SEZs, state procurement, technical regulations — alongside policies that bend or test the constraints. The constraints are real but are routinely overstated by interlocutors who want a particular policy stance.

## How to Apply

1. **Map sectors against developmental properties.** Which have capability potential, linkages, learning curves? Which are mature or non-strategic?
2. **Design a tariff schedule that differentiates.** High protection on targeted catch-up sectors; moderate or low on inputs to those sectors; low or zero on consumer goods that do not have strategic value.
3. **Tie tariffs to performance.** Where possible, structure tariff benefits as conditional on local content, production volume, exports, or capability investment — not as unconditional protection.
4. **Sequence the liberalisation.** Specify which sectors will be liberalised in which order, with what triggers (capability benchmarks, time horizons). Make the schedule public.
5. **Use regional integration strategically.** Negotiate AfCFTA (and similar) tariff schedules as part of the industrial strategy — protected market for the targeted sectors, openness in non-strategic areas.
6. **Build the administrative capacity.** Selective trade policy requires technical capacity to monitor sectors, verify performance, and enforce conditions. Underbuild the capacity and the policy fails.

## Examples

**Situation:** AfCFTA tariff schedule negotiation. South Africa is debating which sectors to liberalise first and which to retain protection on, under the agreed gradual tariff reduction schedule.

**Application:** AfCFTA should be approached as a strategic industrial policy instrument, not as a step toward generalised free trade. The targeted sectors for capability development (automotive, agro-processing, chemicals, renewable energy manufacturing, mining beneficiation) should retain maximum protection allowable under the agreement, with longer phase-down schedules. Inputs to those sectors should be liberalised quickly to lower cost structures. Non-strategic consumer goods can be liberalised on the standard schedule. The result is a tariff schedule that uses the AfCFTA market as protected scale-up territory for South African manufacturing, while reducing input costs and gaining access to other African markets for South African exports.

**Result:** A defensible, strategic tariff schedule consistent with both the AfCFTA framework and South Africa's industrial policy.

**Situation:** Treasury proposes across-the-board tariff reduction to "reduce input costs" and stimulate growth.

**Application:** Across-the-board reduction does not distinguish between inputs and finished goods, between strategic and non-strategic sectors, between catch-up and mature industries. It treats trade as a single-dimensional choice between more and less. The proposal mistakes a slogan for a policy. A more useful tariff reform would be: reduce tariffs on intermediate inputs to manufacturing (lower cost structures for targeted sectors), retain or strengthen tariffs on finished goods that compete with developmental sectors, structure performance conditions on remaining tariffs.

**Result:** The proposal is reframed from "openness vs protection" to "which tariffs, on which products, for what purpose." A productive design conversation replaces the binary debate.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Defend protection without specifying the design.
**Why:** Generalised pro-protection arguments are as weak as generalised pro-free-trade arguments. The design is the policy.

**Don't:** Confuse selective protection with autarky.
**Why:** Selective protection is consistent with substantial trade openness in non-targeted sectors. Autarky is closure. They are different.

**Don't:** Imagine you can build administrative capacity for selective trade policy by simply announcing the policy.
**Why:** Sector targeting, performance monitoring, anti-dumping investigation, and exit enforcement require real institutional capacity. If the capacity is weak, the policy will fail regardless of its design quality.
