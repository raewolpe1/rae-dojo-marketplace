# Manufacturing and Development

## When to Use
- When debating whether a developing country should focus on manufacturing or services-led growth.
- When responding to arguments that the digital economy or financial services can substitute for manufacturing.
- When diagnosing the developmental consequences of premature deindustrialisation.
- When designing a development strategy that needs an anchoring sector logic.

## Fails When
- **The country has already industrialised** and is genuinely climbing the value chain into knowledge-intensive services that build on manufacturing capabilities (Switzerland, Singapore, the Netherlands). The argument applies to catch-up; mature economies have different problems.
- **You treat all manufacturing as identical.** Garment assembly is not the same as automotive assembly is not the same as semiconductor fabrication. Different segments have very different capability and learning properties.
- **You overstate the case to the point of denying any role for services.** Services have their place; the argument is that services cannot *substitute* for manufacturing in a catch-up trajectory.

## Core Concept

Manufacturing is the engine of development. This is not a sentimental attachment to making things; it is an empirical proposition about where productivity growth, technological learning, tradeable surplus, and skill accumulation come from in successful development trajectories.

Four properties distinguish manufacturing from most services in a development context:

**Productivity growth potential.** Manufacturing productivity grows faster, over longer periods, than service productivity. This is partly because manufacturing benefits from economies of scale, learning-by-doing, and capital deepening in ways that most services (haircuts, restaurant meals, retail) do not. The historical pattern is unambiguous: countries that have grown rich have done so through periods of rapid manufacturing productivity growth.

**Technological learning and capability accumulation.** Manufacturing forces engagement with engineering, materials science, process technology, and quality management at scales and tolerances that services rarely require. The skills, knowledge, supplier networks, and institutional capabilities accumulated through manufacturing are foundational for everything else that follows. South Korean software development was built on top of three decades of hardware engineering experience.

**Tradeable surplus.** Manufacturing produces tradeable goods that earn foreign exchange. A country without manufacturing must pay for its imports through commodity exports (vulnerable to price swings and the resource curse), tourism (limited and exposed to global shocks), remittances (a poverty signal, not a development strategy), or aid (not a strategy at all). Tradeable surplus from manufacturing is what funds the imports of capital goods, technology, and inputs that further development requires.

**Backward and forward linkages.** Manufacturing has dense input-output linkages — it buys inputs from many sectors and sells outputs to many sectors. This is why manufacturing-led growth lifts the rest of the economy: agricultural processing creates demand for agriculture; automotive manufacturing creates demand for steel, glass, rubber, electronics, plastics; pharmaceutical manufacturing creates demand for chemicals. Services typically have thinner linkage structures.

The orthodox claim that developing countries can skip manufacturing and go directly to services — the "leapfrog" or "service-led development" thesis — is empirically not supported. The countries that have successfully developed through services either had a pre-existing manufacturing base (Singapore, Switzerland) or are very specific cases (financial entrepôts, micro-states, oil exporters using oil revenue to fund service-economy build-out) that are not generalisable templates. Most countries that have prematurely deindustrialised have not become wealthy service economies; they have become low-productivity informal service economies with commodity dependence and limited tradeable surplus.

The "washing machine matters more than the internet" argument is the polemical version of this case. The technologies that have transformed living standards over the past century are predominantly mechanical and household — running water, electricity, refrigeration, the washing machine, motor vehicles — not digital. These are manufactured goods. A country that does not make them is dependent on countries that do, and the wealth flows accordingly.

## How to Apply

1. **Audit the country's manufacturing base.** What share of GDP and employment is in manufacturing? What is the trend? Which segments? What productivity differentials exist between manufacturing and the rest of the economy?
2. **Distinguish manufacturing segments by capability and linkage properties.** Light manufacturing (garments, food processing) has lower learning potential than capital goods, machinery, automotive, and electronics. The development strategy should weight the segments accordingly.
3. **Diagnose premature deindustrialisation if present.** Has the manufacturing share fallen at income levels well below the historical pattern of developed countries? If so, this is a structural problem requiring industrial policy, not a natural transition.
4. **Resist the service-economy substitution argument.** When proposed, ask: which services, at what productivity levels, generating what tradeable surplus, with what linkage to other sectors? Most service-economy proposals do not survive these questions.
5. **Design the manufacturing strategy around capabilities.** Which segments are you trying to build? What capabilities are you accumulating? How does the strategy interact with manufacturing in regional and global value chains?

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa has experienced significant deindustrialisation since the early 1990s — manufacturing has fallen from around 24 per cent of GDP to around 12 per cent, with employment in manufacturing falling correspondingly. The dominant sectors are now financial services, retail trade, and mining.

**Application:** This is textbook premature deindustrialisation. South Africa's manufacturing share is well below where comparator middle-income countries should be, and the fall has occurred at income levels where developed countries were still industrialising. The consequences are visible: structural unemployment (manufacturing jobs lost are not replaced by equivalent-productivity service jobs), commodity dependence (rand depreciation pressure when commodity prices fall), constrained capacity to absorb new entrants to the labour market, and a fiscal base too narrow for the social spending the country needs.

**Result:** Reindustrialisation is not nostalgia; it is the strategic priority. The components of a reindustrialisation strategy: identify the manufacturing segments with capability base, demand, and learning potential (automotive moving up the value chain, agro-processing for African markets, chemicals, renewable energy manufacturing, mining beneficiation); design protected market access for them (AfCFTA strategy, selective tariff protection, local content requirements); accumulate the supporting capabilities (skills, supplier networks, R&D); attract performance-conditioned FDI in targeted sectors. None of this is achievable through a "service-economy transition" narrative.

**Situation:** A consultancy report recommends South Africa pivot to a "knowledge economy" model led by financial services, BPO, and digital services, on the grounds that this matches global trends.

**Application:** The recommendation reflects a category error. Global trends in the destination economies (already industrialised, accumulating financial and digital capabilities on top of manufacturing bases) are not relevant to South Africa's developmental position. Financial services in South Africa are concentrated, internationally oriented, and weakly linked to the domestic productive economy; expanding them does not solve the structural unemployment problem. BPO is volume-sensitive and exposed to international labour cost arbitrage. Digital services are valuable but have low employment intensity. None of these can absorb the labour-force entrants in the numbers required.

**Result:** Reject the framing. South Africa's developmental challenge is broad-based productive employment, accumulation of manufacturing capabilities, and tradeable surplus generation. These come from manufacturing, not from services. The "knowledge economy" pivot is what advanced economies do; it is not the destination for a country that has not completed the prior stage.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use the argument to dismiss services categorically.
**Why:** Services matter and grow alongside successful manufacturing development. The argument is about substitution and sequencing — services cannot substitute for manufacturing as the development driver; they grow as complements as manufacturing capabilities mature.

**Don't:** Romanticise low-productivity manufacturing.
**Why:** The case for manufacturing depends on its productivity growth and learning properties. Subsistence-level garment assembly does not have these properties to the degree that capital goods, machinery, automotive, or electronics manufacturing does. The segment matters.

**Don't:** Skip the diagnosis of premature deindustrialisation when it is the central problem.
**Why:** In South Africa and several other middle-income countries, premature deindustrialisation is the structural problem behind unemployment, inequality, and growth stagnation. Solutions that do not address it are addressing symptoms.
