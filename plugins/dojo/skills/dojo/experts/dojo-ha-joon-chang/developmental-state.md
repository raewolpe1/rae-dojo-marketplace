# The Developmental State

## When to Use
- When discussing institutional design for an industrialisation strategy.
- When the question is whether a country can build the state capability to coordinate development.
- When evaluating an existing industrial policy architecture for coherence and effectiveness.
- When responding to claims that the state cannot play a developmental role.

## Fails When
- **You romanticise the East Asian cases.** Japan, Korea, Taiwan, and Singapore all combined developmental success with significant authoritarianism, labour repression, and political constraint. Acknowledge the trade-offs.
- **You apply the model without considering political-economy preconditions.** Bureaucratic capacity, political insulation from short-term capture, long-term political horizon — these are not built overnight.
- **You treat "developmental state" as a label rather than a set of institutional features** that need to be specifically designed.
- **You confuse a strong state with a capable state.** State capacity is not the same as state size; many states are large but incapable.

## Core Concept

The developmental state is the institutional configuration that produced the East Asian Miracle — sustained, broad-based industrialisation in Japan (Meiji and postwar), Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, and increasingly China. The model combines four core features that together distinguish it from both laissez-faire capitalism and command socialism:

**A capable, technocratic bureaucracy.** The Japanese MITI, the Korean Economic Planning Board (EPB) and Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy, the Taiwanese Council for Economic Planning and Development (CEPD), and Singapore's EDB recruited the best graduates of the top universities, paid them well, gave them long careers and substantial authority, and insulated them from political churn. The bureaucracy combined deep technical capability with strategic authority — it could understand industries, design policy, and make consequential decisions.

**A long political horizon.** Developmental states have looked ten, twenty, thirty years ahead. The Korean automotive industry was a thirty-year project; Singapore's electronics-and-pharmaceuticals upgrading is a forty-year project. This requires political insulation from short-term electoral or factional cycles. East Asian developmental states have typically achieved this through some combination of single-party dominance, technocratic semi-autonomy, and elite consensus on the development project.

**The capacity to discipline capital.** This is the feature that most distinguishes the developmental state from clientelist or rent-distributing states. The developmental state supports private firms through tariffs, subsidies, cheap finance, and protected markets — but it withdraws support from firms that underperform. Korean chaebol that failed export performance benchmarks lost cheap credit. Japanese firms that did not invest in R&D lost MITI support. The discipline is what turns industrial policy into capability building rather than into rent transfer.

**A focus on building productive capabilities, not just GDP.** The developmental state is oriented to climbing the technology ladder, not to maximising current output. POSCO was built as a steel producer that would supply the shipbuilding, automotive, and machinery industries — its value was its position in the capability hierarchy, not its short-run profitability. The state has the patience and the strategic framing to support capability-building investments that markets would not finance.

The East Asian cases came with costs. Japan's postwar bureaucratic dominance came with weak parliamentary accountability. Korea's developmental period (1961–1987) was authoritarian, with labour repression and political imprisonment. Taiwan's was the same. Singapore remains a soft-authoritarian polity. These costs are not incidental — the political-economy preconditions for developmental state success are not innocent. The question for democracies attempting the developmental state model is how to achieve the institutional preconditions (technocratic capacity, long horizon, capacity to discipline capital) without paying the same authoritarian price. Some have argued it cannot be done; others (post-1987 Korea, post-2000 Taiwan) suggest it can be partially preserved as democratisation occurs.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose state capacity honestly.** What is the technical capability of the relevant economic ministries? What is the quality of bureaucratic recruitment? What is the political insulation from short-term capture? Without an honest baseline, design proposals will not be credible.
2. **Identify the institutional centre of strategic authority.** Every East Asian developmental state had an identifiable centre (MITI, EPB, CEPD, EDB). Where does strategic authority for industrial policy reside? If it is fragmented across many agencies with overlapping mandates, the architecture is not developmental.
3. **Specify the disciplining mechanism.** What withdraws support from underperforming firms? Performance review processes, sunset clauses, public disclosure, independent technical assessment. Without these, the state's role is rent distribution, not capability building.
4. **Build the long political horizon.** This requires either elite consensus that survives political cycles or institutional design that protects strategic continuity (independent industrial development agencies, multi-year budget commitments, depoliticised technical staff).
5. **Accept the political-economy trade-offs explicitly.** The developmental state asks for political insulation, technocratic authority, and the capacity to override short-term private interests. These have costs and need to be defended on their merits.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's industrial policy architecture — DTIC, IDC, NEF, dtic agencies, multiple sector master plans, BBBEE codes, special economic zones. Is this a developmental state?

**Application:** Diagnosed against the developmental state template, the architecture has serious gaps. Bureaucratic capacity in the relevant departments is uneven and has been undermined by state capture; political insulation has been weak (turnover at director-general and minister level has been high, strategic continuity has been disrupted); the disciplining mechanism is largely absent (BBBEE incentives, BIP support, IDC loans frequently lack rigorous performance conditions and withdrawal triggers); and strategic authority is fragmented across multiple agencies without a clear centre. South Africa has many of the *instruments* of a developmental state without the *institutional architecture* that makes those instruments work. The result is industrial policy that resembles the rent-distribution failure mode more than the capability-building success mode.

**Result:** The path forward is not to abandon the developmental state ambition (the alternative is a market-led model that the historical record shows does not produce broad-based industrialisation) but to rebuild the institutional architecture. Concrete first steps: consolidate strategic authority in a coherent industrial development centre with technical capability and political backing; introduce performance conditions and disclosure across the support instruments; protect technical staff from political churn through tenure arrangements; build a longer political horizon through multi-administration consensus on a core industrial agenda.

**Situation:** A donor-funded review of an African country's industrial policy proposes adopting "international best practice" — meaning the OECD checklist of property rights, anti-corruption, light regulation, FDI attraction, and minimal sector targeting.

**Application:** This is the kicking-away-the-ladder template applied as a checklist. None of the East Asian developmental states followed this template. The OECD members did not follow this template during their own catch-up. The historical record suggests the opposite template: build state capability, target strategic sectors, condition support on performance, accumulate manufacturing capabilities, liberalise as you mature. The review is recommending the policies of the destination, not the policies of the journey.

**Result:** Reframe the review. The question is not "do we conform to OECD best practice" but "what institutional architecture do we need to catch up?" That question leads to a developmental state design adapted for the country's specific political economy.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the developmental state as a uniform template.
**Why:** The East Asian cases differ substantially — Japan's bureaucratic dominance, Korea's chaebol-state nexus, Taiwan's SME-and-public-enterprise model, Singapore's state-led FDI model. The general features (capability, insulation, discipline, capability-building focus) are common; the specific designs vary.

**Don't:** Imagine you can have the East Asian results without the East Asian institutional investment.
**Why:** Building a capable, technocratic, insulated bureaucracy takes decades and political commitment. Countries that want the outputs of the developmental state must invest in the inputs.

**Don't:** Apologise for the model on free-market grounds.
**Why:** The developmental state model is empirically the most successful catch-up architecture in the past century. Defend it on its record, not as a regrettable second-best.
