# Industrial Policy

## When to Use
- When designing a national industrial strategy, sector master plan, or specific industrial intervention.
- When reviewing existing industrial policy instruments for coherence and effectiveness.
- When making the case for industrial policy against the orthodox claim that it doesn't work.
- When distinguishing genuine industrial policy (selective, conditional, capability-building) from rent transfers dressed up as industrial policy.

## Fails When
- **You defend or attack industrial policy in the abstract.** "Industrial policy" covers everything from Korean POSCO to American DoD procurement to subsidies to politically connected firms with no performance criteria. The design is the policy.
- **The policy has no performance conditions.** Without measurable, time-bound, withdrawable conditions, industrial policy becomes a subsidy programme captured by incumbents.
- **The policy targets no specific sectors.** Generic "support for manufacturing" with no sector choices is rhetorical industrial policy.
- **The policy lacks a discipline mechanism.** Underperforming firms must lose support. This is the hardest design feature and is missing from most weak industrial policies.

## Core Concept

Industrial policy is selective, sector-specific government action to build productive capabilities. The "selective" and "sector-specific" parts matter: generic horizontal policies (good roads, good schools, predictable regulation) are necessary but are not what is meant by industrial policy. Industrial policy makes choices about which sectors to support, when, how, on what conditions, and for what strategic purpose.

The objection to industrial policy from orthodox economics rests on three claims, each of which is empirically weak. First, "governments cannot pick winners" — but every successful catch-up economy has had governments picking winners, and the pattern of failures is not noticeably worse than the pattern of market-led investment failures. Second, "industrial policy creates rent-seeking" — true, but so does every other policy, and the relevant question is whether rent-seeking is contained by discipline mechanisms. Third, "industrial policy distorts markets" — yes, that is the point; markets do not spontaneously generate the capability accumulation needed for development, which is why the policy exists.

The serious questions about industrial policy are design questions:

**Selection.** Which sectors? Criteria: existing capability base to build on, global demand trajectory, technological learning potential, linkages to other sectors, strategic importance. Korean policy in the 1960s–70s targeted steel, shipbuilding, automobiles, electronics, petrochemicals — sectors with strong learning potential and dense input-output linkages. Singapore targeted electronics, pharmaceuticals, financial services. Taiwan targeted semiconductors. The sector choices have logic and are defensible publicly.

**Instruments.** Tariffs, subsidies, public procurement preferences, cheap directed finance, SEZ infrastructure, R&D co-funding, skill development. The instruments are chosen for the purpose: tariffs to give the domestic industry breathing space; subsidies for capability investments markets won't fund; procurement to anchor demand; cheap finance for scale-building; SEZs to overcome coordination problems in specific locations.

**Performance conditions.** Production volumes, export shares, local content percentages, productivity growth, R&D spend, employment, skill upgrading. These must be specific, verifiable, and tied to continued support. The design needs an answer to: "if the firm does not meet these benchmarks by year three, what happens?"

**Time horizon.** Industrial policy is a decade-or-longer project. Short-term industrial policy is a contradiction in terms. The instruments need to be designed for the time horizon — multi-year commitments, sunset clauses, scheduled review points.

**Institutional architecture.** Who decides which sectors? Who monitors performance? Who has the authority to withdraw support from underperformers? Who reviews the strategy as it unfolds? The institutional design is as important as the policy substance.

## How to Apply

1. **Specify the strategic intent.** What capability are you building? What is the desired position of this sector in ten years? Industrial policy without a strategic intent is industrial spending.
2. **Choose the sectors deliberately and defensibly.** Use criteria (capability base, demand, learning potential, linkages, strategic value) and be transparent about the choice. Name the sectors publicly.
3. **Design the instrument mix to match the purpose.** Tariffs for breathing space; subsidies for capability investment; procurement for demand; cheap finance for scale. Avoid the temptation to use every instrument for every purpose.
4. **Tie support to verifiable performance conditions** with multi-year benchmarks and clear withdrawal triggers.
5. **Build the institutional architecture.** Identify the strategic authority, the technical monitoring function, the independent review process. Insulate them from short-term political churn.
6. **Schedule reviews and exits.** When does the policy review? What are the criteria for continuing, modifying, or winding down? When does protection give way to liberalisation as the sector matures?

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's Black Industrialist Programme (BIP) — designed to support black-owned manufacturing enterprises through grants, equity, and procurement.

**Application:** Diagnosed against industrial policy design criteria, BIP has the structure of a transformation policy more than a capability-building policy. The eligibility is defined by ownership rather than by sector or capability strategy. The performance conditions on grants are weak. The withdrawal mechanism is essentially absent. The integration with broader industrial strategy (which sectors are being targeted? how do BIP firms fit into the strategy?) is unclear. The result is that the programme functions partly as a transformation instrument (which has its own legitimacy) and partly as a rent-distribution mechanism — but not, in its current design, as capability-building industrial policy.

**Result:** Two reform paths. Option A: keep BIP as a transformation programme, accept that it is not industrial policy in the strict sense, and build a separate capability-focused industrial policy alongside it. Option B: redesign BIP to integrate transformation and capability building — eligibility by ownership AND by sector targeting, with performance conditions on production, exports, employment, and skill development. Option B is harder but more consistent with industrial policy in the East Asian sense.

**Situation:** Proposed special economic zone in the Eastern Cape focused on automotive and components.

**Application:** SEZs as industrial policy work when they solve a coordination problem (concentrating suppliers, sharing infrastructure, lowering transaction costs for a cluster), are anchored by performance-conditioned investment by major firms, and connect to a broader strategy for the sector. SEZs as industrial policy fail when they are tax-incentive zones with no specific industry strategy, no anchor firms, no performance conditions, and no link to a national capability-building plan. The proposed Eastern Cape SEZ should be evaluated on these criteria. If it is anchored by a real automotive expansion (e.g., new model production, EV transition, component manufacturing) with local content and skill commitments, and if it connects to the national automotive strategy and AfCFTA market access, it is industrial policy. If it is essentially a tax incentive in a depressed area, it is regional development with industrial policy branding.

**Result:** A clear test for the SEZ proposal: what is the anchor, what are the performance conditions, what is the link to national strategy? Without satisfactory answers, the proposal should be redesigned, not funded.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use the phrase "industrial policy" to dignify rent transfers.
**Why:** Real industrial policy has design features (selection, conditions, discipline, time horizon). Calling subsidies "industrial policy" without those features debases the concept and produces predictable failure.

**Don't:** Try to do industrial policy without a strategic intent.
**Why:** Industrial policy is the application of strategic intent to specific sectors. Without strategic intent, you have spending without direction.

**Don't:** Hide the sector choices.
**Why:** The choices are political and consequential. Make them public, defend them publicly, and accept the political accountability. Hidden sector choices are not democratically legitimate and tend to drift toward capture.
