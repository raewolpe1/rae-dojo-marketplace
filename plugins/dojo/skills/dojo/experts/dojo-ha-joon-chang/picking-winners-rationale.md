# Picking Winners Rationale

## When to Use
- When responding to the orthodox claim that "governments cannot pick winners and shouldn't try."
- When designing sector-targeting frameworks for industrial policy.
- When evaluating mission-oriented innovation policy (Mazzucato-style) or strategic sector investment.
- When debating whether to support specific firms or industries with public resources.

## Fails When
- **The institutional environment cannot discipline failed bets.** Picking winners requires the political and institutional capacity to withdraw support from underperformers. Without that capacity, the failure mode dominates.
- **You ignore the genuine risks.** Capture, white-elephant projects, locked-in subsidies are real risks. Defend the practice acknowledging the risks, not denying them.
- **You defend it in general without addressing the design.** "Governments should pick winners" is not a policy. The design — which winners, on what criteria, with what conditions — is the policy.

## Core Concept

The orthodox claim that "governments cannot pick winners and should not try" is empirically false. Every successful catch-up economy has had governments picking winners — sometimes brilliantly, sometimes badly, but always actively. The historical record is unambiguous and is not seriously contested by economic historians. The relevant question is not whether to pick winners but how to do it well.

A partial inventory of state-picked winners that have driven global industrial development:

**Korea.** POSCO (steel) was a state-created enterprise that became one of the world's most efficient steel producers. Hyundai (shipbuilding, then automobiles) was supported by directed credit, protected markets, and explicit performance benchmarks. Samsung (semiconductors) was supported by chaebol-state investment partnerships and protected from foreign competition during its catch-up phase. LG (electronics) followed a similar trajectory. The Korean state did not just permit these firms; it picked them, supported them, and disciplined them.

**Japan.** MITI picked steel, automobiles, machine tools, electronics, and semiconductors in successive waves. The targets were public, the support was active, and the discipline was real — firms that underperformed lost support, sometimes through orchestrated mergers and consolidations.

**Taiwan.** TSMC was a state-created enterprise that has become the dominant global semiconductor foundry. The Industrial Technology Research Institute (ITRI) — a public R&D institution — incubated TSMC, UMC, and other major Taiwanese tech firms.

**United States.** The Department of Defense procurement created the semiconductor industry, the internet, GPS, and most of the foundational technologies of Silicon Valley. NIH funding built the modern pharmaceutical industry. NASA created markets for advanced electronics, materials, and aerospace components. The CHIPS Act and the Inflation Reduction Act (clean energy subsidies) are contemporary US winner-picking on enormous scale. The American state has picked winners more aggressively than any other in the post-WWII period.

**France.** Postwar industrial policy directly built or supported Renault, Airbus, Alcatel, the nuclear power industry, the TGV high-speed rail system. The dirigiste tradition was active winner-picking.

**Singapore.** Temasek (sovereign wealth fund) and the Economic Development Board picked sectors and firms through six decades of industrial development — initially electronics, then pharmaceuticals, then financial services, then advanced manufacturing.

**Finland.** Nokia was supported by the Finnish state's targeting of telecommunications as a strategic sector, including R&D investment, education policy, and protected domestic market access.

**Brazil.** Embraer (aerospace) is a state-created enterprise that has become a major global aircraft manufacturer.

The pattern is consistent. The orthodox claim that the state cannot pick winners is contradicted by every significant case of successful industrialisation. The claim is not analysis — it is ideological commitment maintained against the evidence.

The relevant question is what makes winner-picking work. The design conditions that distinguish successful winner-picking from rent-distribution failure include:

**Technocratic selection.** Sectors and firms chosen on technical criteria (capability potential, demand trajectory, learning curves, linkages) by competent ministries, not on political criteria.

**Performance conditions.** Continued support tied to verifiable benchmarks — production, exports, R&D investment, employment, capability development.

**Discipline of failures.** Failed bets are recognised, restructured, and exited. The state withdraws support from firms that have not delivered. This is the hardest design feature and is what distinguishes the developmental state from the clientelist state.

**Multiple bets.** No country has picked only winners; every successful case has had failures alongside the successes. The portfolio approach — supporting multiple sectors and firms, expecting some to fail, exiting the failures — is essential.

**Long time horizon.** Winner-picking pays out over a decade or more. Short-term assessment will miss most of the value.

**Public transparency.** The choices are made publicly, defended publicly, and reviewed publicly. Opacity invites capture.

The risks of winner-picking are real. State capture turns winner-picking into rent distribution. Locked-in subsidies become permanent transfers. White-elephant projects waste resources. These failure modes are well-documented and frequent. The serious design question is how to capture the success cases while managing the failure modes — which requires the institutional architecture of the developmental state.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the sectors with capability potential.** Use technical criteria — existing base, learning potential, demand trajectory, linkages, strategic importance.
2. **Make the choices public and defend them.** The bets should be transparent, with clear strategic logic.
3. **Tie support to performance.** Specific, measurable, time-bound benchmarks. Withdrawal triggers for underperformance.
4. **Build discipline into the design.** Independent technical review, public reporting, automatic withdrawal mechanisms.
5. **Accept that some bets will fail.** Build the political and institutional capacity to recognise failure and exit. The state that cannot fail cannot succeed at winner-picking.
6. **Sustain the long time horizon.** Multi-administration commitment, multi-year budget protection, technical staff continuity.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa is debating whether to make a major state-supported bet on electric vehicle and battery manufacturing as the next stage of automotive industrial policy.

**Application:** This is a textbook winner-picking decision. The case for the bet: existing automotive capability, global demand trajectory (rapid EV transition), upstream resource base (platinum group metals, manganese, increasingly lithium), trade access (EU and AfCFTA), regional demand. The case against: capability gaps in battery cell manufacturing specifically, competition from massive Chinese, Korean, and US incentive packages, capital intensity. The bet is defensible if the design is right — anchor firm partnerships with binding capability transfer commitments, R&D investment in battery chemistry and electrode manufacturing, supplier development across the EV value chain, public procurement preferences in fleet electrification, AfCFTA local content rules favouring African-made EVs. Performance conditions: production volumes, local content percentages, R&D spend, employment, supplier ecosystem development. Time horizon: fifteen years. Discipline: independent review every three years with explicit withdrawal triggers.

**Result:** A concrete winner-picking design that satisfies the orthodox sceptic's concerns about capture and locked-in subsidies while preserving the strategic intent.

**Situation:** Treasury argues that the state should not "pick winners" in clean energy and should let market mechanisms (carbon pricing, regulatory standards) drive the transition.

**Application:** Market mechanisms have a role but cannot substitute for active winner-picking in a transition of this scale and speed. Every major economy is actively picking winners in clean energy — the US through the IRA, China through massive subsidies and procurement, the EU through industrial strategy and the Green Deal Industrial Plan. South Africa's choice is not "pick winners or not"; it is "pick winners actively or accept being downstream of others' winner-picking." The strategic question is which segments of the clean energy value chain South Africa should pick — solar PV manufacturing, wind components, battery materials, electrolysers for green hydrogen, transmission technology. Each has different capability requirements and demand trajectories.

**Result:** Reject the "don't pick winners" framing. Engage with which winners to pick and how to design the support. The orthodox position is, in this case, a recipe for being a permanent technology importer.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Defend winner-picking in general without addressing the design.
**Why:** The general defence is weak because it does not engage with the failure modes. The strong defence is design-specific.

**Don't:** Pretend the failure modes don't exist.
**Why:** State capture, locked-in subsidies, white elephants — these are real and frequent. The design must address them, not deny them.

**Don't:** Imagine winner-picking is a substitute for general industrial policy infrastructure.
**Why:** Picking winners requires the institutional capacity to monitor, discipline, and exit failed bets. Without that capacity, sector targeting becomes rent transfer. Build the institutional capacity alongside the bets, not before or after.
