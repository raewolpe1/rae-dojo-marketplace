# Kicking Away the Ladder

## When to Use
- When orthodox advice (free trade, rapid liberalisation, minimal state, property rights first) is being presented as a universal recipe and the historical record needs to be put back on the table.
- When a developing country is being pressured by the IMF, World Bank, OECD, WTO, or bilateral trade partners to abandon industrial policy or protection.
- When defending the legitimacy of industrial policy and selective protection against the claim that "this is not how mature economies operate."
- When debunking the myth that today's rich countries got rich through free trade and laissez-faire.

## Fails When
- **You use it as a slogan rather than an argument.** The thesis is only powerful when accompanied by the specific historical evidence — Walpole's 1721 tariffs, Hamilton's 1791 *Report*, the US average tariff of 40–50 per cent through the late 19th century, Korea's tariffs and capital controls into the 1990s.
- **The interlocutor is already familiar with the history.** Among economic historians and heterodox economists, the thesis is uncontroversial. Don't lecture audiences who already accept it.
- **You apply it to genuinely technical policy questions** where the broad framing is settled and the question is about detail (specific tariff schedules, exchange rate management, etc.).

## Core Concept

Today's rich countries — Britain, the United States, France, Germany, Sweden, Japan, Korea, Taiwan — did not become rich by following the policies they now recommend to developing countries. They used aggressive tariff protection, industrial subsidies, state-owned enterprises, restricted foreign investment, weak intellectual property enforcement, and active sector-specific policy throughout their catch-up phases. They liberalised — partially, selectively, sometimes — only after their industries had become globally competitive and free trade had become the policy that locked in their existing advantages.

This is not a fringe revisionist claim. It is the consensus of serious economic historians and is documented in detail by Friedrich List in 1841, by Reinert and Reinert in recent scholarship, and across the standard reference works on the economic history of industrialisation. What is striking is how systematically this evidence is excluded from mainstream policy discourse, where the founding myth of liberal capitalism — that free trade and minimal state were the engines of Western prosperity — continues to drive the policy advice given to developing countries.

The metaphor is from Friedrich List: a developed country, having climbed up the ladder of development through protection and state activism, then "kicks away the ladder" by which it climbed, denying the same instruments to those below. Britain did this most famously to the 19th-century United States and Germany — who, sensibly, ignored the advice and protected. The United States did it to Korea, Taiwan, and Japan in the postwar period — who, sensibly, ignored the advice and protected. The IMF, World Bank, and WTO do it today to every African, Latin American, and South Asian country that comes to them for support.

> *Kicking away the ladder by which one has climbed up is a very common, but mean act on the part of those who have already reached the top.* — Friedrich List, 1841

The empirical record is not contested at the level of professional economic history. It is contested only at the level of contemporary policy discourse, where the contradiction between the historical record and the current prescription is managed by simply not discussing the history.

## How to Apply

1. **When confronted with orthodox advice, ask which countries followed this advice during their own development.** The answer for protectionism is: all of them, almost always. The answer for free-market orthodoxy is: none of them, at least not during catch-up.
2. **Cite the specific historical periods.** Britain 1721–1846 (Walpole tariffs through Corn Laws repeal). United States 1816–1945 (post-1812 protection through postwar dominance). Germany under Bismarck (1879 tariff). Japan Meiji through Showa. Korea 1961–1997. China 1978–present.
3. **Distinguish liberalisation as a destination from liberalisation as a starting point.** Every developed country liberalised eventually. None started there. The question for a developing country is sequencing, not whether liberalisation is desirable in some future state.
4. **Connect the historical pattern to the present prescription.** "You are being told to do what your developed-country interlocutors did not do. Why is the advice different from the practice?"

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa is being pressed by the World Bank and bilateral donors to reduce industrial subsidies, liberalise the trade regime further, and "let markets pick winners" in manufacturing.

**Application:** The kicking-away-the-ladder lens reframes the conversation. The United States runs the largest industrial policy in the world — the DoD purchases that built Silicon Valley, the NIH that built the pharmaceutical industry, NASA, DARPA, the CHIPS Act, the Inflation Reduction Act subsidies for clean energy. Germany runs sector-specific subsidies and state investment through KfW. South Korea built its automotive, steel, semiconductor, and shipbuilding industries on three decades of explicit protection and targeting. The advice South Africa is receiving is the advice these countries do not follow themselves. The question to put on the table: why is the advice for South Africa different from the practice of the countries giving it?

**Result:** The conversation reorients from "are subsidies good or bad?" to "what does the historical and contemporary record say about industrial policy in successful catch-up — and how do we design ours to match those design features?"

**Situation:** A trade liberalisation case is being made under AfCFTA on the grounds that "free trade is what mature economies practice."

**Application:** Mature economies do not practice free trade. The EU runs the CAP (Common Agricultural Policy) — one of the most extensive protection systems in the world. The United States restricts steel, textiles, sugar, and dairy, and uses national security exemptions liberally. Japan protects its rice farmers. The WTO regime, in practice, is not free trade but a managed system in which rich countries retain extensive protection in politically sensitive sectors while pressing poor countries to open. AfCFTA should be approached as a strategic industrial policy instrument — a protected regional market in which African manufacturing can scale — not as a step toward generalised free trade.

**Result:** The negotiating frame changes. South Africa is not "behind" by maintaining protection; it is doing what every successful developed country does.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the kicking-away-the-ladder thesis as a complete argument for any specific policy.
**Why:** The historical record establishes the *legitimacy* and *feasibility* of industrial policy and selective protection. It does not specify *which* sectors, *what* tariff levels, or *how* to design performance conditions. Those are design questions that require contemporary analysis. The history defends the project; the design carries the project.

**Don't:** Use the argument as if it were a contemporary discovery.
**Why:** This argument is in Friedrich List, 1841. It is in Alexander Hamilton, 1791. It has been recovered by every generation of heterodox economists. Presenting it as a new revelation undersells how robust the evidence is and how thoroughly it has been excluded from mainstream discourse.

**Don't:** Imagine the kicking-away is a conspiracy.
**Why:** It is structural, not conspiratorial. Free trade is in the interest of countries with already-competitive industries; the IMF and World Bank reflect the policy preferences of their dominant shareholders. The pattern emerges from material interest, not from coordinated bad faith. Treating it as conspiracy makes the analysis easier to dismiss.
