# Critique of RCT Development Economics

## When to Use
- When RCT (randomised controlled trial) methodology is being positioned as the gold standard for development policy.
- When debates about "what works" in development are being framed entirely in terms of impact evaluation evidence.
- When evaluating the appropriate role of J-PAL, IPA, and the randomista tradition in a country's policy work.
- When a structural development question is being approached as if it were a micro-intervention question.

## Fails When
- **The question is genuinely a narrow micro-intervention.** Some questions — "does free distribution of bed nets increase usage compared to subsidised distribution?" — are well-suited to RCT methodology. The critique is about scope, not validity.
- **You straw-man the randomistas.** Banerjee, Duflo, and Kremer are serious scholars whose work has produced useful findings. Engage with the actual claims.
- **You deny that any RCT findings are useful.** Some are, within their scope. The critique is about the framing of RCTs as the methodology for development, not about the validity of the technique.

## Core Concept

The randomista movement in development economics — associated with Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer, and the J-PAL/IPA institutional network — has used randomised controlled trial methodology to test specific micro-interventions in poor-country contexts. The methodology has produced rigorous, replicable findings on questions like the effect of deworming on school attendance, the relative efficacy of conditional and unconditional cash transfers, the design of microcredit delivery, and the impact of various small-scale education and health interventions. The methodological rigour is real; the Nobel Prize awarded to Banerjee, Duflo, and Kremer in 2019 recognised genuine contribution.

The critique is not of the methodology in itself but of three derivative tendencies that have followed from its rise:

**Methodological imperialism.** The framing that RCTs are the gold standard for development evidence — and that questions not amenable to RCT testing are second-tier or unanswerable — narrows the discipline's attention to micro-interventions. The questions that drove the East Asian Miracle (the design of industrial policy, the institutional architecture of the developmental state, the sequencing of trade liberalisation) cannot be RCT-tested. They are too large, too historically contingent, too institutionally specific. Framing RCTs as the gold standard implicitly downgrades these questions from the development agenda.

**Structural evasion.** The micro-interventions that RCTs can test — bed nets, deworming, cash transfers, microcredit variants — are useful but secondary to the structural transformations that drive development. A country with deworming-optimised school attendance but no industrial transformation will remain poor. The randomista turn risks producing rigorous answers to second-order questions while leaving the first-order question (how do countries actually develop?) unanswered. The political economy of this evasion is convenient — donors and Western academics can fund and publish rigorous studies on micro-interventions without engaging with the structural transformations that historically drove development.

**Political depoliticisation.** Development economics in the structural-institutional tradition is unavoidably political — it engages with questions of state action, distributional consequences, and policy choice. RCT-based development economics is technocratic — it produces findings about specific interventions without engaging with the political-economic questions that determine which interventions are politically feasible, who benefits, and how the broader institutional environment shapes outcomes. The depoliticisation makes the work easier to publish and easier to fund; it also makes it less useful for the political-economic challenges of actual development.

The historical record makes the critique sharper. The countries that have successfully developed — Japan, Korea, Taiwan, China — did not develop through accumulated findings from RCTs. They developed through industrial policy, trade strategy, capability accumulation, and developmental state action. Some of the interventions involved would have been amenable to RCT testing; most were not. The RCT-led approach to development policy, projected backwards onto the catch-up cases, would have produced an unrecognisable picture of what actually happened.

The randomistas have responded to versions of this critique in different ways. Some have engaged seriously (Duflo's work on scaling, for instance, has shown awareness of the limits of micro-interventions). Others have doubled down on the methodological imperialism. The honest position is that RCTs have a role — for specific micro-interventions where they are methodologically appropriate — alongside other methodologies (historical case study, institutional analysis, structural political economy) for the larger questions they cannot address.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the question's scale.** Is this a narrow micro-intervention question or a structural-strategic question? RCTs are appropriate for the former, not the latter.
2. **Resist the framing that micro-evidence settles structural questions.** Findings about cash transfer design or microcredit configuration do not tell us what industrial policy a country should pursue.
3. **Use multiple methodologies on the structural questions.** Historical case study (what did Korea actually do?), institutional analysis (what state capacity does this require?), political economy (what coalition supports this strategy?), structural macro (what are the demand and supply implications?).
4. **Be selective about RCT findings.** Where RCT findings are well-designed and within their scope, use them. Where they are extrapolated beyond their scope or used to evade structural questions, push back.
5. **Demand that "evidence-based policy" include the structural evidence.** The historical record is evidence. Comparative institutional analysis is evidence. Don't let "evidence" be redefined as "evidence from RCTs."

## Examples

**Situation:** A donor-funded review of South African development policy recommends focusing on RCT-tested micro-interventions (specific labour activation programmes, cash transfer designs, educational interventions) as the path to addressing unemployment and inequality.

**Application:** Micro-interventions in labour activation and education have their place but cannot address the structural causes of South African unemployment and inequality. Structural unemployment in South Africa reflects premature deindustrialisation, a constrained productive base unable to absorb labour-force entrants, and concentrated market structures. These problems require structural responses — industrial policy, trade strategy, capability building, public investment in productive sectors — not micro-interventions. The recommendation reflects a category error about the scale of the problem.

**Result:** Reframe the policy work. Use micro-evaluation evidence for the design of specific programmes where it is appropriate (labour matching design, cash transfer conditionality, specific training programme content). Use structural-institutional analysis for the larger questions about industrial policy, manufacturing, and capability accumulation. The two are complementary; they are not substitutes.

**Situation:** A consulting team proposes that the impact evaluation of an industrial policy intervention be designed as an RCT, randomising the support across firms.

**Application:** Many industrial policy interventions cannot be sensibly randomised. The strategic logic of industrial policy is sector-specific support based on capability potential, not random selection. The relevant evaluation questions — does this sector accumulate capability under this support regime? does the support discipline rent-seekers? does the sector emerge as internationally competitive after a decade? — are not RCT-shaped. The proposal would either distort the policy to fit the methodology (random selection rather than strategic targeting) or produce findings that tell us about average treatment effects without illuminating the strategic logic.

**Result:** Replace the RCT design with a methodology appropriate to the question: comparative case study across sectors and firms, contribution analysis tracing the policy's role in observed outcomes, comparative analysis against similar policies in similar countries, capability accumulation tracking over time. This is what evaluating industrial policy actually requires.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Reject the entire RCT enterprise.
**Why:** Useful work has been done. Some questions are well-suited to the methodology. Selective use is appropriate.

**Don't:** Use the critique to defend bad development practice.
**Why:** "We don't need rigorous evidence because RCTs don't capture structural questions" is a different argument. Structural development work should still be rigorous — through historical analysis, institutional comparison, and theoretical reasoning. The critique of RCT imperialism is not a licence for evidence-free development.

**Don't:** Caricature the randomistas as careerist.
**Why:** They are serious scholars producing serious work. The critique is about scope and framing, not motivation. Treating motivation makes the argument easier to dismiss.
