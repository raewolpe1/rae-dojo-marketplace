# The Blue Commons: Ocean and Marine Resources

## When to Use

- When advising on fisheries policy, marine governance, blue economy strategy, or seabed mining regulation in coastal or island nations
- When analysing how ocean resources — historically the most global of commons — are being enclosed through licences, concessions, and privatisation
- When connecting marine resource governance to basic income financing through ocean commons dividends

## Core Concept

The ocean, Standing argues in *The Blue Commons*, is the last great enclosure. For millennia, the seas were treated as res communis — belonging to no one, open to all. Fisheries were governed by customary rights. The seabed was beyond jurisdiction. Marine genetic resources were part of common heritage. This is ending.

The enclosure of the blue commons takes multiple forms. Industrial fishing fleets, heavily subsidised by wealthy nations, have depleted fish stocks that sustained artisanal fishing communities for generations. Individual Transferable Quotas (ITQs) — the market mechanism beloved of fisheries economists — have converted common fish stocks into tradeable private property, concentrating ownership in corporate fleets and dispossessing small-scale fishers. Seabed mining concessions grant corporations the right to extract minerals from the deep ocean floor — a commons that belongs to humanity under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea but is being parcelled out to private interests through the International Seabed Authority. Aquaculture enclosures privatise coastal waters. Marine genetic resources — organisms from the deep sea with pharmaceutical and industrial applications — are being biopatented, turning common heritage into intellectual property.

The consequences fall hardest on coastal communities in the Global South. Small-scale fisheries employ more people and feed more mouths than industrial fishing, but they are being squeezed out by industrial fleets operating under licences sold by governments that never consulted the fishing communities. West African artisanal fishers compete with Chinese and European supertrawlers operating under opaque bilateral agreements. Pacific Island communities watch their tuna stocks harvested by distant-water fleets under access agreements that return a fraction of the value to the host nations.

Standing's prescription follows his general commons framework: the blue commons must be defended, and where enclosure has already occurred, a blue commons dividend must be paid. Coastal nations should capture resource rents from fishing licences, seabed mining concessions, and aquaculture permits, and return them as a dividend to citizens — particularly coastal communities that bear the costs of enclosure. The blue economy, properly governed, can finance basic income for island and coastal nations.

## How to Apply

1. **Map the enclosure of marine resources in the specific context.** Identify which ocean commons are being privatised: fish stocks through ITQs or bilateral access agreements, coastal waters through aquaculture concessions, seabed through mining licences, marine genetic resources through biopatents. Who holds the licences? Who profits? Who is displaced?

2. **Quantify the rent being extracted.** What are fishing licence fees relative to the value of the catch? What do seabed mining concessions cost relative to the value of extracted minerals? What share of tuna caught in Pacific waters accrues to Pacific Island nations? The gap between the fee paid and the value extracted is the rent — the commons dividend owed.

3. **Connect blue economy policy to basic income.** Coastal nations with significant marine resources can finance a citizens' dividend from ocean resource rents. This transforms "blue economy" rhetoric — usually a cover for further privatisation — into a genuine commons governance framework.

4. **Defend artisanal fisheries as commons governance.** Small-scale fisheries are not inefficient relics to be modernised out of existence. They are functioning commons governance systems — Ostrom-style management of shared resources by the communities that depend on them. Policy should protect and strengthen them, not replace them with ITQs.

## Examples

**Situation:** A West African coastal nation is negotiating a new fisheries partnership agreement with the EU. The agreement would grant European distant-water fishing vessels access to the country's exclusive economic zone in exchange for an annual fee. Artisanal fishing communities — which employ 300,000 people directly and feed millions — are alarmed. The government sees the agreement as a source of budget revenue. A development advisor is asked to evaluate the deal.

**Application:** Apply Standing's blue commons framework. The fish stocks in the EEZ are a national commons — shared wealth belonging to all citizens, sustained by centuries of artisanal management. The proposed agreement encloses this commons: it converts common fish stocks into a licence for European industrial extraction. The fee — typically 5-10% of the value of the catch in such agreements — represents a fraction of the rent being extracted. The rest is captured by European fleet owners. The costs — stock depletion, bycatch destruction, displacement of artisanal fishers — are borne by the coastal communities. Standing's prescription: renegotiate the agreement to capture a far larger share of the resource rent (30-50% of catch value, not 5-10%). Ring-fence this revenue in a blue commons fund. Pay a portion as a direct dividend to citizens of coastal communities — a basic income financed by the ocean commons. Protect artisanal fishing zones from industrial encroachment. This transforms the agreement from a vehicle of enclosure into a mechanism of commons governance. The artisanal fishing communities are not obstacles to the blue economy — they are the blue economy's legitimate custodians.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Accept "blue economy" rhetoric at face value.
**Why:** "Blue economy" in practice usually means further privatisation of ocean resources — aquaculture concessions, seabed mining licences, marine biotechnology patents. Standing reads it as the latest chapter of enclosure dressed in sustainability language. Always ask: who owns the resource, who profits, and who is displaced?

**Don't:** Treat Individual Transferable Quotas as a neutral efficiency tool.
**Why:** ITQs convert common fish stocks into private property. They concentrate ownership in the hands of those who can afford to buy quotas — invariably large corporate fleets. They dispossess artisanal fishers who cannot compete in quota markets. The efficiency gains are real but the distributional consequences are catastrophic. ITQs are the fisheries equivalent of land enclosure.
