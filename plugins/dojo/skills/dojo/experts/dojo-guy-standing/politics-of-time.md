# The Politics of Time: Time as Contested Political Resource

## When to Use

- When analysing how the distribution of time — work time, care time, leisure, rest, political participation — reflects and reinforces structural inequality
- When connecting time poverty to the precariat, to gender inequality, and to the case for basic income
- When advising on policies that affect time allocation: working time regulation, care infrastructure, platform work, the right to disconnect

## Core Concept

Time, Standing argues in *The Politics of Time*, is a political resource — as much a matter of justice as the distribution of income or wealth. Who has time, who is time-poor, who controls the allocation of others' time — these are questions of power, not personal time management.

The precariat is defined partly by its relation to time. It has no control over its own time. Zero-hours contracts mean being perpetually available without knowing when work will come. Platform algorithms dictate pace, scheduling, and availability. The gig economy creates what Standing calls a tertiary time structure — time spent not working and not at leisure but waiting, preparing, applying, commuting, complying with bureaucratic requirements. This tertiary time is unpaid, unrecognised, and growing. The precariat works fewer paid hours than the salariat but has less free time, because tertiary time devours the apparent leisure.

Gender is central. Women's unpaid care work — childcare, elder care, domestic labour, emotional labour — is the largest single category of uncompensated labour in every economy. It is not counted in GDP, not compensated by social protection, not recognised in labour market statistics. Women in the Global South bear the heaviest time burdens: fetching water, collecting fuel, processing food, caring for the sick in the absence of health services. Time-use surveys consistently show that women in sub-Saharan Africa work longer total hours than men but earn less because their work is unpaid or underpaid.

Basic income addresses time poverty in two ways. First, it provides the economic security to refuse time-exploitative work — the power to say no to a zero-hours contract, to refuse unpaid overtime, to withdraw from degrading gig work. Second, it recognises and partially compensates for unpaid care work and community labour that the market does not value. A basic income paid to individuals, not households, gives women economic autonomy within households — the freedom to allocate their own time rather than being compelled by economic dependence.

But Standing is clear that basic income alone is not sufficient. Time sovereignty also requires regulation — limits on working time, the right to disconnect, portable benefits for gig workers — and investment in public services that reduce unpaid care burdens: water infrastructure, childcare, healthcare, elder care. The politics of time requires both cash (basic income) and services (public infrastructure), an insight that partially bridges Standing's position with Coote's Universal Basic Services argument.

## How to Apply

1. **Conduct a time analysis alongside the income analysis.** When evaluating any social protection programme or development intervention, ask not just "does this reduce income poverty?" but "does this reduce time poverty?" A public works programme that requires eight hours of labour per day may increase income while worsening time poverty — especially for women who must add programme labour to existing care responsibilities.

2. **Identify the tertiary time burden.** In any labour market analysis, look for the hidden time costs: commuting in cities without public transport, waiting for gig assignments, complying with benefit conditions (queuing at government offices, attending mandatory training), preparing meals without kitchen infrastructure. This tertiary time is the invisible tax on the precariat.

3. **Use time-use data to reveal gender inequality.** National time-use surveys, where they exist, provide the most direct evidence of how care work is distributed. Use them to make the case that women's time poverty is structural — not a matter of choice — and that basic income, by providing individual economic autonomy, gives women the power to renegotiate household time allocation.

4. **Connect time sovereignty to basic income design.** A basic income that is genuinely individual (not household-based) and unconditional (not requiring work-search activity) directly increases time sovereignty. Designs that are household-based or that require compliance activities (job search, training attendance) reproduce time poverty rather than alleviating it.

## Examples

**Situation:** A development organisation is designing a women's economic empowerment programme in rural Tanzania. The proposed design includes skills training (three days per week), a savings group (weekly meetings), and a conditional cash transfer requiring women to attend health clinics monthly. The programme assumes women have unstructured time that can be filled with empowering activities. The designers have not conducted a time-use analysis.

**Application:** Apply Standing's time analysis before designing the programme. Rural Tanzanian women's days are already full: water collection (average 60-90 minutes per trip, often multiple trips), fuel collection, food processing and preparation, childcare, agricultural labour on family plots, and often market activity. Adding three days of training, a weekly savings group, and monthly clinic visits does not empower — it imposes an additional time burden on women who are already time-poor. The conditional cash transfer compounds this: the condition to attend health clinics punishes women whose time constraints prevent compliance.

A basic income approach is superior on time grounds. An unconditional individual payment requires no time investment — no training attendance, no savings group meetings, no clinic compliance. It gives women the cash to purchase time-saving goods (fuel, prepared food, water delivery) and the economic autonomy to renegotiate household time allocation. The Madhya Pradesh pilots documented exactly this: women who received unconditional individual payments invested in time-saving assets and reported greater control over their own time. The programme should replace the conditional, time-intensive design with an unconditional cash transfer plus investment in time-saving public infrastructure — water points, fuel-efficient stoves, accessible health services — that reduce the unpaid care burden structurally.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat time poverty as a personal productivity problem amenable to "time management" solutions.
**Why:** Time poverty is structural, not individual. It is produced by unequal power relations — in the household, in the labour market, in the state's allocation of public services. Telling a woman in rural Mozambique to "manage her time better" is as absurd as telling her to "manage her poverty better." The solution is structural: basic income, public services, and regulation.

**Don't:** Design development programmes that impose additional time burdens on women in the name of empowerment.
**Why:** This is the most common and most damaging anti-pattern in development practice. Skills training, savings groups, conditional compliance activities — all demand time from women who have none to spare. If the programme does not start with a time-use analysis and address time poverty directly, it is not empowering women; it is exploiting their compliance.
