# UBI Pilots: The International Empirical Record

## When to Use

- When grounding basic income arguments in specific pilot outcomes rather than theoretical claims
- When comparing pilot designs and results across different contexts — income levels, state capacity, cultural settings
- When designing or evaluating a new pilot programme, drawing on lessons from existing experiments

## Core Concept

Standing insists that the case for basic income rests on evidence, not theory. The international pilot record — uneven, imperfect, politically contested — is the empirical foundation. Each pilot teaches different lessons, and each has limitations. What matters is the cumulative weight of evidence across diverse contexts.

The Namibian pilot (Otjivero-Omitara, 2008-2009) was a community-level experiment funded by a coalition of churches, unions, and civil society. Every resident of a small settlement received a monthly payment of N$100. Results were striking: malnutrition among children dropped sharply, school attendance rose, economic activity increased (residents started small businesses, purchased productive inputs), crime fell, and clinic attendance improved. The pilot was small and not randomised, but its comprehensiveness — measuring multiple domains simultaneously — made it influential. Standing was directly involved in the design and evaluation.

The Indian SEWA/UNICEF pilots (Madhya Pradesh, 2011-2013) were larger, better designed, and more rigorously evaluated. Eight villages received unconditional individual payments; twelve comparable villages served as controls. The results confirmed Namibia's findings at greater scale: improved nutrition, increased economic activity (especially among women), higher school attendance, better health outcomes, and — critically — no reduction in labour supply. Recipients worked more, not less, because the security of a basic income enabled them to take productive risks. Women's agency increased measurably. Standing was centrally involved in these pilots and considers them the strongest evidence base for basic income in a developing country context.

The Finnish experiment (2017-2018) targeted 2,000 unemployed people who received EUR 560 per month unconditionally. It was not a true basic income pilot — it was limited to the unemployed, not universal — but it showed that removing conditionality from benefits improved wellbeing and life satisfaction without reducing employment. Recipients reported less stress, better health, and more trust in institutions. The employment effects were modest but not negative, contradicting the "laziness" narrative.

Kenya's GiveDirectly programme (ongoing since 2016) is the largest basic income experiment in history — approximately 20,000 people in Siaya County receiving long-term unconditional transfers. Early results show increased consumption, asset accumulation, and economic activity. The scale and duration make it the most important ongoing experiment for long-term effects.

Brazil's Marica programme, operational since 2013, provides a municipal basic income (in local digital currency, the mumbuca) to residents of Marica, Rio de Janeiro state. It has expanded progressively and now covers a significant share of the municipal population, funded partly by oil royalties — making it a working example of a commons-funded basic income.

The Welsh basic income pilot (2022-2024) provided GBP 1,600 per month to care leavers — young people leaving the care system at age 18. Though categorical rather than universal, it demonstrated the transformative effects of economic security during a critical life transition.

Standing draws several cross-cutting lessons: unconditional cash outperforms conditional cash on most outcomes; recipients do not reduce labour supply — they redirect it toward more productive activities; women benefit disproportionately when payments are individual; economic activity in communities increases because cash circulates locally; and the mental health effects of economic security are large and consistent across every pilot.

## How to Apply

1. **Match the pilot evidence to the claim.** Different pilots support different arguments. For the "laziness" objection, cite Finland and Madhya Pradesh (no reduction in labour supply). For women's empowerment, cite Madhya Pradesh (women's agency increased with individual payments). For community-level effects, cite Namibia (crime reduction, school attendance, local economic activity). For long-term effects, cite GiveDirectly (ongoing). For commons-funded financing, cite Marica (oil royalty funding).

2. **Apply the pilot comparability check.** Before generalising from one pilot to another context, check: are the income levels similar? The labour market structure? The state capacity? The cultural attitudes to cash? The Madhya Pradesh results generalise more readily to sub-Saharan Africa (similar income levels, similar informality) than the Finnish results (very different institutional context). Qualify generalisations appropriately.

3. **Address the methodological objections honestly.** The Namibian pilot was small and not randomised. The Finnish experiment was limited to the unemployed. GiveDirectly is funded by external donors, not domestic revenue. The Welsh pilot was categorical. No pilot is perfect. Acknowledge limitations while showing that the cumulative evidence, across diverse designs and contexts, points consistently in the same direction.

4. **Use pilots to inform design, not just to advocate.** The pilot record contains design lessons: individual payments outperform household payments (Madhya Pradesh); modest but regular payments outperform large but infrequent ones (cross-pilot comparison); digital payments reduce leakage (Marica, GiveDirectly); community-level saturation shows effects that individual-level randomisation misses (Namibia, GiveDirectly village-level randomisation).

## Examples

**Situation:** A Southern African government has commissioned a feasibility study for a basic income pilot. The Treasury is sceptical and has posed three questions: Will people stop working? Can we afford it? How do we know it will work in our context? The consultant needs to answer using pilot evidence.

**Application:** Address each question with specific pilot evidence. On labour supply: the Madhya Pradesh pilots showed that recipients worked more, not less — they used the security of basic income to invest in productive assets, start small businesses, and shift from casual to more stable work. The Finnish experiment showed no reduction in employment among recipients, contradicting the "laziness" hypothesis. The Namibian pilot showed increased economic activity — people used the income to buy seeds, materials for small enterprises, and transport to seek better work. Across every pilot, the evidence is consistent: basic income does not reduce labour supply. It redirects it toward more productive, more chosen activity.

On affordability: the Marica programme demonstrates that a basic income funded by natural resource revenues is fiscally sustainable — it has operated for over a decade, funded by oil royalties, and has expanded progressively. For a Southern African country with mineral wealth, the commons dividend model is directly applicable. Net cost (after accounting for administrative savings from eliminating means-testing) is substantially lower than gross cost.

On contextual relevance: the Madhya Pradesh and Namibian pilots are the most directly comparable — similar income levels, high informality, limited state capacity, rural populations. The results in these contexts were if anything stronger than in higher-income settings, because the marginal impact of a basic income floor is greatest where existing safety nets are weakest. The Namibian experience is the single most relevant data point for a Southern African pilot design. Use its methodology (community-level saturation, multi-domain measurement) and improve on its limitations (add randomisation, extend duration, increase sample size).

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Cite the Finnish experiment as evidence that basic income "works" without noting its limitations.
**Why:** The Finnish experiment was not a true basic income pilot — it targeted only the unemployed, was time-limited, and did not test universality. Its findings on wellbeing and conditionality are valuable, but it cannot answer questions about universal coverage, long-term effects, or community-level impacts. Using it uncritically invites legitimate methodological criticism.

**Don't:** Dismiss pilots as "too small" or "too short" to prove anything.
**Why:** This is the objection Standing hears most often from opponents, and it is designed to make the perfect the enemy of the good. No pilot can replicate the conditions of a national programme. But the cumulative evidence — from Namibia, India, Finland, Kenya, Brazil, Wales, and others — is consistent across diverse contexts and designs. The burden of proof has shifted: those who claim basic income will not work now bear the obligation to explain why the evidence from every pilot points in the same direction.
