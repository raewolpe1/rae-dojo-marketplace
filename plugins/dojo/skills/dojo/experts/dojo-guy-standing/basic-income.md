# Basic Income: Ethics, Design, and Empirical Record

## When to Use

- When a government, donor, or organisation is considering basic income or evaluating its feasibility
- When you need to distinguish basic income from the many policies it is confused with — negative income tax, universal credit, conditional cash transfers, targeted subsidies
- When making the ethical case for unconditional, universal, individual cash transfers grounded in commons justice

## Core Concept

Basic income, as Standing defines it, is a regular cash payment made to every individual in a political community, without means test and without conditions. It rests on five pillars: it is individual (paid to each person, not to households); universal (not targeted to categories of the "deserving poor"); unconditional (no behavioural requirements — no work tests, school attendance, health visits); regular (paid at predictable intervals, not as emergency lump sums); and in cash (not in-kind transfers like food stamps or vouchers).

This definition matters because the policy landscape is littered with things that are called basic income but are not. Negative income tax is household-based, means-tested, and involves a phase-out that creates poverty traps. Universal credit is conditional, sanctioned, and administered through a bureaucracy designed to police compliance. Conditional cash transfers require behaviour change as a condition of payment. Targeted subsidies are in-kind and categorical. Each of these fails at least two of the five pillars.

Standing's ethical case for basic income rests not on charity or poverty reduction but on justice. The commons — natural resources, the knowledge commons, social institutions built over generations — belong to everyone. Rentier capitalism has enclosed these commons for private extraction. Basic income is a commons dividend: each citizen's share of collectively created and inherited wealth. This reframes the entire debate. The question is not "can we afford to give money to the poor?" but "on what grounds do rentiers keep wealth that belongs to all of us?"

The design implications follow directly. Because it is a right, it cannot be conditional. Because the commons belong to individuals, not households, it must be individual. Because everyone inherits the commons, it must be universal. Because people need predictable security, it must be regular. Because freedom requires choice, it must be in cash.

## How to Apply

1. **Run the five-pillar test on any proposed transfer.** Map the policy against individual, universal, unconditional, regular, and cash. Identify which pillars it fails and what the consequences are — poverty traps from means-testing, exclusion from targeting, paternalism from conditionality, inflexibility from in-kind provision.

2. **Make the commons dividend argument explicit.** Identify the commons being enclosed in the specific context — natural resources, land, spectrum, knowledge, genetic material — and frame basic income as a dividend on that shared wealth. In resource-rich African countries, the link between extractive revenues and a citizens' dividend is particularly direct.

3. **Present the fiscal architecture honestly.** Gross cost is misleading. Net cost — the amount redistributed to people below the tax threshold minus the administrative savings from eliminating means-testing — is the relevant figure. Model it transparently and show the comparison with existing programme costs including administration.

4. **Build in evaluation from the start.** Standing insists that the case for basic income is empirical, not just theoretical. Any implementation should include rigorous evaluation — randomised where possible, longitudinal always — to build the evidence base.

## Examples

**Situation:** A Southern African government is redesigning its social protection system. The current architecture includes a means-tested child grant, a categorical disability grant, and a public works programme. Coverage is low (25% of eligible households for the child grant), administration costs are high (18% of programme budget), and exclusion errors are significant. The government wants to know whether consolidating into a basic income is feasible.

**Application:** Apply the five-pillar test to each existing programme: the child grant fails universality and unconditionality; the disability grant fails universality; the public works programme fails unconditionality, regularity, and the cash pillar (payment is delayed and irregular). Calculate the combined budget of all three programmes including administration. Model a universal individual transfer at a level set to 30-35% of the national poverty line — the level at which Indian and Namibian pilots showed significant welfare effects. The administrative savings from eliminating means-testing and compliance monitoring (typically 15-20% of programme costs) partially offset the expansion to universality. Frame the reform as a commons dividend funded partly through a natural resource levy — connecting the country's mineral wealth to every citizen's entitlement. Build in a two-year evaluation comparing outcomes in early-implementation districts against the existing programmes.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Present basic income as "just giving money to the poor" or frame it as an anti-poverty programme alone.
**Why:** This loses the commons justice foundation, makes the policy vulnerable to "dependency" and "affordability" attacks, and misses the structural critique of rentier capitalism that is the heart of Standing's case.

**Don't:** Concede that conditionality is "evidence-based" without examining the evidence critically.
**Why:** The evidence from direct comparisons (Zambia, Malawi, Kenya) shows unconditional transfers performing as well or better than conditional ones. The apparent success of Latin American CCTs is largely attributable to the cash, not the conditions — conditions in Bolsa Família were barely enforced. Conceding this ground surrenders the strongest empirical argument for unconditionality.
