# The Commons: Substrate of Rentier Capitalism

## When to Use

- When tracing how a specific resource — land, knowledge, genetic material, spectrum, data — has been enclosed from common use into private rent extraction
- When grounding the case for basic income in justice rather than charity by framing it as a commons dividend
- When analysing privatisation, intellectual property expansion, land grabs, or data monopolies through the lens of enclosure

## Core Concept

Standing's argument across *Plunder of the Commons*, *The Blue Commons*, and *The Politics of Time* follows a single structural logic: the commons — natural resources, the knowledge commons, social and cultural institutions, the ocean, and time itself — are being systematically enclosed by rentier capitalism, and basic income is the dividend owed for this theft.

The commons are not simply "public goods" in the economist's sense. They are inherited shared wealth — resources and institutions built, maintained, or sustained over generations by communities and by nature, to which every member of the political community has a legitimate claim. Land was historically common before enclosure created private property. Knowledge was shared before intellectual property regimes created artificial scarcity. Genetic material was common heritage before biopatents claimed ownership. The electromagnetic spectrum was a public resource before it was auctioned to corporations. Data generated by all of us is captured by platforms that contribute nothing to its creation.

Rentier capitalism is the system that profits not from production but from ownership of these enclosed commons. Landlords extract ground rent. Patent holders extract knowledge rent. Platform monopolies extract data rent. Financial institutions extract credit rent. None of these rents reflect productive contribution — they reflect control over access to resources that were once shared or that were created collectively. The rentier share of national income has grown in every OECD country, and in many developing countries, for four decades.

Standing connects this directly to both the precariat and basic income. The precariat is produced by enclosure: it is the class that has been excluded from the commons — from secure access to land, knowledge, time, and social institutions. Basic income is the response: a regular payment to every citizen as their share of the commons dividend. This is not redistribution in the conventional sense — taking from the productive rich to give to the unproductive poor. It is restitution — returning to people what was taken from them.

The policy implication is that funding basic income through commons levies — resource royalties, land value taxation, spectrum fees, data levies, financial transaction taxes — is not just pragmatically sensible but ethically coherent. The revenue source matches the ethical justification.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the specific commons being enclosed.** In any policy context, trace which commons — natural resources, land, knowledge, data, spectrum, genetic material — are being privatised and who is extracting the rent. This grounds the analysis in material reality rather than abstract theory.

2. **Quantify the rent where possible.** Land rents in major African cities, mineral royalties that accrue to licence-holders rather than citizens, intellectual property rents on pharmaceuticals, data rents extracted by platform monopolies — putting numbers on enclosure makes the abstract concrete.

3. **Frame basic income as a dividend, not a handout.** The language matters: a commons dividend is owed as a matter of right; a handout is offered as a matter of grace. The Alaska Permanent Fund is the working model — citizens receive a share of oil wealth not because they are poor but because they are citizens of a resource-owning community.

4. **Connect commons levies to basic income financing.** A natural resource royalty funds the dividend from enclosure of the natural commons. A land value tax funds the dividend from enclosure of the land commons. A digital services levy funds the dividend from enclosure of the data commons. The revenue instrument should match the enclosure.

## Examples

**Situation:** A resource-rich Central African country earns substantial revenue from mining concessions — cobalt, coltan, copper. The revenues flow to the national treasury but are captured by elite consumption, military spending, and corruption. Rural communities near mining sites bear the environmental and health costs. The country has no functioning social protection system. A development partner is advising on a natural resource governance reform.

**Application:** This is a classic case of commons enclosure. The mineral wealth is a national commons — shared inheritance of all citizens — but the concession system has privatised the returns while socialising the costs. Standing's framework prescribes a commons fund: a sovereign wealth mechanism (drawing on the Alaska, Norway, and Timor-Leste models but adapted to the institutional context) that captures a portion of mining revenues into a ring-fenced fund. The fund pays a regular individual dividend to every citizen — a basic income financed directly from the enclosed commons. This is not charity; it is restitution. The ethical case is that every citizen owns a share of the subsoil wealth and is entitled to a dividend. The practical case is that direct individual payments bypass the corruption that vitiates budget-funded programmes, create a constituency for transparent resource governance (citizens who receive a dividend have a direct interest in monitoring extraction), and provide economic security to the rural communities bearing the costs of extraction. The commons framing transforms the political economy: it shifts the question from "how do we distribute mining benefits more fairly?" to "who owns the minerals and how do we return their share?"

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat "the commons" as a vague metaphor for "public goods" or "things that should be shared."
**Why:** Standing's argument is historically and materially specific. Particular commons — particular pieces of land, particular knowledge systems, particular marine resources — were enclosed through identifiable legal and political processes at identifiable historical moments. The analytical power lies in this specificity. Vague appeals to "the commons" have no policy traction.

**Don't:** Propose commons levies without connecting them to the dividend.
**Why:** A resource royalty that flows into general revenue is just another tax. The commons framework requires that the revenue from the enclosed commons be returned to the commoners as a dividend. Without this link, the ethical architecture collapses and the policy becomes indistinguishable from conventional progressive taxation.
