## When to Use

Use this topic when someone wants to know how to find good new tools relevant to their work, when someone is building a practice of tool exploration and wants to do it more systematically, when someone wants to understand how to evaluate whether a tool is worth trying (not just worth knowing about), or when someone wants to learn how communities and peer networks contribute to tool discovery. This is the "meta-skill" behind Wonder Tools — how to run your own tool discovery practice.

## Fails When

Tool discovery advice fails when it provides a list of current tools rather than teaching the practice of finding tools — the list becomes stale almost immediately. It also fails when it encourages promiscuous tool adoption without clear criteria for what makes a tool worth trying, or when it treats discovery and adoption as the same thing (they're not — most discovered tools shouldn't be adopted, and the skill is knowing which ones should).

## Core Concept

Tool discovery is a practice, not an event. The landscape of tools changes continuously — new tools emerge, existing tools add features, business models shift, communities develop around specific tools. Maintaining awareness requires ongoing investment in discovery channels, not a one-time search. The goal is building an infrastructure of discovery that surfaces relevant tools without requiring constant active search.

**Your discovery channels should match your work context.** Tools that are transformative for journalists might be irrelevant to educators; tools that researchers love might be cumbersome for communicators. The most efficient discovery happens through communities of people doing similar work, because they're already filtering for relevance. Generic technology news is a less efficient source than specialized communities in your domain.

**Try before you evaluate.** The difference between a tool that looks good in a review and a tool that actually fits your workflow is only discoverable through use. Many tools that seem impressive in demonstrations feel awkward in actual work; some tools that seem basic become indispensable when integrated into your workflow. Evaluation requires use, even brief use, not just reading.

**A trial period deserves structure.** Unstructured tool trials — installing something, poking around, forgetting about it — are the least informative. A structured trial has a specific use case (I'm going to use this tool for X for two weeks), a success criterion (if it does Y, I'll keep it), and a defined exit point (I'll decide at the end of two weeks). This is how Caplan approaches Wonder Tools evaluations: specific use cases, honest reflection on what worked and what didn't.

**Word of mouth from trusted sources beats comprehensive reviews.** A trusted colleague who has actually used a tool for your specific kind of work and can tell you what it's like to use it daily is more valuable than a feature comparison table. Building a small network of people who share tool recommendations is a high-value discovery channel.

**Observation is a discovery channel.** Noticing what tools are used in screenshots people share, asking what tool someone used when you see impressive output, paying attention to what tools come up repeatedly in conversations you're having — this ambient observation is one of the richest discovery sources and requires no dedicated time investment.

**Discovery produces a pipeline, not a decision.** The output of good tool discovery practice is not "I found a great new tool"; it's "I have a pipeline of tools I want to try." Most discovered tools won't pass a structured trial; many won't even deserve a trial. The pipeline is the practice — a running list of tools to investigate when you have a relevant need or available time, not a commitment to adopt everything you discover.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify your highest-value discovery channels.** For most people, these are: one or two domain-specific communities (Slack groups, Discord servers, forums where people in your field discuss tools), one or two curated newsletters with good track records in your area, and a small set of trusted colleagues you exchange recommendations with. These channels should be deliberately chosen, not accumulated.

2. **Maintain a "to try" list distinct from a "to use" list.** Keep a simple running list of tools you want to evaluate, separate from the tools you're actually using. This gives you a pipeline to draw from when you have a relevant need (a new project, a workflow problem, available time for exploration). Tools on the list are not commitments — they're candidates.

3. **When you have a specific workflow problem, search for tools before building workarounds.** Before solving a problem with an elaborate workaround in existing tools, spend 15 minutes searching for tools that specifically address that problem. The solution often exists; the discovery skill is knowing to look.

4. **Structure your trials.** When you try a new tool, define in advance: What specific task am I using this for? How long will I try it? How will I decide if it's worth keeping? Without this structure, trials produce vague impressions rather than useful evaluations.

5. **Share what you learn.** Posting about a tool you've tried — even in a private Slack, even in a short note — forces you to crystallize what you think about it and adds to the discovery infrastructure of your community. People who share tool discoveries tend to get more recommendations in return.

6. **Revisit tools that weren't right the first time.** Tools evolve. A tool that didn't fit your workflow 18 months ago may have added exactly the features it was missing. A periodic revisit (once a year is enough) of tools you dismissed keeps your evaluation current.

## Examples

**Example 1: Building a Domain-Specific Discovery Practice**
A marketing consultant felt perpetually behind on new tools. She spent significant time reading general tech news but found few tools relevant to her specific work in content marketing. She shifted her discovery strategy: joined two Slack communities specifically for content marketers and newsletter writers, subscribed to two newsletters focused on marketing tools (cutting six general tech newsletters), and established a quarterly call with two trusted peers specifically to exchange tool recommendations. Within six months, she was finding more relevant tools with less time investment. The key was narrowing to domain-specific sources rather than broad technology coverage.

**Example 2: The Structured Trial**
A researcher wanted to try Obsidian after hearing about it repeatedly from colleagues. His previous tool trials had been vague: install, explore, abandon. This time he structured it: "I will use Obsidian as my primary notes app for reading and research for one month. Success criterion: at the end of the month, do I feel my reading notes are more connected and retrievable than before? I'll decide whether to continue on day 30." He kept a brief log of friction points and moments of clarity. At day 30 he had a clear answer (yes, worth continuing) based on concrete experience rather than vague impressions.

## Anti-Patterns

**Discovery as consumption.** Following many tool discovery sources and reading about many tools without ever trying any. Tool reading as a substitute for tool use. The output of a discovery practice should be occasional trials and occasional adoptions, not an accumulation of tool knowledge that never changes how you work.

**FOMO-driven adoption.** Adding new tools to your workflow because they're getting attention or because people you respect are excited about them, without a specific use case that would benefit. The excitement around a new tool is not evidence that it's right for your workflow.

**Never revisiting dismissed tools.** Deciding a tool isn't for you after a brief or poorly-structured trial and never reconsidering. Tools improve significantly. The tool that wasn't ready 18 months ago might be the best option today. A lightweight revisit practice prevents this from hardening into permanent blind spots.
