## When to Use

Use this topic when someone needs to do serious research — going beyond the first page of Google results, evaluating source credibility, managing citations and references, or fact-checking claims before publishing. Also applies to journalists, academics, and professionals whose work requires accurately representing what the evidence actually says.

## Fails When

Research tool guidance fails when it treats "found on the internet" as equivalent to "verified" — the volume of search results is not evidence of accuracy. It also fails when it ignores the significant variation in search engine quality for different research tasks (Google for current events, Google Scholar for academic literature, specialist databases for domain-specific research). And it fails when it doesn't address the emotional pull toward confirming what you already believe.

## Core Concept

Good research is not about finding information — it's about finding credible information, evaluating it honestly, and representing it accurately. Tools make each of these steps faster; they don't replace the critical thinking that makes research trustworthy.

**Search strategy matters more than search engine choice.** The most important research skill is constructing searches that find what you actually need. For academic literature: Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, PubMed, and JSTOR surface peer-reviewed work that general search misses. For grey literature and reports: use site: operators to search within domains (site:who.int, site:brookings.edu). For historical news: specialized newspaper archives. For current events: news aggregators and source-specific search. Knowing which database fits your question is more valuable than any particular tool.

**Primary sources beat secondary sources beat summaries.** This hierarchy matters practically. If you're writing about a study, read the actual study — not just the press release, news article, or social post about it. Each layer of interpretation adds opportunities for distortion. Tools like Unpaywall (browser extension that finds legal free versions of academic papers), Sci-Hub alternatives, and institutional library access exist specifically to reduce the friction of reaching primary sources.

**Citation management tools are underused and high-value.** Zotero (free, open source), Mendeley, and Paperpile save research time substantially over any career measured in years. They capture metadata automatically, store PDFs, generate formatted citations in any style, and sync across devices. The upfront investment of about two hours to set up a citation manager pays back within weeks. Researchers and journalists who build citation management habits early save enormous time over their careers.

**Fact-checking has specific tools designed for it.** For verifying images: Google Reverse Image Search, TinEye, and Yandex Image Search. For tracing the origin of viral claims: Snopes, PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, and the IFCN directory of verified fact-checkers. For verifying social media accounts and detecting fakes: CrowdTangle (for researchers), Social Blade, and platform-specific verification processes. For verifying locations in images: Google Street View, Wikimapia, and GeoGuessr-trained reverse geolocation.

**AI changes research in important and risky ways.** AI tools like Perplexity, ChatGPT with browsing, and various AI research assistants can accelerate literature review, help synthesize across sources, and surface related work. They also hallucinate citations, misrepresent study findings, and produce confident-sounding summaries of sources that don't say what the summary claims. AI is most safely used as a starting point for identifying search directions — not as a citable source and not as a substitute for reading primary sources.

**Research requires intellectual humility about what you don't know.** Confirmation bias is the most dangerous research error. The discipline of research includes actively seeking disconfirming evidence, noting the limits of what your sources establish, and being honest about uncertainty. No tool solves this — it's a practice.

## How to Apply

1. **Match your database to your research type.** General question: start with a broad search engine but don't stop there. Academic question: Google Scholar + at least one domain-specific database. Medical/health: PubMed, Cochrane Library, ClinicalTrials.gov. Policy/advocacy: organization websites, government databases, SSRN for preprints. Journalism: newspaper archives, Freedom of Information requests, court records.

2. **Install the research browser extensions.** Unpaywall (finds legal free PDFs of academic papers), Web of Science or Semantic Scholar citation tracking (shows which papers cite which), and Zotero Connector (captures citations with one click). These are small installs that dramatically reduce friction.

3. **Set up a citation manager before you need it.** Zotero is free, cross-platform, and the best default choice. Spend two hours learning it before your next project. Capture every source you read into it, even if you don't use it. The habit pays forward.

4. **Verify claims proportionate to their stakes.** A quick fact for a casual email: Google is fine. A claim going into a published piece or important document: find the primary source and read it. For medical or legal claims: add additional verification against authoritative sources.

5. **Trace viral or surprising claims to their origin.** Before amplifying surprising statistics or claims, ask: where did this actually come from? Run it through a fact-checking site, search for the original source, and check whether the original says what the summary claims. This takes five minutes and prevents significant errors.

6. **Build a source evaluation habit.** For any new source, ask: Who is the author and what are their credentials? Who funds this organization? What is the evidence basis — is it primary research, secondary synthesis, or opinion? Is there peer review or editorial oversight? Has this been independently corroborated? This doesn't need to be a checklist — internalize it as a default posture.

## Examples

**Example 1: The Newsletter Writer Who Almost Got Burned**
A journalist writing a weekly policy newsletter shared what she thought was a well-sourced statistic about education funding: "studies show X% of students..." She had found it in a policy brief, which cited a report, which cited a study. When a reader pushed back, she traced it back to the original: a working paper, not a peer-reviewed study, with a much more limited sample than the claim implied. She now has a rule: any statistic she includes in her newsletter, she traces to the primary source. She installed Unpaywall and uses Zotero religiously. The time cost is about 20% higher; the trust cost of a retraction is immeasurably higher.

**Example 2: The Academic Who Streamlined Literature Review**
A researcher doing a literature review for a grant proposal was drowning in papers — too many to read, uncertain which were most relevant. He started using Connected Papers (free tool that visualizes relationships between academic papers) to map the literature landscape before diving deep. He identified the foundational papers his subfield kept citing, read those first, and used their bibliographies to trace the most important threads. Combined with Zotero for capture and an Obsidian vault for synthesis notes, he completed a literature review in half the time of his previous approach.

## Anti-Patterns

**Citing whatever confirms your point.** Finding sources that support a conclusion you've already reached rather than letting the evidence shape the conclusion. This is motivated reasoning dressed as research, and sophisticated readers can recognize it.

**Treating AI-generated citations as real.** Using Perplexity, ChatGPT, or other AI tools to generate bibliographies without verifying that the cited papers exist and say what the AI claims. AI systems regularly fabricate plausible-sounding citations. Every AI-generated citation needs manual verification before use.

**Stopping at the summary.** Reading the headline, abstract, or news story about a study rather than the study itself, then citing "the research" as if you understand what it established. Abstracts are designed to communicate findings favorably; the actual methodology and limitations section often tells a more complicated story.
