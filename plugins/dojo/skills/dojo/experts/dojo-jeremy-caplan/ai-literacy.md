## When to Use

Use this topic when someone wants to understand what AI tools can and cannot do, wants to get better at using AI for real work, is frustrated that AI keeps failing them in specific ways, or is trying to decide whether AI is useful for a particular task. Also use when someone is either dismissing AI entirely or expecting it to be magic.

## Fails When

AI literacy guidance fails when it treats AI as either uniformly capable or uniformly useless — both extremes prevent honest evaluation. It also fails when it focuses only on prompting tricks rather than understanding underlying capabilities and limitations. And it fails when it ignores the important distinctions between different AI systems, assuming all "AI" is the same.

## Core Concept

AI literacy isn't about knowing which tools exist — it's about developing accurate mental models of what these systems actually do, so you can use them confidently where they help and skip them where they don't.

**The capability-limitation gap is the core literacy challenge.** People routinely expect AI to do things it can't do well (remember context reliably, count letters accurately, know what happened last week, reason through multi-step math), while under-using it for things it does remarkably well (rephrasing, brainstorming, explaining, synthesizing, generating options). Accurate literacy means knowing which side a given task falls on.

**AI as thinking partner, not oracle.** The most productive relationship with AI isn't "ask a question, get the answer." It's closer to working with a well-read but occasionally overconfident colleague who generates useful first drafts, surfaces options you hadn't considered, and helps you think through problems — but whose output you verify before acting on. AI changes what you do, not whether you think.

**Prompting is a skill, not a secret code.** There's no magic formula. What works is being explicit about your context, your constraints, your audience, and the format you want. Treat prompting like briefing a contractor: give them enough to do the job without leaving them guessing. The best prompt is often just a clear description of the actual situation.

**Calibrate your expectations to the task.** A rough taxonomy that holds up in practice:

- AI is reliably good at: rephrasing, summarizing, brainstorming options, explaining concepts, generating structured drafts, adapting tone, translating, converting formats
- AI is hit-or-miss at: factual recall (especially recent events, specific numbers, proper nouns), multi-step reasoning, following very complex instructions, maintaining consistency across long outputs
- AI struggles with: anything requiring real-time information, precise counting or arithmetic, deeply creative work that requires lived experience, ethical judgment in nuanced situations

**Verification is part of the workflow.** Using AI responsibly means building in checking. For factual claims, dates, statistics, and proper names — verify independently. For consequential decisions — don't let AI make them for you. The key is not paranoia about everything AI produces but proportionate verification calibrated to the stakes and the known weakness patterns.

**Privacy and dependency are real considerations.** What you put into AI tools matters. Confidential client information, sensitive personal data, and proprietary details deserve careful handling. Additionally, building workflows entirely dependent on one AI tool creates a fragility point. Good AI literacy includes thinking about what happens when the tool changes, fails, or disappears.

**The adoption curve is steeper than people expect.** Getting genuinely good at AI tools takes time — not because they're complicated, but because developing accurate intuitions about where they help and hinder requires experimentation. The people who use AI most effectively have usually gone through a period of deliberate exploration, including enough failures to understand the limitations.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify what type of task you're attempting.** Generation, summarization, reformatting, reasoning, factual retrieval? The task type predicts reliability better than any other variable. If you don't know where it falls on the capability map, run a small test before depending on the output.

2. **Brief, don't command.** Write prompts the way you'd brief a smart new colleague who doesn't know your context. Include: who you are, what you're trying to accomplish, who the audience is, what constraints matter, what format you want. Adjust based on what you get back.

3. **Iterate, don't retry.** If the first output isn't what you wanted, don't just ask again. Ask yourself what information you withheld, what constraint you forgot to mention, or whether the task is actually in AI's sweet spot. Change the prompt based on that diagnosis.

4. **Build in appropriate verification checkpoints.** Before using AI output consequentially — in a document others will read, in a decision you'll act on — run a quick mental check: Are there facts that could be wrong? Are there numbers I should verify? Is there a judgment call the AI shouldn't be making for me?

5. **Notice when AI is hurting rather than helping.** Track where it slows you down, produces outputs you have to fix extensively, or makes you less confident in your own thinking. These are signals that the task isn't a good fit, not that you're doing something wrong.

6. **Experiment systematically.** Pick one use case, try it for two weeks, evaluate honestly. Then expand or cut based on what you learned. Random experimentation without evaluation doesn't build literacy — it builds opinions.

## Examples

**Example 1: The Newsletter Editor**
A freelance editor was skeptical of AI because her first attempts at using it to "write" articles produced generic, forgettable drafts she had to rewrite anyway. She concluded AI wasn't useful for writing. On reflection with a literacy lens: she was asking AI to do the hardest part (generate original ideas and distinctive voice) while handling the parts she was already good at herself. She reframed the question: what parts of editing are tedious and mechanical? Answer: reformatting client submissions to house style, writing consistent section headers, generating headline options for A/B testing, and creating social captions from finished articles. She now uses AI for these tasks and saves four to six hours per week — without touching her actual editorial work.

**Example 2: The Research Analyst Who Over-Trusted**
A policy analyst at a think tank started relying on AI to draft literature summaries. The outputs looked confident and well-organized, so she used them with minimal checking. Then a colleague caught a subtle error: a study the AI cited had different findings than described. She investigated further and found that across several summaries, dates were occasionally off by a year, and some study details were plausible-sounding confabulations. The failure wasn't AI being untrustworthy across the board — it was a calibration failure. She now uses AI to generate a first-pass structure and surface themes, but verifies all specific citations and numerical claims against primary sources before they go into client documents.

## Anti-Patterns

**The magic prompt fallacy.** Believing that if you just find the right prompt structure, AI will reliably do almost anything. This leads to ever-more-elaborate prompts trying to compensate for capability limits rather than accepting that some tasks aren't a good fit for AI at this time.

**Uniform skepticism as intellectual cover.** Dismissing all AI tools as hype or fundamentally problematic, which lets someone avoid the more difficult work of figuring out where these tools genuinely help. This is often framed as principled concern but functions as avoidance.

**Outsourcing judgment.** Using AI to make decisions that require your judgment — about tone in a sensitive conversation, about whether a claim is accurate, about what recommendation to make — rather than using it to generate options your judgment then evaluates. This degrades the quality of the work while also degrading your own judgment over time.
