## When to Use

Use this topic when someone is feeling overwhelmed by the pace of tool and technology change and wants a sustainable strategy for staying informed, when someone wants to build a curated information diet rather than consuming everything, when someone is wondering how to evaluate newsletters and feeds about tools and technology, or when someone wants to understand how Jeremy Caplan thinks about the curation work he does in Wonder Tools and how that translates to their own staying-current practice.

## Fails When

Staying-current advice fails when it assumes that more information is always better, when it prescribes a specific set of sources without considering the person's context and interests, or when it treats the problem of information overload as a technical problem solvable by better tools rather than a judgment problem solvable by clearer priorities. You cannot follow every source worth following; the question is always which sources are worth following *for you* given your work, interests, and the time you're willing to invest.

## Core Concept

Staying current with tools and technology is fundamentally a curation problem, not an information problem. There is no shortage of information about new tools — the shortage is of trustworthy, well-filtered, contextually relevant information that helps you decide what's worth your attention. The challenge is building an information diet that gives you enough awareness to recognize relevant developments without overwhelming you with noise.

**Curation is a skill, and curators are valuable.** A good curator — someone who follows a domain closely, has developed judgment about what matters, and communicates clearly — saves you significant time. Finding a small number of curators you trust is more valuable than having many RSS feeds. Trust is the key variable: a curator whose judgment consistently tracks your interests is worth more than 10 sources whose signal-to-noise ratio is lower.

**Different sources serve different functions.** Some sources give you awareness (you learn a new tool exists); others give you evaluation (you learn whether a tool is worth trying); others give you depth (you understand a tool well enough to use it effectively). An information diet needs all three, but most people over-index on awareness and under-invest in evaluation and depth.

**The real cost of staying current is attention, not time.** The problem with following too many sources isn't that reading takes too long — it's that partial reading, tab-stacking, and guilt about unread items creates cognitive overhead that exceeds the time saved. A smaller, fully-processed information diet is usually more valuable than a larger partially-processed one.

**Secondary sources often outperform primary sources for efficiency.** Reading every AI company's blog post is less efficient than following three or four sharp analysts who read all of them and synthesize what matters. This is what good newsletters, podcasts, and curators do. The efficiency gain from good secondary sources is substantial.

**Serendipity needs to be protected.** Pure efficiency maximization in information consumption produces a narrow, predictable information diet — the algorithmic trap. Serendipitous discovery — following a link you didn't expect to follow, reading something tangentially related to your work, exploring a domain out of curiosity — is the source of unexpected connections and novel ideas. Any good information diet needs some slack for serendipity.

**The staying-current problem is iterative, not solved.** Sources go stale. Curators' interests diverge from yours. New curators emerge. The quality of newsletters varies over time. Staying current with how to stay current — auditing your information sources annually, pruning aggressively, making room for new additions — is part of the practice.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify your core use case for staying current.** Are you trying to keep up with a specific professional domain? Stay broadly informed about technology? Discover tools that might improve your workflow? Or stay current for a specific project? Different use cases favor different sources and update frequencies.

2. **Build a tiered source list.** Identify three to five sources you read every single item from (high trust, high relevance, high efficiency). Identify five to ten sources you scan the headlines of and click selectively. Let everything else go. Protect the high-trust, always-read tier jealously — that's where your attention goes, and it should earn its place.

3. **Choose newsletters over social media feeds for intentional learning.** Social media discovery is valuable but unpredictable and attention-expensive. Newsletters come on your schedule, maintain curation across issues, and don't penalize you for not checking in constantly. For deliberate staying-current practice, newsletters are more reliable than following people on social platforms.

4. **Schedule your information consumption.** Read newsletters and check feeds at set times rather than continuously. This batches the context-switching cost and reduces the drip of partial attention throughout the day. Many people find that a 20-30 minute newsletter reading session two to three times per week captures most of what they need.

5. **Create a fast-discard rule.** When you open a newsletter or feed item, make a quick decision: read now, save for later, or discard. The "save for later" category should be small and actioned regularly; otherwise it becomes a second inbox of guilt rather than a useful reading queue.

6. **Review and prune your sources quarterly.** Which sources are you actually reading? Which ones pile up unread? Which ones have introduced ideas or tools that mattered to your work in the last quarter? Add the best new sources you've encountered; remove sources that aren't earning their place.

## Examples

**Example 1: The Overwhelmed Teacher's Information Diet**
An educator was subscribed to 40 newsletters about edtech, AI in education, and classroom technology. She read about 10% of each, felt guilty about the 90% she didn't read, and still felt behind. She did a one-week audit: she noted every newsletter item she read fully and found it useful. Only six newsletters had items in her "read and found useful" pile. She unsubscribed from 30 newsletters and committed to reading the remaining ten fully, which took about 30 minutes twice a week. Six months later she reported knowing significantly more about what was actually happening in edtech because she was processing fewer sources more thoroughly.

**Example 2: The Wonder Tools Newsletter as a Model**
Caplan's Wonder Tools newsletter demonstrates a specific curation approach: a small number of tools per issue, each with enough description to evaluate usefulness, explicit framing of who the tool is for and what problem it solves, honest acknowledgment of limitations, and a consistent format that makes rapid scanning possible. Readers know what they're getting in each issue: a curated, evaluated set of tools with enough context to decide if any are relevant to them. This is the model for a good curation source — not comprehensiveness but selectivity, evaluation, and context.

## Anti-Patterns

**Following every source someone recommends.** When someone you respect recommends a newsletter or feed, subscribing immediately without testing whether it matches your interests. Better: put new subscriptions on probation for a month and evaluate whether you're actually reading them and finding them useful before committing to them.

**Using "saving for later" as a substitute for reading.** Building a large archive of saved articles, newsletters, and videos you plan to read someday. Most saved items are never read. The psychological overhead of a large unread queue is real. Saving less and reading more is usually a better allocation.

**Staying current as performance.** Following many sources and reading enough to be conversationally informed without going deep enough to actually apply anything. Knowing *about* many tools and understanding how to use a few are different. An information diet skewed entirely toward awareness and away from depth produces broad shallow knowledge rather than actionable expertise.
