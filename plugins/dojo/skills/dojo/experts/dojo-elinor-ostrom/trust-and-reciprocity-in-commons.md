# Trust and Reciprocity in Commons

## When to Use
- When the user attributes commons success to "social capital" or "community trust" as an exogenous factor.
- When debating how trust is built (or eroded) in user communities.
- When designing institutional features that depend on cooperative behaviour.

## Fails When
- **Treated as cultural endowment.** Trust is endogenous to institutional features; calling it "culture" obscures the analysis.
- **Confused with naive interpersonal warmth.** Trust in commons is functional — about expectations of rule-following — not affective.

## Core Concept

Trust is often invoked as the explanatory variable for why some communities sustain commons and others do not. Communities with high social capital cooperate; communities without it fail. The framing has empirical support but is analytically weak — it explains success by post-hoc labelling.

Ostrom's contribution is to identify the institutional features that produce trust and reciprocity, rather than treating these as cultural endowments. Trust is produced when:

**Interaction is repeated.** Users encounter each other again and again; defection has reputational consequences and cooperative behaviour accumulates trust.

**Identification is possible.** Users know who is who. Anonymous large groups produce less trust than identified medium-sized ones.

**Rules are visible and stable.** When rules are clear, followed by most, and enforced when violated, expectations are stable. Trust is the expectation that others will follow the rules.

**Monitoring is mutual.** When users observe each other, defection is detected; the credible threat of detection sustains cooperation; observed cooperation reinforces trust.

**Sanctions are graduated and credible.** When rule-breakers face proportionate consequences, users do not need to police obsessively; the system handles defection. Knowing the system works produces trust.

**Communication is open.** Users can talk about the resource, the rules, and disputes. Communication identifies problems before they fester, allows joint adaptation to changes, and reinforces shared norms.

Reciprocity is the action principle behind cooperation. Most users are conditional cooperators: they will follow rules if others do. Reciprocity-based cooperation can be sustained indefinitely if the institutional architecture supports it. Once the architecture decays — monitoring fails, sanctions are not imposed, defection goes unaddressed — conditional cooperators stop cooperating, and the commons collapses rapidly.

The implication for development practice is uncomfortable but important: building trust is the work of building institutions. There is no shortcut through "community development training" or "social-capital interventions" that bypass the underlying institutional features. Reform that fails to build the architecture will not produce trust no matter how much it talks about trust; reform that builds the architecture will produce trust as an output even if it never uses the word.

## How to Apply

1. **When low trust is identified as a problem, ask which institutional features are missing.**
2. **Resist "build trust" as a programme objective**; replace with the specific architectural features that produce trust.
3. **Track trust as an output, not an input.** Measure changes in cooperative behaviour, not affective surveys.

## Examples

**Situation:** A community-based water management programme in the Eastern Cape reports that "trust in the user association is low." The reflexive intervention is community-relationship workshops. What is the Ostrom alternative?

**Application:** The reflexive intervention treats trust as the binding constraint and tries to build it directly. The Ostrom alternative asks why trust is low: probably because rule-making is concentrated in a small executive, rules are not transparent to ordinary users, monitoring of water use is absent, sanctions for over-abstraction are inconsistent or unenforced, and disputes have no accessible resolution mechanism. These are the conditions under which conditional cooperators stop cooperating and trust accordingly falls. The reform programme should address these conditions — transparent rule-making, peer monitoring, graduated sanctions, accessible disputes resolution — rather than running workshops about trust. The workshops without the architectural changes will produce verbal commitments that decay within months; the architectural changes without workshops will produce sustained cooperative behaviour that registers, eventually, as trust.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat trust as the cause when it is the consequence.
**Why:** It produces interventions that try to engineer the consequence directly rather than building the conditions.

**Don't:** Assume trust is uniformly distributed across the user group.
**Why:** Subgroups may have very different trust patterns based on their different institutional experiences. Disaggregate.
