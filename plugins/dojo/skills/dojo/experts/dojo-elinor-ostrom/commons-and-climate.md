# Commons and Climate

## When to Use
- When the user is engaging with climate-change governance.
- When debating whether climate needs a single global treaty or polycentric action.
- When extending commons thinking to other global problems (oceans, atmosphere, biodiversity).

## Fails When
- **Read as suggesting climate is "just another commons."** The scale and properties of the climate system differ from local commons in important ways; Ostrom acknowledged this.
- **Treated as anti-Paris-Agreement.** Ostrom's polycentric framing is consistent with international agreement; it cautions against expecting agreement alone to solve the problem.

## Core Concept

Ostrom's later work (notably her 2009 World Bank Policy Research Working Paper "A Polycentric Approach for Coping with Climate Change") extended the commons framework to the largest commons of all: the global climate system. The argument has three parts.

**1. Climate is a commons.** Greenhouse-gas concentrations in the atmosphere are non-excludable (no one can be prevented from contributing to or benefiting from atmospheric composition) and rivalrous (each unit of emissions degrades the climate system's regulating function for everyone). The closest analogy is a global open-access commons under threat of degradation — Hardin's setup elevated to planetary scale.

**2. The expected single-treaty solution is harder than it looks.** Negotiating, ratifying, monitoring, and enforcing a comprehensive global treaty involves coordination among nearly 200 states with radically different emissions profiles, development needs, and political systems. The Kyoto experience and the long road to Paris demonstrated the difficulty. Even the Paris Agreement, the best result so far, works because it accommodates diverse national approaches rather than imposing a single rule. The standard expectation — "we need a comprehensive global treaty before anything serious can happen" — is empirically not how progress has been made.

**3. The polycentric alternative is more realistic and more robust.** Climate action emerges from multiple centres: national governments setting different policies, sub-national governments (cities, states, provinces) acting independently of national policy, sectoral agreements (aviation, shipping, finance), corporate commitments, civil-society pressure, international agreements that coordinate without commanding. Each centre addresses part of the problem. The aggregate effect may exceed what a single global authority could achieve.

The polycentric framing yields several practical implications.

- **Local action matters.** Even if global treaties stall, local commons-style governance of local emissions sources (urban transport, building standards, agricultural emissions) can deliver real reductions.
- **Multiple agreements are not failure.** A patchwork of national, regional, sectoral, and city-level commitments is what polycentric governance of a global commons looks like.
- **The design principles still apply, adapted.** Boundaries (which emissions, which jurisdictions), congruence (rules matching national circumstances), collective-choice (which parties decide), monitoring (UNFCCC inventories, civil-society tracking), graduated responses (carbon border adjustments, sanctions for non-participation), conflict resolution (dispute-resolution procedures in trade and climate regimes), recognition of right to organise (sub-national actors), nesting (local-national-international layers).

The framework does not promise success — climate is harder than the local commons Ostrom studied. But it offers a more realistic stance than the "comprehensive global treaty or nothing" framing.

## How to Apply

1. **For climate-relevant policy questions, look beyond the international-treaty level.**
2. **Map the polycentric structure** — what cities, regions, sectors, countries, agreements are acting?
3. **Identify gaps and coordination failures** rather than just the absence of global comprehensive action.
4. **Apply the design principles** to specific climate problems (e.g., national emissions reduction, sub-national adaptation, sectoral agreements).

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African policy team is debating whether to prioritise lobbying for stronger international climate commitments or implementing domestic just-transition measures more aggressively.

**Application:** The Ostrom framing treats this as a false dichotomy. Both matter, but neither needs to wait for the other. The polycentric structure means South African action contributes to climate outcomes regardless of the pace of international negotiations; international action also matters but it is not a prerequisite for domestic action. The more useful framing is: which actions, at which scales, with which partners (sub-national governments in South Africa, regional partners in SADC, sectoral initiatives, international agreements) best fit the just-transition objective? The polycentric framing allows acting on multiple fronts without making any one front a hostage to progress on others.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Wait for the comprehensive global agreement before acting.
**Why:** It may not come, and the time costs of waiting are paid in irreversible climate change.

**Don't:** Treat sub-national or sectoral action as a substitute for international cooperation.
**Why:** Both are part of the polycentric structure. Substitution arguments miss the architectural point.
