# The Tragedy of the Commons — Rebuttal

## When to Use
- When Hardin's 1968 *Science* article is invoked as established truth.
- When a policy argument depends on the inevitability of commons degradation.
- When the user assumes self-interest necessarily destroys shared resources.

## Fails When
- **Read as denying that commons can fail.** They can and do. The claim is that failure is contingent on institutional conditions, not inevitable.
- **Used to dismiss Hardin entirely.** Hardin posed a real problem; he just specified the wrong solution space.

## Core Concept

Garrett Hardin's 1968 parable describes herders sharing a common pasture. Each herder, rationally seeking to maximise his own gain, adds animals to the pasture. The benefit of an additional animal accrues entirely to him; the cost (overgrazing) is distributed across all herders. The "rational" outcome: each herder keeps adding animals until the pasture collapses. Hence the "tragedy" — and the implied prescription: convert the commons to private property (each herder owns his patch and bears the full cost of his own use) or to state property (a sovereign authority allocates use).

Ostrom's rebuttal has three layers.

**1. The empirical layer.** Hardin's parable assumes the herders cannot communicate, cannot make rules, cannot monitor each other, and cannot sanction free-riders. In actual commons systems, herders communicate, make rules, monitor, and sanction routinely. Across hundreds of documented cases — Swiss alpine pastures (some governed under the same rules for 500+ years), Japanese mountain commons, Spanish irrigation *huertas*, Maine lobster fisheries, Nepalese forest commons — communities have sustainably managed common-pool resources without privatisation or state takeover. The empirical record refutes the parable's predictive claim.

**2. The conceptual layer.** Hardin conflated **open-access regimes** (where anyone can use the resource and no rules govern access — these do tend to collapse) with **commons regimes** (where a defined community of users governs access with rules). The "tragedy" is real for open-access; it is not the default for commons. The conceptual move that opens the analytical space is to distinguish these two cases.

**3. The prescriptive layer.** Hardin offered two solutions: privatisation or government control. Ostrom's work establishes a third: self-governance by user communities. The third option is sometimes appropriate, sometimes not — but its existence changes the policy choice from a binary to a comparative institutional question.

## How to Apply

1. **When Hardin is invoked, distinguish open-access from governed commons.**
2. **Ask whether the actual governance arrangement looks like the parable** — do the users communicate, make rules, monitor, sanction? Almost always they do, at least to some degree.
3. **Treat privatisation and state control as candidate institutions among several**, not as default solutions.

## Examples

**Situation:** A consultancy is advising a Southern African government on rangeland degradation. The brief assumes communal grazing systems are inherently unsustainable and proposes individual title.

**Application:** The first question is whether the communal system has functioning rules-in-use governing stocking rates, seasonal movement, exclusion of outsiders, monitoring, and dispute resolution. Often these rules exist — sometimes formalised by traditional authority, sometimes informally — and have been undermined by external pressures (drought, land alienation, state policy that delegitimated local rule-making). Privatisation in such cases destroys the existing institutional architecture rather than improving it. The Ostrom alternative is to diagnose the actual institution, identify the design-principle failures, and reinforce the commons rather than dismantle it.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Cite Hardin as if his parable were empirical evidence.
**Why:** It is a thought experiment, and the empirical evidence is contrary.

**Don't:** Read Ostrom as romantic about commons.
**Why:** She documented many failed commons as well as successful ones; the framework is about what distinguishes them, not about commons being inherently good.
