# Ostrom's Eight Principles — In Detail

## When to Use
- When the user is operationalising the design principles for a specific commons institution.
- When the user wants the substantive content of each principle beyond the headline.
- When debating how each principle applies to a specific case.

## Fails When
- **Confused with the design-principles overview file.** That file gives the overview; this one goes deeper into each.
- **Treated as a one-size-fits-all checklist.** Each principle requires contextual instantiation.

## Core Concept

Detailed treatment of each principle.

**Principle 1 — Clearly defined boundaries.** The community of users and the resource itself must be identifiable. Membership criteria specify who has rights (often inherited, sometimes purchased, sometimes granted by community decision). Geographic and material limits of the resource are agreed and known. Where boundaries are unclear, free-riding by outsiders is undetectable and rule-following by insiders is unrewarded.

**Principle 2 — Congruence between rules and local conditions.** Both appropriation rules (how much, when, by whom) and provision rules (contributions to maintenance, labour, capital) must reflect local ecology, technology, and social structure. A rotation schedule appropriate to a perennial river is inappropriate for an intermittent one. A rule developed in a Swiss village is unlikely to fit Andean conditions even for a similar resource.

**Principle 3 — Collective-choice arrangements.** Users affected by the rules can participate in modifying them. This is not equivalent to formal democracy; it is closer to functional rule-making access. The crucial point: rules are not handed down from authorities insulated from the conditions they govern. Reform of rules in response to changing conditions is itself a design feature.

**Principle 4 — Monitoring.** Compliance with rules is observed and reported on. The monitoring is typically done by the users themselves or by people directly accountable to them. External inspectors with infrequent visits are not the model; mutual peer observation is. Strong monitoring is the foundation of credible sanctioning.

**Principle 5 — Graduated sanctions.** Rule violators face sanctions, but the sanctions are graduated. A first-time minor violation usually triggers a small fine or warning; repeated violations or major ones trigger escalating responses. Zero-tolerance systems tend to fail because users won't impose disproportionate sanctions on neighbours; lax systems fail because there is no real cost. Graduation reflects the social reality of repeated interaction.

**Principle 6 — Conflict-resolution mechanisms.** Disputes among users and between users and enforcers have low-cost, locally accessible resolution procedures. Where disputes can only be resolved through distant courts or external arbitration, conflicts fester, compliance erodes, and the commons collapses. Often these mechanisms are informal — village elders, user-association committees, traditional councils — and the informality is part of their accessibility.

**Principle 7 — Minimal recognition of rights to organise.** External authorities recognise (or at least do not actively undermine) the local right to self-organise. Where state authorities preempt local rule-making, even nominally "to help," commons institutions tend to wither. The Communal Land Rights Act experience in South Africa, where state intervention into customary tenure created legal complexity without empowering local governance, is one Southern African illustration.

**Principle 8 — Nested enterprises.** For commons that are part of larger systems — fisheries within ocean systems, irrigation within catchments, forests within landscapes — the institution is nested in multi-level governance with appropriate division of authority and information across levels. Local rules for local conditions; regional rules for regional pollutants and externalities; national rules for genuinely national concerns.

## How to Apply

1. **For each principle, ask: present, partial, or absent?**
2. **For absent principles, identify the proximate cause and the institutional path to reform.**
3. **For partial principles, ask what would tip them to present.**
4. **Build the reforms in the order that unlocks the most other principles.** Often principle 3 (rule-making) is the gateway — without it, principles 4 and 5 cannot be sustained.

## Examples

**Situation:** A water-user association in a Western Cape catchment has poor compliance with abstraction limits and frequent conflict among irrigators.

**Application:** Detailed audit:
- Principle 1: Boundaries are clear (the WUA jurisdiction is defined).
- Principle 2: Rules generic — abstraction limits drawn from national templates, not calibrated to local hydrology.
- Principle 3: Decision-making concentrated in a small executive committee; broader users feel rule-making is closed.
- Principle 4: Monitoring nominally by the catchment management agency; rare in practice.
- Principle 5: Statutory fines, not graduated; enforcement rare.
- Principle 6: Disputes go to water tribunals — distant and slow.
- Principle 7: State recognises the WUA but tightly constrains its decision space.
- Principle 8: Nesting weak — relationship to national framework unclear.

Diagnosis: Six of eight principles weak. Reform sequence: open up collective-choice (principle 3); enable peer monitoring (principle 4); calibrate rules to local conditions (principle 2); introduce graduated sanctions (principle 5).

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the principles as theological commitments.
**Why:** They are empirical regularities. If your specific case suggests a different combination works, document it — Ostrom's own work celebrated such variation.

**Don't:** Apply all eight at once via a reform plan.
**Why:** Sequencing matters. Reforming everything simultaneously usually fails because the institutional foundations are not in place to support the surface changes.
