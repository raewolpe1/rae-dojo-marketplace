# Polycentric Governance

## When to Use
- When debating whether a problem needs centralised or decentralised governance.
- When the question is how multiple authorities can act on the same problem.
- When the user assumes that "one problem, one authority" is the right framing.

## Fails When
- **Confused with simple decentralisation.** Polycentricity is not the absence of higher-level governance; it is the productive coexistence of multiple levels.
- **Treated as romantic celebration of fragmentation.** Polycentric systems require coordination; the claim is that the coordination is more robust than monocentric alternatives, not that fragmentation is good in itself.

## Core Concept

The term *polycentric* originated with Vincent Ostrom, Charles Tiebout, and Robert Warren in their 1961 paper on metropolitan area governance. They were arguing against the then-prevailing view that metropolitan areas needed unified consolidated governments to function effectively. The alternative they articulated: multiple centres of decision-making — municipalities, special districts, regional bodies, voluntary associations, courts — making decisions independently but with mutual awareness, in ways that produced surprisingly effective service delivery without any single centre being in charge.

Elinor Ostrom extended this framework substantially. **Polycentric governance** is characterised by:

- **Multiple centres of decision-making.** Authorities at different scales (local, regional, national, international), in different sectors, in different forms (public, private, civic).
- **Formal independence among centres.** Each centre has its own legal personality, jurisdiction, and rule-making authority within its scope.
- **Functional interaction.** Centres communicate, contract, monitor each other, exchange information, compete for legitimacy.
- **No single sovereign over the whole system.** Coordination occurs through multiple mechanisms — markets, hierarchies where they apply, networks, mutual adjustment — rather than through unified command.

The advantages over monocentric governance:

**Redundancy.** Multiple centres acting partially independently mean that failure at one centre does not cascade to total failure.

**Information and innovation.** Different centres face different conditions and try different approaches; the system as a whole learns faster than any single centre could.

**Error correction.** Centres can challenge each other; mistakes are caught more reliably than in unitary systems.

**Legitimacy.** Multiple centres provide multiple channels for participation, complaint, and recourse.

The disadvantages: coordination costs, jurisdictional disputes, potential for inefficiency through duplication, occasional gaps where no centre is responsible. These are real, but Ostrom's argument is that they are usually overstated, and that monocentric systems have analogous (and often worse) coordination problems.

The framework applies at all scales. Local commons governance is typically polycentric — user associations, traditional authorities, municipalities, sector regulators all play roles. Climate governance is polycentric — national pledges, sub-national initiatives, sectoral agreements, voluntary corporate commitments, international treaties all contribute. The question is rarely whether to be polycentric (most consequential governance is) but how to make polycentric arrangements work better.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the centres of decision-making that bear on the problem.**
2. **Map their formal authorities and informal influences.**
3. **Look for coordination failures and look at the existing coordination mechanisms.**
4. **Resist the urge to consolidate.** Often the right reform strengthens individual centres and the connections among them, not unifying them.

## Examples

**Situation:** A donor is funding climate adaptation in a Southern African country. The implementing partner wants to "coordinate" by consolidating decision-making into a single agency.

**Application:** Polycentric framing pushes back. The adaptation problem touches agriculture (Department of Agriculture, agricultural extension services, commodity associations), water (Department of Water and Sanitation, catchment management agencies, water-user associations), disaster management (provincial and municipal), urban infrastructure (municipalities, civil society), and climate finance (Treasury, donors). Consolidating these into one agency would either fail (the existing centres would not actually surrender their authority) or destroy productive variation (loss of sectoral expertise and local responsiveness). The better reform is to map the existing polycentric structure, identify coordination gaps, and build cross-centre mechanisms — joint planning processes, shared data platforms, harmonised indicators — that strengthen the existing centres rather than replace them.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Equate polycentric with decentralised.
**Why:** Polycentric systems often have strong centres at high scales; the key is the coexistence of multiple centres, not the absence of national ones.

**Don't:** Assume polycentricity emerges naturally.
**Why:** It does emerge, but it can also be undermined by consolidation efforts. Active institutional work is required to sustain productive polycentricity.
