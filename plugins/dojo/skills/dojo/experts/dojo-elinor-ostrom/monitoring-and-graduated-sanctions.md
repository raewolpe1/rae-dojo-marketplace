# Monitoring and Graduated Sanctions

## When to Use
- When the user is designing enforcement architecture for a commons or other collective institution.
- When current enforcement is failing (either lax or zero-tolerance).
- When debating who should monitor and how violations should be sanctioned.

## Fails When
- **Treated as a generic call for stricter enforcement.** The argument is structural — about who monitors and how sanctions escalate — not about being harsher.
- **Confused with formal legal enforcement.** Effective commons monitoring is usually informal and peer-based, even where backed by legal authority.

## Core Concept

Design principles 4 and 5 are tightly coupled. **Monitoring** (principle 4) is the observation of compliance with rules; **graduated sanctions** (principle 5) is the response to detected violations. Together they constitute the enforcement architecture, and they are typically the practical hinge between durable commons and failing ones.

**Why peer monitoring works.**

In enduring commons, users themselves monitor — fishers watch other fishers, farmers watch other farmers, herders watch other herders. Three reasons this is more effective than external monitoring:

- **Information advantage.** Users know what the resource normally looks like and what suspicious behaviour signals. Inspectors do not, except after long apprenticeship.
- **Low cost.** Users are already on site; monitoring is a by-product of normal activity. External monitoring requires dedicated trips, equipment, and time.
- **Credible follow-through.** Users have skin in the game. Each detected violation, unsanctioned, costs the monitor directly through resource degradation. External monitors have weaker incentives to escalate.

**Why graduated sanctions work.**

Three reasons:

- **Proportionality.** Most rule violations are minor (forgetting, misjudging, occasional carelessness). Heavy sanctions for minor violations are not credible — users will not impose them on neighbours. Graduated sanctions match offence severity and so are imposable.
- **Repeat-game discipline.** A first warning costs the violator reputation; a second penalty costs more; persistent violation can ultimately trigger exclusion. The escalation creates an incentive to return to compliance early.
- **Social embeddedness.** Sanctions imposed by neighbours carry social meaning beyond their material content. The same fine from a distant authority and from one's neighbours has different behavioural effects.

**The classic graduated structure.**

- **First minor violation:** Informal warning from a peer or low-level authority.
- **Repeat minor violation:** Small formal fine.
- **Major violation or persistent repetition:** Larger fine and public acknowledgement.
- **Persistent serious violation:** Exclusion from the user community.

The structure adapts to the resource and community. In some commons the graduations are formal and codified; in others they are informal and discretionary. What matters is the graduation itself — not the specific schedule.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify who currently monitors.** If no one in the user community does, this is the binding constraint.
2. **Examine the sanction structure.** Are sanctions uniform and rare? Or graduated and routine?
3. **Build the missing layers.** Move from external inspection to peer monitoring; introduce graduations between zero and severe.

## Examples

**Situation:** A communal-grazing area in a former homeland zone of South Africa faces overstocking. The current "enforcement" is sporadic visits by livestock-department officials.

**Application:** This is monitoring with no graduated sanctions and no peer involvement. The Ostrom reform: assign monitoring responsibility to the user community itself (perhaps through a livestock owners' association linked to the traditional authority), introduce a graduated sanction schedule (informal warning for first instance of over-stocking; modest fine for repeated; required reduction in herd size for persistent; ultimately, exclusion from the commons in extreme cases), and provide credible backing from the traditional and state authorities for the sanctions imposed. The reform requires building peer-monitoring norms, which is slow work — but the absence of these features is why the current system fails.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Outsource monitoring to state inspectors as the default solution.
**Why:** It is more expensive, less informationally rich, and less credibly enforced than peer monitoring in most commons.

**Don't:** Use uniform statutory fines as the only sanction.
**Why:** Uniform fines under-discipline minor violations (the fine isn't worth imposing) and over-punish accidental ones (the fine is imposed regardless of cause). Graduation matches the actual variation in offences.
