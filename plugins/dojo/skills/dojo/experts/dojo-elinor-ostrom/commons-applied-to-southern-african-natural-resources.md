# Commons Applied to Southern African Natural Resources

## When to Use
- When the user is working on a Southern African commons case (rangeland, fisheries, water, forests, wildlife).
- When debating CBNRM (community-based natural resource management) programmes in the region.
- When analysing the tension between customary commons and statutory frameworks.

## Fails When
- **Treated as importing the global Ostrom framework unchanged.** Southern African contexts have specific colonial and post-colonial features that condition how the framework applies.
- **Used to romanticise traditional governance.** Customary systems can be patriarchal, exclusionary, and degraded; the framework supports honest analysis, not nostalgia.

## Core Concept

Southern Africa offers a rich set of commons cases that test and extend Ostrom's framework. Five domains illustrate the applicability and the limits.

**1. Communal rangelands.** Across the former homeland areas of South Africa, in much of Botswana, Zimbabwe, Namibia, and Lesotho, livestock-grazing land is held under customary commons arrangements. Traditional rules govern seasonal movement, herd size, water-point use, and exclusion of outsiders. These rules have been variously weakened by colonial land alienation, post-colonial state restructuring (the Communal Land Rights Act experience in South Africa, the Communal Areas Management Programme for Indigenous Resources — CAMPFIRE — in Zimbabwe), drought, urbanisation, and shifts in livestock economy. The Ostrom diagnostic: where the design principles are intact, the commons works; where they have been undermined, degradation follows. The reform path is institutional, not technical.

**2. Inshore and small-scale fisheries.** Coastal communities along the South African, Mozambican, Tanzanian, and Namibian seaboards have customary fisheries rules — gear restrictions, seasonal closures, exclusion of outside fishers. National fisheries legislation usually overrides these customary rules with quota or licensing systems. The pattern: where co-management has formally recognised customary rules (some Mozambican community fisheries, parts of the South African Small-Scale Fisheries Policy), outcomes improve; where statutory frameworks displace customary commons, both compliance and stocks suffer.

**3. Water and irrigation.** South Africa's National Water Act framework establishes water-user associations as the local commons governance layer. Performance is mixed — strong in some established irrigation areas, weak in many new ones. The diagnostic is design-principle-by-design-principle: where boundaries are clear, rules calibrated locally, monitoring active, sanctions graduated, conflict resolution accessible, the WUAs function. The Lesotho Highlands Water Project and the Limpopo and Orange-Senqu basins add a transboundary nesting layer with its own governance challenges.

**4. Forests and woodlands.** Miombo woodlands across central Southern Africa support millions of users through fuelwood, medicines, food, and timber. State forestry agencies have historically treated these as state property; CBNRM programmes (Mozambique's *delimitação de terras comunitárias*, Zambia's joint forest management, Tanzania's CBFM) attempt to recognise community commons rights. Outcomes vary with the design-principles instantiation; where recognition is real and local rule-making is enabled, results are better.

**5. Wildlife and conservation areas.** CAMPFIRE in Zimbabwe, Namibian communal conservancies, the South African transfrontier parks — all attempt commons or co-management arrangements over wildlife resources. The Namibian conservancies are the most-studied success: legal recognition, devolved rights, monitoring, conflict resolution, and nesting in regional and national frameworks. The CAMPFIRE experience shows what happens when recognition is partial, rule-making remains with central authorities, and benefits are captured upward.

The Southern African experience teaches three lessons that extend Ostrom's framework. **Colonial legacy** matters specifically — the alienation of customary commons land, the imposition of administrative boundaries that do not match resource or community boundaries, the displacement of customary rule-making by colonial-era statutes — all condition what reform is possible. **Statutory recognition** is necessary but not sufficient — the design-seventh principle (recognition of rights to organise) is the binding constraint in many cases. **Hybrid institutions** are the norm — pure customary or pure statutory rarely exist; effective governance is usually a hybrid that admits both.

## How to Apply

1. **For a Southern African commons case, audit using the eight design principles** with explicit attention to colonial overlays.
2. **Map the formal and informal authority structures.** Both matter; reform that addresses only one usually fails.
3. **Look for hybrid arrangements that work.** Pure customary or pure statutory rarely succeed; the hybrid space is where to design.

## Examples

**Situation:** A donor proposes to support a Southern African government in reforming its communal-land system. The brief mentions "secure tenure" and "productive use."

**Application:** Ostrom-shaped intervention: distinguish secure tenure (which can be commons-secure, not just individually-secure) from titling (which is one form of security among several); push back on the implicit assumption that productive use requires individual titles; conduct design-principles audits of the existing communal-land institutions to identify which are functioning, which weakened, and what reforms would strengthen them; ensure reforms preserve and recognise commons rights rather than dismantling them; provide statutory backing for community rule-making, monitoring, and conflict resolution; build nesting that connects communal-land governance to the national land-administration framework. The work is institutionally hard; the alternative — dismantling commons by titling — has a long, mostly negative track record in the region.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Equate "secure tenure" with "individual title."
**Why:** Commons can provide secure tenure to the user community; the conflation forecloses an institutional option that often fits better.

**Don't:** Treat customary governance as monolithically traditional or unchanging.
**Why:** Customary systems have evolved continuously; they are not pre-modern relics and they are not static. The Ostrom framework supports analysing what they actually do today.
