# Beyond Markets and States

## When to Use
- When policy debates are framed as a choice between privatisation and nationalisation.
- When the user assumes the only institutional options are markets and states.
- When self-governance is being dismissed as unrealistic or pre-modern.

## Fails When
- **Read as anti-market or anti-state.** Ostrom is neither. She is pluralist about institutional arrangements.
- **Treated as romanticising community.** Self-governance can fail; the claim is that it is a real option that often works, not that it always does.

## Core Concept

The dominant 20th-century framework for thinking about economic institutions treated markets and states as the two principal organising structures: market exchange under property rights for goods that can be privately owned and traded, and state provision (or regulation) for goods that cannot. The framework was elaborated by Samuelson on public goods, by Buchanan and Tullock on public choice, by the Coase theorem and its critics.

Ostrom's work establishes a third option as analytically and empirically distinct: **self-governance by user communities under their own institutional arrangements**. Hundreds of documented cases show such arrangements producing sustainable outcomes for centuries. The arrangements are not pure markets (they involve collective rule-making and non-market norms) and not pure states (they emerge from and are sustained by the user community, not imposed by sovereign authority).

The implication is a different policy question. The market-vs-state framing asks: should we privatise or nationalise? The Ostrom framing asks: what governance arrangement — including self-governance, hybrids, and combinations of all three — fits this resource, this community, this context?

Three concrete consequences:

**1. Privatisation is not always the answer.** Private property is one institutional arrangement. It works well for many goods; it works poorly for others. Imposing it on commons that were working creates transaction costs, displaces existing institutional architecture, and frequently produces worse outcomes. The Soviet collectivisation, the colonial enclosures, the IMF-era African land reforms — examples where forced privatisation or nationalisation destroyed working commons institutions.

**2. State control is not always the answer.** State management has informational and accountability problems that often match or exceed those of communities. State officials may be distant from local conditions; they may have weak incentives to monitor; they may be politically captured. State management of fisheries, forests, and water has produced as many failures as community management has.

**3. Self-governance is a legitimate institutional choice.** Where the design principles can be met, self-governance is often superior to either market or state alternatives. Where they cannot be met, self-governance fails; one of the other arrangements is then preferable. The question is comparative-institutional, not ideological.

Hybrids are common and often desirable. Co-management — partial devolution from the state to user communities — is one hybrid. Markets within commons — quota systems, transferable use rights inside a commons-governed framework — are another. The institutional pluralism the framework enables is not a slogan; it is a serious analytical and policy stance.

## How to Apply

1. **When privatisation or nationalisation is proposed, add the self-governance option to the comparison.**
2. **Ask which design principles can be met by each option.**
3. **Consider hybrids explicitly.** Mixed arrangements are often the right answer.
4. **Be honest about the conditions under which each works.** No arrangement is universally superior.

## Examples

**Situation:** A Southern African national fisheries policy proposes converting open-access small-scale fisheries to a private-quota system to reduce overfishing.

**Application:** The Ostrom alternative is to ask whether the existing arrangement can be moved toward a commons rather than toward private quotas. Commons arrangements — territorial use rights for fisheries (TURFs), community quotas, co-management with state recognition of community rules — have produced sustainable outcomes in many comparable cases (Japan, Maine, Chile, parts of the Philippines). They retain access for the existing fishing community (whom privatisation typically dispossesses), engage local knowledge in monitoring, and can be calibrated to local stock conditions. The trade-off: commons arrangements require institutional development that private-quota systems do not. Whether the trade-off is worth making depends on the community, the resource, and the political environment.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Default to one of the three options ideologically.
**Why:** The question is empirical and contextual. Different goods, different communities, different histories produce different answers.

**Don't:** Treat self-governance as romantic or pre-modern.
**Why:** Many of the world's most enduring institutions — Swiss alpine commons, Spanish *huertas*, Japanese coastal fisheries — are self-governed and have outlasted multiple national-level reforms.
