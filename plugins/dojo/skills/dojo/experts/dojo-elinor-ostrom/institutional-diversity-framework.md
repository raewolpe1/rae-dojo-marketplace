# The Institutional Diversity Framework

## When to Use
- When a one-size-fits-all reform is being proposed across diverse contexts.
- When best-practice case studies are being lifted across countries with weak attention to fit.
- When the user is asking why a successful institution in one place fails in another.

## Fails When
- **Read as relativism.** Institutional diversity does not imply all arrangements are equally good. The framework gives criteria for assessment.
- **Treated as anti-reform.** Diversity-respecting reform is harder, not impossible. The argument is for contextual fit, not stasis.

## Core Concept

Ostrom's *Understanding Institutional Diversity* (2005) is the methodological statement of a stance running through her whole work: institutions are diverse, and the diversity is not noise to be eliminated but variation to be analysed. Three connected propositions.

**1. Institutions are configurations of rules.** Not single rules but bundles, interacting in ways that produce outcomes neither rule alone would predict. Ostrom distinguished seven categories of rules: position rules (who can occupy what positions), boundary rules (entry and exit), choice rules (what actions positions can take), aggregation rules (how decisions are made), information rules (what information is available), payoff rules (rewards and sanctions), and scope rules (what outcomes are allowed). Institutional design works on the bundle, not on individual rules in isolation.

**2. Multiple configurations can produce similar outcomes.** Different rule bundles can produce sustainable fisheries; different rule bundles can produce reliable irrigation; different rule bundles can produce sound forestry. The same outcome from different institutions is not surprising; it is the empirical norm.

**3. Outcomes depend on the fit between institutions and conditions.** A rule bundle that works in conditions A may fail in conditions B. The variables that matter — resource characteristics, user community size and heterogeneity, broader political environment, historical trajectory — combine to produce conditions that fit some institutional bundles and not others. The work of institutional analysis is to specify which bundles fit which conditions.

This stance has methodological implications. **Case studies and small-n comparison** are at least as informative as large-n statistical work, because the relevant variation is in configurations and contexts that large-n work tends to flatten. **Within-case mechanism work** matters more than cross-case correlation, because the same outcome emerges from different mechanisms in different cases. **Best-practice transfer** is dangerous unless the receiving context has the conditions for the practice to work.

For development practice the implication is sharp. Programmes designed around "global best practice" — co-management templates, water-user-association templates, community-driven-development templates — fail repeatedly because the contextual fit is missing. The right move is not to abandon templates but to use them as starting points subject to local institutional design work.

## How to Apply

1. **When a template is proposed, ask what conditions it requires for success.**
2. **Check whether those conditions exist in the target context.**
3. **Adapt the template to local conditions explicitly.**
4. **Resist the framing "evidence-based" as if best practice from elsewhere were automatically applicable.**

## Examples

**Situation:** A development agency wants to introduce a community-forest-management model that worked in Nepal into a rural area of a Southern African country.

**Application:** The Nepal model worked under specific conditions: relatively cohesive village communities, weak but supportive state recognition, agricultural-pastoral livelihood mix, history of customary forest use. The Southern African context may share some of these (customary use history) and differ on others (community cohesion may be lower after migration; state recognition may be more ambivalent; livelihood structures may be different). Lifting the model wholesale will produce paper compliance without behaviour change. The right move is to use the Nepal experience as input — what design principles it instantiated, what conditions made it work — and design the local institution to instantiate the same principles in the local context. The institution will look different; the underlying logic is the same.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat institutional diversity as an obstacle to scaling.
**Why:** Scaling requires building the same institutional logic in different contexts, not replicating the same surface form. Institutional diversity is what scaling actually looks like.

**Don't:** Use "context matters" as a refusal to act.
**Why:** Context matters as a discipline on action, not as an excuse against it.
