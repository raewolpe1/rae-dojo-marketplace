# Collective Action Problems

## When to Use
- When the user is using a one-shot prisoner's-dilemma framing to predict failure of cooperation.
- When the question is under what conditions cooperation emerges and persists.
- When debating whether self-interest necessarily defeats collective action.

## Fails When
- **Read as denying that collective action problems are real.** They are real; the claim is that they are solvable, not that they are illusory.
- **Mistaken for a generic appeal to "trust" or "social capital."** The argument is more specific: identifiable institutional conditions produce cooperation, and absent them, cooperation does not emerge.

## Core Concept

The standard rational-choice framing of collective action — Mancur Olson's *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965), the one-shot prisoner's-dilemma model — predicts pervasive free-riding. If each individual gains from defecting regardless of others' choices, rational individuals defect, and collective goods are not produced. Olson concluded that large groups cannot organise without selective incentives or coercion.

The empirical record refutes the strong form. Communities routinely organise to produce collective goods, manage commons, fund collective infrastructure, and discipline free-riders — without selective incentives and without external coercion. The puzzle for theory is not whether cooperation occurs (it clearly does) but under what conditions.

Ostrom's framework, drawing on experimental work and field research, identifies several mechanisms.

**Repeated interaction.** The one-shot game predicts defection; the repeated game permits reciprocity-based cooperation. Most real commons interactions are repeated, often over generations.

**Communication.** Even in laboratory experiments, face-to-face communication before action dramatically increases cooperation rates — by typically 30–50 percentage points. Communication does not bind, but it builds shared expectations and identifies defectors.

**Heterogeneity of actors.** Real groups contain conditional cooperators (will cooperate if others do), unconditional cooperators (will cooperate regardless), and free-riders. The mix matters: a small share of conditional cooperators, supported by a smaller share of unconditional ones, can sustain cooperation that pure free-riders alone would destroy.

**Identification and monitoring.** Knowing who is contributing and who is not allows reciprocity. Anonymous large groups cooperate less than identified medium-sized ones.

**Norms and rules.** Internalised norms (you shouldn't cheat) and externally enforced rules (cheating brings sanctions) reduce the share of defection in equilibrium.

The prescription is institutional. Cooperation does not depend on people being naturally cooperative; it depends on institutional features that enable repeated interaction, communication, identification, and norm-enforcement. The eight design principles are, in this sense, a theory of how to construct the conditions under which collective action problems are solved.

## How to Apply

1. **When predicting cooperation or non-cooperation, look at the institutional conditions, not just the payoff structure.**
2. **For settings where cooperation is needed, identify what would enable repeated interaction, communication, and identification.**
3. **For settings where cooperation is failing, ask which institutional features are absent.**

## Examples

**Situation:** A donor-funded community development project notes that contributions to a collective fund are falling. Free-riding is the diagnosis. What is the institutional response?

**Application:** Free-riding is the symptom; the cause is institutional. Likely candidates: contributions are made anonymously (no identification → no reciprocity); the community has no forum to communicate about contributions (no shared expectations); there are no sanctions for non-contribution (no enforcement); the perceived effectiveness of the fund is unclear (no payoff visibility). The reform is to make contributions visible, hold periodic discussion forums, introduce mild norms-based sanctions (public acknowledgement of contributors and quiet questioning of non-contributors), and report visibly on what the fund is achieving. None of this is exotic; it is the institutional architecture that enables collective action.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Predict collective action failure from the payoff matrix alone.
**Why:** The payoff matrix is one input to a much richer institutional picture. Actual cooperation rates routinely exceed what the matrix alone predicts.

**Don't:** Romanticise "community trust" as the solution.
**Why:** Trust is the output of institutional features, not an input that can be willed into being. Build the features; the trust follows.
