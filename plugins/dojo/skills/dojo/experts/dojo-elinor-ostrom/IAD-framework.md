# The Institutional Analysis and Development (IAD) Framework

## When to Use
- When the user is doing structured comparative analysis of policy or governance arrangements.
- When the question is how to analyse an institution systematically.
- When the user needs a framework for comparing institutional cases that resists premature theorising.

## Fails When
- **Used as a fill-in-the-blank template.** The framework structures analysis; it does not substitute for analytical judgement.
- **Confused with a theory.** It is a framework — a way of organising the question — not a theory of how outcomes emerge.

## Core Concept

The Institutional Analysis and Development (IAD) framework was developed at the Workshop in Political Theory and Policy Analysis to provide a systematic structure for analysing institutions across cases. It is the methodological backbone of Ostrom's comparative work.

The framework centres on the **action arena**: the analytical space where actors, in their positions, choose among allowed actions, given the rules in use, the physical and material conditions, and the attributes of the community. The action arena produces patterns of interaction, which produce outcomes, which feed back to the actors and (slowly) to the conditions and rules.

Three sets of variables shape the action arena:

**1. Biophysical/material conditions.** The actual characteristics of the resource or problem: ecology, technology, geography, scale. A fishery and a forest pose different physical problems; a small irrigation system and a large basin pose different scale problems.

**2. Attributes of the community.** The characteristics of the user group: size, heterogeneity (economic, ethnic, religious), history of interaction, shared norms, trust, leadership. A homogeneous longstanding village community differs from a heterogeneous urban community.

**3. Rules-in-use.** The seven categories of rules (position, boundary, choice, aggregation, information, payoff, scope) that govern the action arena. These are the rules actually followed and enforced, not just the rules written down.

Within the action arena, actors interact: they negotiate, communicate, monitor, sanction, comply, defect, innovate. The patterns of interaction produce outcomes (sustainable resource use, depletion, conflict, cooperation), and the outcomes can be evaluated against multiple criteria (efficiency, equity, accountability, sustainability).

The framework is **deliberately general**. It does not specify what causes what; it specifies what to look at when asking causal questions. This generality is its strength: it can structure analysis across radically different cases (a Maine lobster fishery, a Nepali irrigation system, a Southern African water-user association, a global climate agreement) and produce comparable findings.

The IAD framework is most useful when:
- Analysing a single case in depth, to understand why outcomes are what they are.
- Comparing several cases systematically, to extract regularities.
- Designing institutional reform, by working backward from desired outcomes through the framework to identify what rules-in-use or community attributes would need to change.

## How to Apply

1. **Specify the action arena.** Who are the actors? What positions can they occupy? What actions are open to them?
2. **Map the three variable sets.** What biophysical conditions, community attributes, and rules-in-use shape the arena?
3. **Trace patterns of interaction.** What actually happens in the arena?
4. **Identify outcomes and evaluate them against the relevant criteria.**
5. **Locate leverage points for reform** at the rules-in-use level primarily; community and biophysical conditions are slower to change.

## Examples

**Situation:** Diagnose why a particular Southern African community-based natural-resource-management programme is underperforming.

**Application:**
- **Action arena:** Resource users (farmers, herders), programme staff, traditional leaders, state officials.
- **Biophysical:** Rangeland with seasonal pasture; long-term degradation accelerated by drought.
- **Community attributes:** Heterogeneous (large livestock owners, smallholders, recent migrants); declining trust; weak shared norms after migration disruptions.
- **Rules-in-use:** Formal programme rules unenforced; informal rules under traditional leadership weakened; effectively closer to open access than to commons.
- **Interaction pattern:** Free-riding on grazing; conflict between large and small herd owners; minimal collective action on rangeland management.
- **Outcomes:** Continued degradation; programme reporting compliance because it measures attendance, not behaviour.
- **Leverage:** Reform of rules-in-use (clearer boundaries, devolved rule-making, peer monitoring) — but only if the community attributes (trust, shared norms) can also be strengthened, which requires sustained relational work, not just rule changes.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Fill in the framework cells and treat the result as analysis.
**Why:** The framework structures the questions; the analytical work is in answering them well.

**Don't:** Skip the biophysical and community-attributes work and jump to rules.
**Why:** Rules-in-use depend on the conditions and the community. Rule reforms that ignore those conditions usually fail.
