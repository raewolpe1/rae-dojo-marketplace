# Nesting and Multi-Level Governance

## When to Use
- When local institutions are part of larger systems (catchments, ecosystems, national policy).
- When the user is debating the appropriate division of authority across scales.
- When analysing why a well-designed local institution is failing because of higher-level dynamics.

## Fails When
- **Conflated with simple hierarchy.** Nesting is not top-down command; it is structured division of authority across levels with productive interaction.
- **Treated as automatic.** Nesting works when designed; it produces conflict and gridlock when not.

## Core Concept

The eighth design principle for enduring commons is nesting: that the institution sits in a multi-level structure with rule-making, monitoring, conflict resolution, and governance arrangements at appropriate scales.

Most consequential resource problems are not at a single level. A small irrigation system sits within a catchment within a basin within a region. A coastal fishery sits within a marine system within a national jurisdiction within international waters. A village forest sits within a landscape within a national forest policy framework within a global biodiversity regime. Single-level governance — addressing the problem only at the local level, or only at the national, or only at the international — misses the cross-scale interactions that drive the problem.

**Effective nesting** has several features.

**Subsidiarity.** Decisions are made at the lowest level capable of addressing them. Local resource use rules are made by user communities; cross-community externalities are addressed at higher levels; genuinely national or international concerns are addressed at those levels.

**Mutual recognition.** Higher levels recognise the authority of lower levels within their scope, and lower levels recognise the authority of higher levels within theirs. Where higher levels preempt lower-level rule-making, local institutions wither; where lower levels reject higher-level frameworks, coordination fails.

**Information flow.** Information moves up (local conditions inform regional and national policy) and down (broader trends inform local decisions). Without information flow, levels operate in isolation and produce contradictory rules.

**Conflict resolution across levels.** When level decisions conflict — local rules incompatible with national regulations, regional rules with local conditions — there are accessible procedures for resolution. Without these procedures, conflicts paralyse the system.

**Layered rule-types.** Operational rules (day-to-day use), collective-choice rules (who can change operational rules), and constitutional rules (who can change collective-choice rules) operate at potentially different levels. Reform requires identifying which level of rule is the binding constraint and addressing it there.

For South African examples: a water-user association sits within a catchment management agency within the National Water Act framework within international transboundary water arrangements (Lesotho Highlands, Limpopo, Orange). Each level has authority within scope; effective governance requires that authorities recognise each other, that information flows, and that conflicts have resolution procedures. Where any of these fails, the WUA's local design quality cannot save the system.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify all the levels at which the problem is governed (or should be).**
2. **For each level, ask: what decisions are made here? What information is available? What enforcement?**
3. **Look for cross-level interactions** — particularly preemption (higher level overrides local rule) and abandonment (higher level fails to provide enabling framework).
4. **Design reforms that strengthen the level relationships, not just any single level.**

## Examples

**Situation:** A community forestry programme in a Southern African country produces strong local rules but achieves poor outcomes because national forest legislation does not recognise community management decisions.

**Application:** The nesting principle is the binding constraint. The local institution can be perfectly designed and still fail because the legal framework does not provide the recognition (principle 7) and nesting (principle 8) that the local institution needs to be effective. The reform is not at the local level — it is at the national legal framework, to provide legal standing for community management decisions, integrate community plans into the national framework, and provide accessible procedures for resolving conflicts between community decisions and national policy. Without this higher-level reform, no amount of local capacity-building will produce sustainable outcomes.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume nesting just means "have a local committee and a national authority."
**Why:** Nesting is about the productive relationship between levels, not just their existence.

**Don't:** Address only one level when the problem spans levels.
**Why:** Single-level fixes for multi-level problems produce paper compliance and persistent failure.
