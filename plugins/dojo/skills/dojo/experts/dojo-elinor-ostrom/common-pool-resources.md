# Common-Pool Resources

## When to Use
- When the user is unsure what category of good a resource falls into.
- When debating whether something is a public good, a club good, or a commons.
- When designing governance for a resource and the prior classification matters.

## Fails When
- **The categorisation is treated as a property of the resource alone.** Excludability and rivalry depend on technology and institutions, not just on the physics.
- **Used as a static classification.** Resources can move between categories as technology and institutions change.

## Core Concept

Goods can be classified by two characteristics: **rivalry** (does one person's use diminish what is available for others?) and **excludability** (can users be prevented from accessing the resource?). The four cells produce a typology:

|  | Excludable | Non-excludable |
|---|---|---|
| **Rivalrous** | Private goods (food, clothing) | **Common-pool resources** (fisheries, forests, irrigation, groundwater) |
| **Non-rivalrous** | Club goods (toll roads, satellite TV) | Public goods (national defence, ambient air quality) |

**Common-pool resources** are the goods category Ostrom's framework principally addresses. They are rivalrous — one person's catch is not another's — and non-excludable, or at most weakly excludable, in their natural state. Without institutional arrangements, anyone can access them. With institutional arrangements, access becomes excludable to defined user communities.

This is the key analytical move. Excludability is not given by nature; it is produced by institutions. A coastal fishery is naturally hard to exclude from (the water flows; the fish move). But with the right institutional architecture — recognised user communities, monitoring norms, sanctions against outsiders, conflict-resolution mechanisms — the fishery becomes substantially excludable in practice. The community then governs a *common-pool resource managed as a commons*.

The distinction between common-pool resources (as a goods category) and commons (as an institutional arrangement) matters. The same common-pool resource can be governed:

- As **open access** — no exclusion, no rules, free-for-all (this is Hardin's setup).
- As **private property** — divided into excludable parcels owned individually.
- As **state property** — managed by a sovereign authority.
- As **commons** — governed by a defined user community under its own rules.

Each of these is a candidate institutional arrangement for the same underlying resource. The right choice depends on the resource characteristics, the user community, the broader political environment, and the history.

For South African examples: communal grazing land is a common-pool resource often governed under a commons arrangement (traditional rules, with statutory overlay); inshore fisheries similarly; groundwater in shared aquifers; small-scale informal fisheries within marine protected areas. Each is rivalrous and only partly excludable; each has institutional options beyond pure privatisation or pure state control.

## How to Apply

1. **Classify the resource by rivalry and excludability** — but treat excludability as institutionally variable.
2. **Identify the current institutional arrangement** — open access, private, state, commons, or hybrid.
3. **Consider alternative arrangements.** What are the candidates and their likely properties?
4. **Don't confuse the goods category with the governance arrangement.** Same goods, different institutions, different outcomes.

## Examples

**Situation:** A municipal council is debating how to manage a river that supplies both irrigation and domestic water.

**Application:** The water is a common-pool resource (rivalrous; partly excludable through abstraction permits). The current arrangement is hybrid — partly state-managed (the catchment management agency issues licences), partly private (some upstream landowners), partly commons (informal water-user customs). The question is not "should we privatise or nationalise?" but "which combination of these governance modes, at which scales, produces the most workable arrangement given local conditions?" Often the answer involves strengthening the commons elements (water-user associations with real rule-making rights) within the existing legal framework, rather than replacing the framework wholesale.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat rivalry and excludability as fixed physical attributes.
**Why:** Both depend on institutions and technology. Fish in international waters are hard to exclude; the same fish in a national EEZ become more excludable.

**Don't:** Assume the goods type determines the governance.
**Why:** The same common-pool resource can be governed in different ways, and the choice is institutional, not technical.
