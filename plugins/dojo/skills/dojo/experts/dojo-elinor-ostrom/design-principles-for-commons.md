# Design Principles for Commons

## When to Use
- When the user is designing or reforming a commons governance institution.
- When diagnosing a failing community-based natural-resource management programme.
- When the user needs the operational checklist that distinguishes enduring commons from failing ones.

## Fails When
- **Treated as a fixed recipe.** Each principle must be instantiated in context-specific form.
- **Applied mechanically without diagnosing the resource and community first.** The principles answer "how" questions; the prior "what" questions still need answers.

## Core Concept

After cross-case analysis of long-enduring and failing commons institutions worldwide, Ostrom identified eight features that recurrent in the enduring cases and tended to be missing in the failing ones. They are not derivations from theory; they are empirical regularities elevated to the status of design principles.

**1. Clearly defined boundaries.** The user community is identifiable; the resource boundary is identifiable. Both internal members and outsiders know who has rights to use the resource and where the resource's geographic or material limits are.

**2. Congruence between rules and local conditions.** Rules governing appropriation and provision are matched to local ecology, technology, and social structure. Generic rules imposed across diverse conditions fail; locally calibrated rules succeed.

**3. Collective-choice arrangements.** Most users affected by the rules can participate in modifying the rules. Rule-making is not concentrated in distant authorities; it is accessible to those whose actions the rules govern.

**4. Monitoring.** Users themselves, or people accountable to them, monitor compliance with rules. External monitoring is rare in enduring commons; internal mutual monitoring is the norm.

**5. Graduated sanctions.** Users who violate rules face sanctions calibrated to the severity and context of the violation. Sanctions are typically light for first offences and rise for repeat or severe ones. The system is not zero-tolerance, nor is it lax — it is graduated.

**6. Conflict-resolution mechanisms.** Users have access to low-cost, locally accessible procedures for resolving disputes among themselves and between users and rule-enforcers.

**7. Minimal recognition of rights to organise.** External authorities (state, regional) do not undermine the local right to self-organise. Where state action overrides community rule-making, commons institutions tend to collapse.

**8. Nested enterprises.** For commons that are part of larger systems, the institution is nested in a multi-level structure — local commons inside regional networks inside national frameworks — with rule-making, monitoring, conflict resolution, and governance happening at appropriate levels.

The principles are interactive. A commons may have strong monitoring (principle 4) but weak conflict resolution (principle 6) and will eventually fail. A commons may have clear boundaries (principle 1) but no rule-making rights (principle 3) and will become a rigid relic. The principles together constitute a system; partial instantiation is partial protection.

## How to Apply

1. **Audit the candidate institution against each principle.** Which are present, which absent, which partial?
2. **Diagnose the gaps in their interactions.** Often the binding constraint is one principle, but the leverage is at the interaction between it and another.
3. **Design reforms that strengthen the weakest principles without disrupting the strong ones.**
4. **Resist universal templates.** Instantiation is local.

## Examples

**Situation:** A small-scale fisheries co-management programme in a Southern African coastal community is producing poor compliance and ongoing stock decline. Diagnose using the design principles.

**Application:** A typical pattern: boundaries are formally defined but contested (principle 1 partial); rules were set by national fisheries authorities without local input (principle 3 weak); monitoring is treated as a state inspection function (principle 4 weak — locals do not monitor each other); sanctions are uniform statutory fines rather than graduated (principle 5 weak); conflict resolution requires accessing distant tribunals (principle 6 weak); the broader policy environment second-guesses local rule-making (principle 7 weak); nesting is poorly developed (principle 8 weak). The diagnostic surface is that compliance is poor; the underlying issue is that six of the eight principles are weak. Reform should address the weak principles in the order that produces fastest payoff: usually devolving rule-making (principle 3), enabling local monitoring (principle 4), and graduating sanctions (principle 5) first.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the eight principles as a scoring rubric and stop at the score.
**Why:** The score is diagnostic; the analytic value is in the institutional design implications, which require qualitative judgement.

**Don't:** Assume the principles are universal in their specific form.
**Why:** "Clear boundaries" looks very different in a Swiss alpine commons, a Filipino irrigation system, and a Southern African rangeland. The principle is the same; the instantiation is not.
