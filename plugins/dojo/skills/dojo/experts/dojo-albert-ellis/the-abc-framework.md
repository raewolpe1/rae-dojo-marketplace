# The ABC Framework

## When to Use
- When someone says an event "made" them feel something.
- When the mechanism connecting events to emotional responses needs to be made explicit.
- When teaching the foundational tool of REBT.

## Core Concept
The ABC framework is the engine of REBT. A is the Activating event — what happened. C is the Consequence — the emotional and behavioural response. Most people believe A causes C: "My boss criticised me (A) and that made me depressed (C)." Ellis's central insight is that A does not cause C. B does. B is the Belief — what you tell yourself about the event.

Two people can experience the same activating event and have entirely different emotional consequences because they hold different beliefs. One person is criticised by their boss and thinks, "That's annoying. I'll consider whether the criticism is valid and adjust if necessary." They feel mildly irritated — a healthy negative emotion. Another person is criticised by their boss and thinks, "I must perform perfectly at all times. This criticism proves I am incompetent. My boss must approve of me, and the fact that he doesn't means I am worthless." They feel depressed — an unhealthy negative emotion driven by irrational beliefs.

The framework extends to D and E. D is Disputation — actively challenging the irrational belief. "Where is the evidence that I must perform perfectly? Why does one criticism prove I am incompetent? How does one person's disapproval make me worthless?" E is the Effective new philosophy — the rational alternative that replaces the irrational belief. "I strongly prefer to do well, but I don't have to. This criticism is about one piece of work, not about my total worth as a person."

The power of the ABC framework is that it locates the source of disturbance within the individual's belief system, not in external events. This is not victim-blaming — it is empowerment. You cannot always change A. You can almost always change B. And when you change B, C changes automatically.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify A clearly.** What actually happened? Strip it of interpretation. Not "My boss was unfair" but "My boss said my report needed significant revision."
2. **Identify C clearly.** What did you feel? What did you do? Be specific. Not "I felt bad" but "I felt depressed, withdrew from colleagues, and avoided starting the revision."
3. **Find B.** This is the critical step. What were you telling yourself about A? Look for musts, shoulds, and oughts. Look for catastrophising, global rating, and low frustration tolerance.
4. **Dispute B.** Apply empirical, logical, and pragmatic challenges.
5. **Arrive at E.** Formulate a rational alternative that maintains the preference without the demand.

## Examples
**Situation:** A woman says, "My friend cancelled our lunch plans and I feel devastated. She obviously doesn't care about me."
**Application:** A = Friend cancelled lunch. C = Devastation, withdrawal, rumination. The person has skipped B entirely and attributed C directly to A. The REBT intervention is to find B: "What are you telling yourself about the cancellation?" Likely beliefs: "My friend must not cancel on me. If she does, it means she doesn't value me. If she doesn't value me, that proves I am unlovable." The demands are: she must not cancel (a demand on others), and if she does, it proves I am unlovable (a global self-rating based on another person's behaviour). Disputation: "Is there evidence that her cancellation means she doesn't care? Has she shown care in other ways? Even if she did cancel because she preferred something else — does that make you unlovable? Can one person's one action determine your total worth?" The effective new philosophy: "I would have preferred she kept our plans. Her cancellation is disappointing. It does not mean she doesn't care, and even if she cared less than I want, that would not make me unlovable."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss the activating event.
**Why:** REBT does not claim that events don't matter. Getting fired, being betrayed, losing someone — these are genuinely bad events. The point is that the irrational belief about the event adds unnecessary suffering on top of the real pain.

**Don't:** Rush to disputation before the belief is clearly identified.
**Why:** If you dispute vaguely, the person feels argued with but not helped. The belief must be specific and explicit before disputation can be effective.
