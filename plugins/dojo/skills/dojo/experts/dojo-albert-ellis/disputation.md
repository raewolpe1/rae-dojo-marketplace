# Disputation

## When to Use
- When an irrational belief has been identified and needs to be actively challenged.
- When the three types of disputation need to be taught.
- When someone is intellectually aware of their irrationality but emotionally stuck.

## Core Concept
Disputation is the active, vigorous challenging of irrational beliefs. It is the D in the extended ABCDE model. Ellis insisted that disputation must be forceful — not gentle suggestion but energetic confrontation of the belief's logical, empirical, and pragmatic foundations. Irrational beliefs have emotional momentum. A polite challenge will not overcome them.

Three types of disputation work together. Empirical disputation asks: "Where is the evidence?" Where is the evidence that you must be approved of by everyone? Where is the proof that one failure makes you a total failure? What data supports the claim that you cannot survive disapproval? Most irrational beliefs collapse under empirical scrutiny because they make claims about reality that reality does not support.

Logical disputation asks: "Does this follow logically?" Even if it is true that you failed at this task, does it logically follow that you are a failure as a person? Even if it is true that your partner treated you unfairly, does it follow that they must not have? The logic of irrational beliefs is almost always non sequitur — the conclusion does not follow from the premise.

Pragmatic disputation asks: "Is this belief helping you?" Even if you cannot be convinced that the belief is false, you can often see that holding it is destructive. Does believing "I must be perfect" make you more or less productive? Does demanding that others treat you fairly make your relationships better or worse? Does catastrophising about the future reduce your anxiety or increase it? Pragmatic disputation works when the other two have not fully broken through.

Ellis emphasised that disputation is not a one-time event but an ongoing practice. You dispute the belief, you feel better, you encounter a similar situation, the belief returns, and you dispute again. Over time, the belief weakens — not because you argued it away once but because you argued it away a hundred times and acted against it each time.

## How to Apply
1. **State the belief explicitly.** Write it down if possible. "I must be approved of by my colleagues."
2. **Apply empirical disputation.** "Is there evidence that I must be? Has anyone ever survived disapproval? Have I survived disapproval before?"
3. **Apply logical disputation.** "Even if my colleagues disapprove, does it follow that I am worthless? How does their opinion determine my value?"
4. **Apply pragmatic disputation.** "What happens when I hold this belief? Does it make me more effective or less? More relaxed or more anxious?"
5. **Formulate the effective new philosophy.** "I would strongly prefer my colleagues' approval, but I do not need it. Their disapproval is inconvenient, not catastrophic."
6. **Repeat.** The belief will return. Dispute it again. And again.

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant tells herself, "If this proposal is rejected, it means I'm not competent enough for this work."
**Application:** The belief to dispute: "Rejection of the proposal = proof of incompetence." Empirical: "Is there evidence that proposal rejection proves incompetence? Have competent people had proposals rejected? Have I had proposals accepted in the past? Does one outcome define competence?" Logical: "Even if the proposal is weak, does it follow that I — as a total person — am incompetent? Am I my proposal?" Pragmatic: "What does holding this belief do? It makes me so anxious about rejection that I either procrastinate (avoiding the possibility) or over-work (trying to make it perfect). Both outcomes are worse than the rejection I'm trying to avoid." Effective new philosophy: "I want this proposal to succeed and I'll work to make it strong. If it's rejected, that's information about this proposal, not about my worth."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dispute gently.
**Why:** Ellis was explicit — irrational beliefs have emotional force. A gentle suggestion will not overcome them. Disputation must match the belief's intensity or it will be overwhelmed.

**Don't:** Stop after one round.
**Why:** Beliefs recur. Disputation is a practice, not a procedure. The person will need to dispute the same belief many times before it weakens significantly.
