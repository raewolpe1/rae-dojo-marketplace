# The REBT Model

## When to Use
- When someone asks what Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy is.
- When the philosophical foundations of the approach need to be explained.
- When the relationship between REBT, CBT, and the broader cognitive-behavioral tradition needs clarification.

## Core Concept
Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy, created by Albert Ellis in 1955, was the first cognitive-behavioral therapy — the original prototype from which Beck's cognitive therapy, and later the entire CBT family, descended. Ellis was trained as a psychoanalyst but became frustrated with its inefficiency and what he saw as its fundamental error: the belief that insight into the past causes change. He concluded that people are disturbed not by events but by their rigid, absolutistic beliefs about events — an insight he traced to the Stoic philosopher Epictetus, who wrote in the first century: "It is not things that disturb us, but our judgments about things."

REBT rests on three pillars. The cognitive pillar holds that irrational beliefs — particularly rigid demands (musts, shoulds, oughts) — are the primary source of emotional disturbance. The emotive pillar holds that change requires emotional as well as intellectual engagement — the person must feel the irrationality of their beliefs, not just understand it abstractly. The behavioural pillar holds that lasting change requires action — doing the things you fear, acting against your irrational beliefs, building new habits through repetition and homework.

Ellis identified a biological predisposition toward irrational thinking. Humans do not simply learn to think crookedly — they have an innate tendency toward demandingness, catastrophising, and global self-rating. This means that therapeutic change is not a one-time achievement but an ongoing practice. You will drift back toward irrationality. The work is to keep catching it, disputing it, and acting against it.

REBT differs from Beck's cognitive therapy in several ways. Ellis emphasised philosophical change — a fundamental shift in one's orientation toward life — rather than the correction of specific cognitive distortions. Ellis was more confrontational. Ellis placed greater emphasis on unconditional self-acceptance (rather than building self-esteem). And Ellis insisted on the emotive and behavioural components — shame-attacking exercises, rational-emotive imagery, and vigorous behavioural homework — rather than relying primarily on cognitive restructuring.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the irrational belief.** What is the person demanding? What must happen or not happen?
2. **Use the ABC framework.** Separate the activating event from the belief from the consequence.
3. **Dispute vigorously.** Challenge the belief empirically, logically, and pragmatically.
4. **Generate a rational alternative.** Replace the demand with a strong preference.
5. **Assign homework.** Cognitive, emotive, and behavioural. Change happens between sessions.

## Examples
**Situation:** A client says, "I've been in therapy for three years and I still feel anxious. Maybe I'm just an anxious person and nothing will help."
**Application:** Ellis would challenge the implicit beliefs: "You've been in therapy for three years — what have you been doing in therapy? If your therapist has been helping you explore your childhood for three years without challenging your current beliefs, I'm not surprised you're still anxious. You haven't been doing the wrong thing for three years — you've been doing the wrong therapy. The anxiety is not a permanent feature of who you are. It is a product of what you are telling yourself — probably something like 'I must not feel anxious, and the fact that I still do proves something is wrong with me.' That belief is maintaining the anxiety. Let's find the specific demands you're making and dispute them. Then let's get you doing homework — real homework, not just talking about your feelings once a week."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present REBT as only about thinking.
**Why:** Ellis was insistent that REBT involves three modalities — cognitive, emotive, and behavioural. A purely cognitive approach is incomplete and was not what Ellis advocated.

**Don't:** Reduce REBT to "positive thinking."
**Why:** REBT is not about thinking positively — it is about thinking rationally. Rational thinking includes acknowledging that bad things happen. The goal is to respond to bad things without irrational escalation, not to pretend they are good.
