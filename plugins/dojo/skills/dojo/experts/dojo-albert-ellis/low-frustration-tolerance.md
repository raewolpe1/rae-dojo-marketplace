# Low Frustration Tolerance

## When to Use
- When someone is avoiding necessary but uncomfortable actions.
- When procrastination is driven by the belief that discomfort is unbearable.
- When the concept of discomfort disturbance or low frustration tolerance needs to be explained.

## Core Concept
Low frustration tolerance (LFT) — what Ellis also called discomfort disturbance — is the belief that discomfort is unbearable and must be avoided. It is one of the three derivative irrationalities that flow from frustrated demands, and Ellis considered it one of the most underrecognised sources of self-defeating behaviour.

The person with LFT does not merely dislike discomfort — everyone dislikes discomfort. They believe they cannot stand it. "I can't stand this boring work." "I can't stand the anxiety of public speaking." "I can't stand the effort required to exercise." The belief that the discomfort is unbearable produces avoidance. And avoidance produces worse outcomes — the work piles up, the fear grows, the body deteriorates — which generates more discomfort, more avoidance, and a worsening spiral.

Ellis pointed out that the statement "I can't stand it" is almost always empirically false at the moment it is uttered. The person is standing it. They are alive. They are functioning. The discomfort is real but it is not literally unbearable — they are bearing it. The belief that it is unbearable is what makes it feel unbearable.

LFT is the mechanism behind most procrastination, most addiction, and most avoidance behaviour. The procrastinator does not lack time management skills. They hold the irrational belief that the discomfort of doing the task is unbearable, and so they escape into distraction — which provides short-term relief and long-term worsening. The addict does not lack willpower. They hold the irrational belief that the discomfort of craving is unbearable, and so they use — which provides short-term relief and long-term devastation.

The intervention is to build high frustration tolerance (HFT) — not through willpower but through philosophical change. The person learns that discomfort is uncomfortable (true) but bearable (also true), and that tolerating short-term discomfort for long-term gain is worth the effort (pragmatically demonstrated).

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the "I can't stand it" belief.** It may be implicit. Procrastination, avoidance, and escape behaviours usually have LFT underneath.
2. **Challenge empirically.** "Can you stand it? Are you standing it right now? Have you stood similar things before?"
3. **Distinguish uncomfortable from unbearable.** The task is boring. That does not make it unbearable. The exercise is effortful. That does not make it impossible.
4. **Do a cost-benefit analysis.** What is the cost of avoiding the discomfort? What is the cost of tolerating it?
5. **Assign graduated exposure.** Start with small doses of the discomfort. Build tolerance through practice, not through insight alone.

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant keeps postponing writing a difficult evaluation report. He says, "I just can't get started. Every time I sit down, I feel overwhelmed and go do something else."
**Application:** The presenting problem is procrastination, but the mechanism is LFT. He sits down, feels the discomfort of the difficult writing task, tells himself "I can't stand this" or "This is too hard," and escapes into easier activities. The relief reinforces the avoidance. The REBT approach: "The report is uncomfortable to write. Agreed. Writing difficult things is uncomfortable. But can you stand it? You've written difficult things before. You survived. The discomfort lasts — what — an hour? Two hours? And then it's done. Right now you're trading two hours of discomfort for two weeks of guilt, anxiety, and a growing deadline problem. Which is worse? The discomfort of writing, or the discomfort of not writing?" Homework: write for twenty minutes. Not well. Just write. Tolerate the discomfort for twenty minutes and stop. Tomorrow, twenty minutes again. Build tolerance through practice.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Tell someone to "just push through."
**Why:** White-knuckling through discomfort without addressing the underlying belief is unsustainable. The belief must change for the behaviour to change durably.

**Don't:** Dismiss the discomfort as trivial.
**Why:** The discomfort is real. The irrationality is in the belief that it is unbearable. Acknowledge the discomfort while challenging the catastrophisation of it.
