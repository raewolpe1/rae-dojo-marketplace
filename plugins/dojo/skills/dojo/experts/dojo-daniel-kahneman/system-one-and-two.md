# System 1 and System 2

## When to Use
- When someone asks about thinking fast and slow, intuition versus analysis, or automatic versus deliberate thought.
- When diagnosing why a particular judgment was made quickly or poorly.
- When designing processes that need to account for how people actually think.

## Core Concept
System 1 operates automatically, rapidly, with little effort and no sense of voluntary control. It recognises faces, completes familiar phrases, detects hostility in a voice, and generates an intuitive answer to "2 + 2." System 2 allocates attention to effortful mental activities — complex computations, self-control, deliberate choice. It is what you engage when someone asks you to multiply 17 × 24.

The critical insight is not that we have two systems — this is a useful fiction, not a neuroanatomical claim — but that most of our thinking is System 1, and we systematically overestimate how much System 2 is involved. System 1 generates impressions, feelings, and inclinations; when endorsed by System 2, these become beliefs, attitudes, and intentions. The problem is that System 2 is lazy. It often endorses System 1's output without checking, particularly when cognitive load is high, when we are tired, or when the question feels easy.

System 1 is not the enemy. It is extraordinarily effective for navigating everyday life, reading social situations, and making rapid judgments that are usually adequate. The errors it produces are not random — they are systematic, predictable side-effects of the heuristics that make rapid judgment possible. Anchoring, availability, representativeness, and affect heuristic are all System 1 operations. The goal is not to suppress System 1 but to recognise when it is likely to mislead and to create conditions where System 2 actually engages.

Expert intuition — the chess master's immediate recognition of a pattern, the experienced firefighter's feeling that a building is about to collapse — is System 1 operating in a high-validity environment with extensive practice. It can be trusted when two conditions are met: the environment provides regular, reliable feedback, and the person has had prolonged practice in that environment. Most professional judgment does not meet both conditions.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose the system.** When a judgment has been made, ask: "Was this System 1 or System 2? Was the answer generated automatically, or was there genuine deliberation?"
2. **Check for lazy System 2.** "Did System 2 actually check this, or did it just endorse what System 1 produced?"
3. **Assess the validity environment.** "Is this a domain where expert intuition is reliable — regular feedback, stable patterns — or is it a low-validity environment where intuition is unreliable?"
4. **Design for System 2 engagement.** When accurate judgment matters, create friction — checklists, structured formats, mandatory delays — that forces System 2 to do actual work.

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme manager says "I've got a gut feeling this implementing partner is going to underperform. I can't point to anything specific, but something feels off."
**Application:** Kahneman would say: "Your System 1 has detected a pattern and generated an intuitive judgment. The question is whether to trust it. Two conditions make expert intuition reliable: a high-validity environment with regular feedback, and prolonged experience in that environment. If you have managed many implementing partners and received clear feedback on which ones performed and which didn't, your intuition may be picking up subtle cues that your conscious mind hasn't articulated — and it's worth taking seriously. But if this is a relatively new domain for you, or if feedback on partner performance is delayed and ambiguous, your System 1 may be responding to something irrelevant — perhaps the partner reminds you of someone you distrust, or their presentation style triggered a negative affect heuristic. Don't ignore the feeling, but don't act on it unchecked either. Ask System 2 to do the work: what specific indicators would you look for if you were evaluating this partner formally? Check those. If the data and the intuition agree, you have convergent evidence. If they disagree, trust the data."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present System 1 as the problem and System 2 as the solution.
**Why:** System 2 has its own failures — it is lazy, it rationalises System 1's conclusions, and it is subject to motivated reasoning. Better judgment comes from better procedures, not simply from "thinking harder."

**Don't:** Use "System 1" and "System 2" as literal descriptions of brain architecture.
**Why:** They are useful metaphors for two modes of cognitive processing. They do not correspond to specific neural structures. Kahneman was explicit about this.
