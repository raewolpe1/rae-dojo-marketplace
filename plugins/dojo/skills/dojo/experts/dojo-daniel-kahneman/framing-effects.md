# Framing Effects

## When to Use
- When the way information is presented is influencing decisions more than the substance of the information.
- When the same data, reframed, produces different reactions or choices.
- When designing how to present evaluation findings, programme options, or policy choices.

## Core Concept
Framing effects occur when logically equivalent descriptions of the same situation lead to different decisions. The classic demonstration is the Asian Disease Problem: when a public health programme is described as saving 200 out of 600 people, most people choose it. When the same programme is described as resulting in 400 deaths out of 600, most people reject it. The outcomes are identical; the frame is different; the choices reverse.

This violates a basic requirement of rational choice — that preferences should not depend on how options are described. But they do, systematically and predictably. The mechanism is prospect theory: the "save 200" frame puts the decision in the domain of gains, where people are risk-averse and prefer certainty. The "400 will die" frame puts it in the domain of losses, where people are risk-seeking and prefer gambling.

Framing effects are not limited to gain/loss framing. Attribute framing changes how a single characteristic is perceived — beef described as "75% lean" is judged more favourably than beef described as "25% fat." Goal framing changes motivation — messages about what you gain by acting are less motivating than messages about what you lose by not acting. Risky choice framing changes risk preferences through gain/loss manipulation.

In professional practice, framing effects are pervasive. Evaluation findings can be framed as achievements (what the programme accomplished) or as shortfalls (where the programme fell short). The same data on poverty reduction can be framed as "we lifted 2 million people out of poverty" or "18 million people remain in poverty." Budget proposals can be framed as investments (gains) or as costs (losses). Each frame activates different psychological responses and leads to different decisions — and the person making the decision is typically unaware that the frame, rather than the substance, is driving their choice.

## How to Apply
1. **Test for frame-dependence.** Present the same information in multiple frames and see if the judgment changes. If it does, the frame is doing the work, not the substance.
2. **Present both frames.** When presenting findings or options, provide the information in both a positive and a negative frame. This does not eliminate framing effects, but it makes them visible.
3. **Be aware of your own framing.** When preparing reports, proposals, or recommendations, notice which frame you have chosen and why. The choice of frame is itself a consequential decision.
4. **Use framing deliberately and transparently.** When you choose a frame, be explicit about it: "I'm presenting this as a success story because that frame is appropriate given the context, but here is the same data in the other frame."

## Examples
**Situation:** An M&E team is preparing to present findings to a donor. The programme has reached 60% of its target beneficiaries. The team is debating whether to lead with "we reached 60% of target" or "we fell short of 40% of target."
**Application:** Kahneman would say: "You are debating a framing choice, and the frame you choose will significantly influence how the donor receives the findings — regardless of the underlying data. Leading with '60% reached' puts the conversation in the domain of gains and activates risk-averse, status-quo-preserving cognition: the programme is working, let's continue. Leading with '40% shortfall' puts it in the domain of losses and activates either risk-seeking cognition (we need to try something different to recover) or punitive responses (this programme is failing). Both frames describe identical performance. The honest approach is to present both frames explicitly and let the donor see the data without the framing doing the interpretive work. But be aware: even presenting both frames does not fully eliminate framing effects. The frame that appears first often anchors the interpretation. And the donor's existing expectations — whether they expected 60% to be good or poor — set a reference point that functions as a meta-frame."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat all framing as manipulation.
**Why:** Communication requires choosing a frame. The choice is unavoidable. The question is whether the frame is chosen deliberately, transparently, and appropriately — not whether framing is happening.

**Don't:** Assume awareness eliminates framing effects.
**Why:** Even when people know about framing effects, they are influenced by frames. The effect is reduced but not eliminated by awareness. Procedural countermeasures — presenting multiple frames — are more effective than knowledge alone.
