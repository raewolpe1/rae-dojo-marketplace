# Planning Fallacy

## When to Use
- When someone is estimating how long a project will take, how much it will cost, or how many resources it will require.
- When an inside view — focused on the specifics of this particular plan — is producing optimistic estimates.
- When reference class forecasting can provide a corrective outside view.

## Core Concept
The planning fallacy is the systematic tendency to underestimate the time, cost, and risk of future actions while overestimating their benefits. It is one of the most robust findings in the judgment and decision-making literature, and one of the most consequential. Kahneman considered it the most impactful bias for practical decision-making because it affects virtually every project, budget, and timeline ever constructed.

The mechanism is the inside view. When people plan, they focus on the specific features of the project at hand — the tasks, the sequences, the anticipated steps. This focus generates a narrative that is optimistic because it is constructed from the best-case mental simulation of how things will go. The planner imagines the project unfolding step by step and estimates time and resources accordingly. But this mental simulation systematically neglects the unexpected — delays, complications, dependencies, external disruptions — because unpredicted events, by definition, are not available for inclusion in the simulation.

The outside view corrects this by asking a different question: not "how do I think this specific project will go?" but "how do projects like this typically go?" This is reference class forecasting — identifying the appropriate reference class of similar past projects and using their distribution of outcomes as the baseline estimate. If similar programmes typically take 30% longer than planned and cost 25% more than budgeted, those percentages should be applied to your estimate as a correction, regardless of how confident you feel about your specific plan.

Kahneman and Tversky experienced the planning fallacy themselves when writing a curriculum textbook. They estimated the project would take two years. A colleague who knew the base rate told them similar projects took seven to ten years, with 40% never completing. They finished in eight years. Their inside view — constructed by the experts doing the actual work — was wrong by a factor of four.

The planning fallacy is not corrected by experience. People who have experienced cost overruns and schedule delays in previous projects still produce optimistic estimates for future ones. Each new project feels genuinely different — "this time we've learned from our mistakes" — and System 1 constructs a fresh, coherent, optimistic narrative.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the reference class.** Before estimating, ask: "What is the appropriate class of similar projects, and what do their outcomes look like?"
2. **Use base rates as the starting point.** Begin with the distribution of outcomes for similar projects, then adjust for specific factors — but be sceptical of large adjustments.
3. **Conduct a pre-mortem.** "Imagine this project has failed. What went wrong?" This counteracts the optimistic inside view by forcing consideration of failure modes.
4. **Build in explicit buffers.** If the base rate says similar projects take 30% longer, add 30% to your timeline. Do not say "but ours is different" unless you have specific, verifiable evidence for why.

## Examples
**Situation:** A consulting firm is bidding on a large programme evaluation. The team lead estimates 120 person-days over six months.
**Application:** Kahneman would say: "Before you submit that bid, apply the outside view. How many programme evaluations of comparable scope and complexity have you completed? What was the average ratio of actual person-days to estimated person-days? In my experience, and in the research literature, the answer is typically 1.3 to 1.5 — that is, actuals are 30 to 50 percent higher than estimates. Your 120 person-days should probably be 156 to 180 person-days. Your six-month timeline should probably be eight to nine months. I know this feels wrong. Your inside view is telling you that 120 days is realistic because you have imagined each task and estimated each one carefully. But that is precisely the planning fallacy: careful estimation of individual tasks still produces optimistic totals because it neglects unexpected complications, coordination costs, and the general friction of complex work. The pre-mortem is your best friend here. Before you submit the bid, ask: 'Imagine we're at month nine and we've used 160 person-days. What happened?' Your team will immediately generate scenarios — the data was messier than expected, the client took three weeks to respond to the inception report, a team member left, the sampling frame was wrong. Those scenarios are not edge cases. They are the normal course of complex work."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use the planning fallacy to justify cynicism or inaction.
**Why:** The point is not that planning is futile. It is that plans should be corrected by base rates. Better estimation is possible — it just requires discipline and procedures that most planners resist.

**Don't:** Dismiss the inside view entirely.
**Why:** The inside view contains genuinely useful information — knowledge of the specific context, the team's capabilities, the unique features of this project. The best estimates combine the inside view with the outside view, using the reference class as the anchor and the specific details as the adjustment.
