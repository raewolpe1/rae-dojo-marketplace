# Anchoring

## When to Use
- When a first number, estimate, or value is influencing subsequent judgments.
- When numerical assessments — budgets, timelines, valuations, targets — may be contaminated by irrelevant starting points.
- When designing processes that need to minimise anchoring effects.

## Core Concept
Anchoring is one of the most robust findings in the heuristics and biases programme. When people make numerical estimates, they are systematically pulled toward an initial value — the anchor — even when that value is arbitrary, irrelevant, or obviously uninformative. Kahneman and Tversky demonstrated this in their classic study: spinning a rigged wheel of fortune that stopped at either 10 or 65, then asking participants to estimate the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. Those who saw 65 estimated significantly higher than those who saw 10, despite the number being generated by a wheel of fortune.

Two mechanisms produce anchoring. The first is insufficient adjustment: people start from the anchor and adjust away from it, but the adjustment is typically insufficient — they stop too soon, leaving the final estimate closer to the anchor than it should be. The second, more powerful mechanism is selective accessibility: the anchor makes anchor-consistent information more accessible. If someone suggests a project will cost R5 million, your mind automatically generates reasons why R5 million might be correct, making that figure seem more plausible than it would have without the anchor.

Anchoring is remarkably resistant to correction. Telling people about anchoring does not eliminate its effect. Asking people to consider whether the anchor is relevant does not eliminate it. Even paying people for accuracy does not eliminate it. The only reliably effective countermeasure is procedural: generate your own estimate before encountering the anchor, or use structured estimation procedures that prevent exposure to irrelevant anchors.

In professional settings, anchoring operates constantly. The first budget figure mentioned becomes the anchor for negotiation. The first evaluation score in a group discussion anchors everyone else. Previous year's performance becomes the anchor for this year's expectations. A consultant's proposed fee becomes the anchor for perceived value.

## How to Apply
1. **Generate independent estimates before discussion.** Have each evaluator or team member commit to a number privately before any group discussion. The first number voiced in a group anchors everyone else.
2. **Be suspicious of round numbers and convenient starting points.** When a budget is "approximately R10 million" or a timeline is "about 18 months," ask what generated that figure. Often it is an anchor, not an estimate.
3. **In negotiations, understand who sets the anchor.** The first offer in a negotiation becomes the anchor. This can work for you or against you — but you must be aware it is operating.
4. **Use structured estimation.** Decompose complex estimates into components and estimate each separately, rather than adjusting from a global anchor.

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluation team is estimating the number of beneficiaries a livelihoods programme has actually reached. The programme report claims 45,000, but the team suspects the real number is lower.
**Application:** Kahneman would say: "You have an anchoring problem. The programme's claim of 45,000 is now the anchor, and your team's estimate will be pulled toward it regardless of your intention to be independent. This is not a problem of integrity — it is a problem of cognitive architecture. The 45,000 figure has activated anchor-consistent reasoning: your minds are already generating explanations for why the number might be around 45,000, even as you try to assess it independently. The countermeasure is procedural. Before anyone looks at the programme's claim, have each team member independently estimate the number of beneficiaries based on the raw data — sampling evidence, administrative records, field observations. Record those estimates. Then compare them with the programme's figure. If you've already been exposed to the 45,000 anchor, at minimum use decomposition: estimate the number of communities reached, the average household participation rate per community, and multiply. This bottom-up approach is more resistant to the top-down anchor."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that informative anchors are just as problematic as arbitrary ones.
**Why:** An expert's initial estimate based on relevant information is a useful starting point. The problem is with irrelevant anchors — random numbers, wishful targets, manipulative opening offers — not with legitimate prior information.

**Don't:** Tell people "just ignore the anchor."
**Why:** This is like telling someone "just ignore the elephant in the room." Anchoring operates at the level of System 1; conscious intention to ignore it is insufficient. Use procedural countermeasures, not willpower.
