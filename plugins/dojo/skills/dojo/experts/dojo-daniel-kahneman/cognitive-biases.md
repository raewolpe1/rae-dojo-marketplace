# Cognitive Biases

## When to Use
- When someone asks about biases in general or wants to understand the heuristics and biases research programme.
- When multiple biases may be operating in a situation and need to be identified.
- When the question is about debiasing — whether and how it works.

## Core Concept
Biases are not mistakes. They are the predictable, systematic side-effects of cognitive heuristics — mental shortcuts that are generally useful but produce errors in specific, identifiable circumstances. The heuristics and biases research programme, launched by Kahneman and Tversky in the early 1970s, demonstrated that human judgment is not merely imperfect but systematically biased in ways that can be catalogued, predicted, and studied empirically.

The three original heuristics are representativeness (judging probability by similarity to a prototype), availability (judging frequency or likelihood by how easily examples come to mind), and anchoring (being pulled toward an initial value). Each produces a family of biases. Representativeness generates base-rate neglect, conjunction fallacy, and insensitivity to sample size. Availability generates risk perception distortions, recency bias, and the tendency to overweight vivid examples. Anchoring generates insufficient adjustment and contamination of quantitative judgments by irrelevant numbers.

The crucial finding is that these biases are not corrected by intelligence, expertise, or motivation. Smart people are biased. Experts are biased — sometimes more so, because expertise increases confidence without proportionally increasing accuracy. And wanting to be accurate does not prevent biases from operating, because they are generated by System 1 before System 2 has the opportunity to intervene.

Debiasing through awareness is largely ineffective. Knowing about anchoring does not prevent you from being anchored. The evidence on individual debiasing is weak. What works better is designing procedures, environments, and decision architectures that reduce the opportunity for bias to operate — structured interviews instead of unstructured ones, algorithms instead of clinical judgment where the evidence supports it, checklists that force consideration of base rates.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the heuristic, not just the bias.** "This is availability bias" is less useful than "You're judging the likelihood of this risk based on a vivid recent example rather than the base rate — that's the availability heuristic producing a distorted probability estimate."
2. **Look for multiple biases.** In any complex decision, several biases are likely operating simultaneously. Map them before recommending solutions.
3. **Don't promise debiasing through training.** Recommend procedural changes instead: structured formats, independent assessments, algorithms where appropriate.
4. **Distinguish bias from noise.** Bias is systematic error in one direction. Noise is random variability. Both degrade decisions, but they require different interventions.

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor evaluation team says "We've done bias training with our staff — they know about these biases now. Should we be confident the problem is addressed?"
**Application:** Kahneman would say: "You should not be confident. The evidence on debiasing through training is disappointing. People who learn about biases can often identify them in others but remain largely unable to detect or correct them in their own thinking — this is itself a bias, the bias blind spot. What bias training does effectively is increase awareness that bias exists, which can create organisational permission to implement procedural safeguards. The value is not in the knowledge itself but in the mandate it creates for better decision architecture. Ask instead: have you changed your evaluation procedures? Do evaluators assess criteria independently before group discussion? Do they consult base rates before making predictions? Are quantitative judgments elicited without anchoring values? Those procedural changes will do more than any amount of training."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Create long lists of biases without explaining how they work.
**Why:** Naming a bias without explaining the mechanism is intellectually lazy and practically useless. The mechanism is what enables prediction and intervention.

**Don't:** Use "bias" as a general accusation.
**Why:** Telling someone "you're biased" without specifying which bias and how it's operating is argumentative, not analytical. It generates defensiveness, not insight.
