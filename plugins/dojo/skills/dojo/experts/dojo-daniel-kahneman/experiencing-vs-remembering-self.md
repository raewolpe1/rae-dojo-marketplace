# Experiencing Self vs Remembering Self

## When to Use
- When the question involves how experiences are evaluated, remembered, or used to make future decisions.
- When wellbeing measurement — how satisfied people are with their lives, their work, their participation in a programme — is at stake.
- When the peak-end rule or duration neglect may be distorting retrospective assessments.

## Core Concept
Kahneman's research revealed that we have two selves — an experiencing self that lives through each moment and a remembering self that keeps score and makes decisions. These two selves evaluate the same experience differently, and it is the remembering self, not the experiencing self, that has the authority to make future choices.

The remembering self follows the peak-end rule: the retrospective evaluation of an experience is determined primarily by the intensity of the experience at its peak (best or worst moment) and at its end, with remarkably little weight given to the duration of the experience. A colonoscopy that is very painful at the end is remembered as worse than one that is equally painful throughout but ends on a less painful note — even if the second procedure lasted longer and involved more total pain. A holiday with a wonderful final day is remembered more fondly than a slightly better holiday with a mediocre ending.

Duration neglect means that the length of an experience has remarkably little impact on how it is remembered. A three-week holiday is not remembered as three times better than a one-week holiday, even if the moment-by-moment experience was equally good throughout. This has profound implications for how we evaluate programmes, plan events, and measure impact.

The practical dominance of the remembering self creates a paradox. People make decisions based on their memories of past experiences, not on the experiences themselves. If your memory of a programme is shaped by how it ended rather than by its cumulative impact, your decisions about whether to participate again, recommend it to others, or fund it in the future are driven by the ending, not by the totality. This is not irrational in any simple sense — memory is the record we carry forward — but it does mean that retrospective evaluation is a biased sample of the actual experience.

## How to Apply
1. **Distinguish experience measures from memory measures.** "Are we measuring how people felt during the programme, or how they remember feeling? These may differ substantially."
2. **Attend to endings.** If you want an experience to be remembered well, invest in how it concludes. A programme that ends with a strong positive experience will be remembered more favourably than one that fades out.
3. **Be sceptical of retrospective self-report.** Asking "how was the programme?" after completion captures the remembering self's narrative. Real-time measures — experience sampling, momentary assessments — capture the experiencing self.
4. **Consider what question is being answered.** Sometimes the remembering self's evaluation is the relevant one (will people choose this again?). Sometimes the experiencing self's evaluation matters more (is this programme actually improving daily wellbeing?).

## Examples
**Situation:** A community health programme ran for 18 months. Exit surveys show high satisfaction, but routine monitoring data from months 6–14 showed participants reporting frustration, confusion, and irregular attendance.
**Application:** Kahneman would say: "You are looking at a discrepancy between the experiencing self and the remembering self. The monitoring data captures the experiencing self — how people actually felt during the programme. The exit survey captures the remembering self — how people evaluate the programme retrospectively. The peak-end rule predicts this discrepancy: if the programme had a strong positive ending — perhaps the final phase involved a successful community event, a graduation ceremony, or tangible outcomes becoming visible — the remembering self will overweight that ending and underweight the months of frustration. The satisfaction score is not wrong; it is a genuine measure of how people remember the experience. But it is a poor guide to what the experience was actually like for most of its duration. If you are trying to decide whether to replicate this programme, the monitoring data is more diagnostic than the exit survey. If you are trying to predict whether participants will recommend it to others, the exit survey is more predictive, because people recommend based on memory, not on moment-by-moment experience."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume the remembering self is always wrong.
**Why:** The remembering self is a different evaluator, not an incorrect one. It answers a different question: not "how was this?" but "how do I feel about this now, and would I choose it again?" Both questions matter.

**Don't:** Use peak-end only for endings.
**Why:** The "peak" matters as much as the "end." An experience with a profoundly positive (or negative) peak moment will be strongly coloured by that moment, regardless of the ending.
