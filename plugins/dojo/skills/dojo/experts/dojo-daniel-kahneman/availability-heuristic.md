# Availability Heuristic

## When to Use
- When people are judging the frequency or probability of events based on how easily examples come to mind.
- When a vivid, recent, or emotionally powerful example is distorting risk assessment or resource allocation.
- When media coverage or personal experience is driving decisions that should be based on statistical evidence.

## Core Concept
The availability heuristic is a mental shortcut where people estimate the frequency or probability of an event by how easily examples of it come to mind. Events that are vivid, recent, emotionally salient, or heavily covered in media are easier to recall, and therefore judged as more frequent or probable than they actually are. Events that are less dramatic, more gradual, or personally unfamiliar are judged as less frequent or probable than they actually are.

This heuristic is often adequate. Things that happen frequently are generally easier to recall than things that happen rarely. But it produces systematic errors when ease of recall diverges from actual frequency — when something is memorable because it is vivid rather than because it is common. After a plane crash, people overestimate the risk of flying. After a colleague's child is diagnosed with a rare disease, parents overestimate that disease's prevalence. After a fraud case makes headlines, auditors overweight fraud risk relative to more common but less dramatic errors.

Availability also operates through personal experience. If you have personally witnessed a programme failure, you will overestimate the frequency of programme failure generally. If you have never encountered corruption in your work, you will underestimate its prevalence. Your sample of one — your own experience — is weighted far more heavily than it deserves, simply because it is the most available evidence.

The institutional consequence is that organisations often allocate resources to vivid, available risks rather than to the risks that data shows are most significant. Donor priorities shift after dramatic events — a famine, a pandemic, a natural disaster — not because those events changed the base rates of what matters most, but because they changed what is most available in decision-makers' minds.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask for base rates.** When someone judges a risk as high, ask: "What is the actual frequency? Where is the data?" Availability makes people skip this step.
2. **Identify the vivid example.** "Is there a specific recent event that is making this seem more likely than the data supports?"
3. **Check for asymmetric availability.** "Are you aware of cases where this went wrong, but unaware of the much larger number of cases where it went right? Failures are more available than successes."
4. **Counter with statistics, not competing stories.** Another vivid example in the opposite direction just adds noise. Data counters availability; stories amplify it.

## Examples
**Situation:** After a high-profile case of aid diversion in a neighbouring country, a development agency is considering adding three additional layers of financial controls to all its programmes, significantly increasing transaction costs.
**Application:** Kahneman would say: "The availability heuristic is driving this response. A vivid, recent, emotionally charged example of aid diversion has made diversion feel like a much larger risk than the base rate data supports. The question to ask is: what is the actual rate of aid diversion across your portfolio? If you have data on that — and many organisations do not, which is itself a problem — compare the expected cost of diversion against the expected cost of the additional controls. Three layers of new controls will slow disbursement, increase administrative burden, reduce programming flexibility, and absorb staff time. If the base rate of diversion is low, the controls may cost more than the problem they prevent. But right now, no one in the room is doing that calculation. They are responding to the availability of a dramatic recent case, and they are feeling the pressure to act — because not acting after a vivid event feels reckless, even when acting produces a net cost."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss availability-based judgments entirely.
**Why:** Sometimes what comes to mind easily is genuinely common. The heuristic often works. The question is whether ease of recall is tracking actual frequency or is being driven by vividness, recency, or emotional impact.

**Don't:** Counter one vivid story with another.
**Why:** Competing anecdotes do not cancel out — they just make both scenarios feel more available. Counter availability with data, not with different stories.
