# Kahneman Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When Kahneman's findings need to be translated into practical improvements in professional judgment and decision-making.
- When someone is designing evaluation processes, strategic planning, or organisational decision architecture.
- When the question is "how do I actually make better decisions, given everything we know about bias and noise?"

## Core Concept
Kahneman's applied legacy is not a list of biases to memorise but a set of principles for designing better decision processes. The core insight is that better decisions come from better procedures, not from better intentions. You cannot think your way out of bias. You can, however, build environments, processes, and systems that reduce the opportunity for bias and noise to operate.

The practical framework has several pillars. First, structured decision-making: break complex judgments into independent components, assess each component separately before forming a global judgment, and use explicit criteria with anchoring examples. This reduces both bias (particularly halo effects and anchoring) and noise (particularly pattern noise). Second, reference class forecasting: for any planning exercise, identify the base rate for similar projects before constructing an inside-view estimate. Third, independent elicitation: have each decision-maker form their judgment independently before group discussion, to prevent anchoring on the first opinion voiced. Fourth, pre-mortem analysis: before committing to a plan, imagine it has failed and work backward to identify what went wrong, to counteract overconfidence and the planning fallacy. Fifth, decision hygiene: a set of organisational practices analogous to hand hygiene in medicine — not solving any single dramatic problem but reducing the background rate of error across all decisions.

For consultants and evaluators, the implications are direct. Evaluation rubrics should decompose quality into dimensions assessed independently. Interview protocols should be structured. Budget estimates should be checked against reference classes. Donor presentations should include both gain and loss frames. Team assessments should be elicited independently before group discussion. These are not theoretical ideals — they are practical procedures that reduce measurable error in professional judgment.

## How to Apply
1. **Design evaluation rubrics with independent dimensions.** Assess relevance, effectiveness, efficiency, and sustainability separately before forming an overall judgment. This prevents halo effects.
2. **Use pre-mortem in programme design.** Before finalising a programme design, have the team imagine it has failed at midline and diagnose the failure. This surfaces risks that optimism conceals.
3. **Implement reference class forecasting in budgets and timelines.** Before submitting a budget or timeline, identify five similar projects and use their actual outcomes as the baseline.
4. **Elicit independent judgments before group discussion.** Have each team member write down their assessment before any group conversation. Compare the independent assessments before discussing.
5. **Conduct noise audits.** Periodically have multiple evaluators independently assess the same case. If the variability is large, fix the process before fixing the people.

## Examples
**Situation:** A consulting firm wants to improve the quality of its programme evaluations. They ask: "What's the single most impactful change we could make?"
**Application:** Kahneman would say: "If I had to choose one intervention, it would be structured independent assessment. Currently, your evaluation teams likely form their assessments through group discussion, where the first person to voice an opinion anchors everyone else, where the most senior person's view dominates, and where the team converges on a consensus that feels robust but is actually an artefact of the discussion process rather than a genuine synthesis of independent judgments. Change this: have each evaluator independently rate each evaluation criterion before any group discussion. Record the independent ratings. Then discuss the discrepancies — not to pressure anyone to change, but to understand why qualified professionals see the same evidence differently. You will discover that much of what you thought was agreement was actually anchoring, and much of the variability you discover will be noise that you can reduce through better calibration. This single procedural change will improve both the accuracy and the consistency of your evaluations more than any amount of training in evaluation methodology."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present Kahneman as a catalogue of cognitive failures.
**Why:** The practical legacy is constructive: designing better procedures, not lamenting human imperfection. Lists of biases without procedural recommendations are intellectually interesting and practically useless.

**Don't:** Apply decision hygiene dogmatically.
**Why:** Not every decision warrants structured protocols. The investment in procedure should be proportional to the stakes. For consequential, irreversible decisions, invest heavily in decision hygiene. For routine, reversible ones, efficient judgment is fine.
