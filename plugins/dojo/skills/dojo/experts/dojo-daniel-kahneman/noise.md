# Noise

## When to Use
- When equally qualified professionals are producing substantially different judgments on the same case or question.
- When organisational decision-making shows unwanted variability — different evaluators, different days, different outcomes.
- When structured decision procedures are needed to reduce inconsistency.

## Core Concept
Noise, in Kahneman's technical sense, is unwanted variability in judgments that should be identical. Where bias is systematic error in one direction, noise is scatter — random variability that degrades the accuracy of decisions without anyone noticing. Kahneman's final major work, "Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment" (2021, with Sibony and Sunstein), argued that noise is as costly as bias and far more neglected.

The noise audit is the diagnostic tool. Take a set of cases — insurance claims, criminal sentences, programme applications, employee evaluations — and have multiple qualified professionals judge each case independently. The variability across judges on the same case is noise. In study after study, the amount of noise is shocking: different judges assign wildly different sentences for the same crime; different underwriters quote dramatically different premiums for the same risk; different doctors give different diagnoses for the same symptoms.

Noise has three components. Level noise is systematic differences between judges — one evaluator is consistently harsh, another is consistently lenient. Pattern noise is idiosyncratic responses to specific cases — one evaluator responds harshly to a particular type of case while another responds leniently, in ways that vary unpredictably. Occasion noise is variability within a single judge over time — the same person makes different judgments on Monday than on Friday, before lunch than after lunch, in good weather than in bad weather.

The organisational cost of noise is enormous and invisible. If your evaluation team would assign different quality ratings to the same programme depending on which evaluator is assigned, the ratings are partly about the programme and partly about the evaluator. Neither the organisation nor the evaluated programme knows how much of the rating reflects genuine quality and how much reflects the noise of evaluator assignment.

The antidote to noise is structured decision-making: breaking complex judgments into component assessments, using rating scales with explicit anchors, having evaluators assess dimensions independently before forming global judgments, and using algorithms where appropriate. These procedures reduce noise at the cost of constraining individual judgment — which is precisely the point.

## How to Apply
1. **Conduct a noise audit.** Have multiple qualified people independently judge the same cases. Measure the variability. Most organisations that do this are shocked by the results.
2. **Use structured protocols.** Break complex judgments into component dimensions. Assess each dimension independently. Use explicit criteria and anchoring examples.
3. **Delay holistic judgment.** Assess individual criteria first, then form the overall assessment. Starting with a global impression and working backward contaminates the component assessments.
4. **Accept the trade-off.** Structured procedures reduce noise but also reduce flexibility. The trade-off is real, and organisations need to decide how much variability they are willing to accept.

## Examples
**Situation:** A development agency asks three independent evaluators to rate the same programme against the same criteria. They produce ratings of 3/5, 4/5, and 2/5 — a range of two points on a five-point scale.
**Application:** Kahneman would say: "You have just conducted an informal noise audit, and the result should concern you. A two-point range on a five-point scale means the rating is substantially determined by which evaluator you happened to assign. This is noise — unwanted variability that degrades the information value of the rating. The question is: is this spread due to level noise (one evaluator is systematically more generous), pattern noise (they respond differently to specific features of this programme), or occasion noise (one evaluator had a bad day)? The solution is procedural. First, calibrate your evaluators — give them the same five cases, compare their ratings, discuss the discrepancies, and align on what each rating level means. Second, structure the assessment — instead of 'rate the programme overall,' have each evaluator independently rate specific dimensions: relevance, effectiveness, efficiency, sustainability. Third, use explicit anchoring examples: 'a score of 4 means [specific description].' These procedures will not eliminate noise, but they will reduce it significantly. The alternative — accepting that your programme ratings are substantially influenced by evaluator assignment — is accepting that your quality assurance system is partly random."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Confuse noise with legitimate disagreement.
**Why:** Two evaluators who disagree because they weight different criteria or hold different values are not producing noise — they are reflecting genuine differences in perspective. Noise is unwanted variability, not all variability.

**Don't:** Eliminate noise through rigid standardisation without acknowledging costs.
**Why:** Reducing noise requires constraining individual judgment. This improves consistency but may sacrifice the nuance and contextual sensitivity that human judgment at its best provides. The trade-off should be explicit.
