# The Price of Money

## When to Use
- When evaluating central bank rate decisions — whether the Fed, the ECB, the Bank of England, the SARB, or any monetary authority.
- When diagnosing the consequences of rates held below the rate of inflation, below the rate of nominal GDP growth, or below the rate that a free market would set.
- When a discussion touches on Bagehot's principles, the classical gold standard, or the history of monetary arrangements.
- When someone asks whether government bond yields are "attractive" without asking whether they compensate for the risks embedded in the sovereign's fiscal trajectory.
- When applying the framework to South Africa: the SARB's repo rate, SA CPI, SA real rates, rand sensitivity to rate differentials with developed markets.

## Fails When
- **You confuse a rate diagnosis with a rate prediction.** Grant diagnoses whether rates are honest — whether they reflect the true scarcity of capital, the true rate of inflation, the true creditworthiness of the borrower. He does not predict where rates will be next quarter. The diagnosis is about integrity, not forecasting.
- **You treat all rate cuts as errors and all rate hikes as virtues.** The argument is not "rates should always be high." It is that rates should be set by the market, not administered by a committee, and that when they are administered below the market-clearing level, distortions accumulate. A rate cut in response to genuine deflation is different from a rate cut in response to an equity-market tantrum.
- **You forget the human element.** Rate suppression is not an abstract policy error. It is a policy that redistributes wealth from savers to borrowers, from the prudent to the speculative, from the old to the leveraged. It has distributional consequences that Grant, unlike most monetary economists, insists on naming.

## Core Concept

The interest rate is the price of money. More precisely, it is the price at which the present is exchanged for the future — the rate at which a lender parts with purchasing power today in return for a promise of more purchasing power tomorrow. It is, in Grant's formulation, the gravitational constant of the financial universe: the force that holds all other valuations in orbit. Change the gravitational constant, and every object in the system shifts position. Suppress the gravitational constant, and the objects float free — untethered from the discipline of scarcity, from the calculus of risk and reward, from the ancient truth that money has a cost because time has a value.

Every asset in a market economy is, at bottom, a claim on future cash flows, and the value of that claim is determined by the rate at which those future cash flows are discounted back to the present. The interest rate is the discount rate. Lower it, and the present value of every future cash flow rises: equities, real estate, long-duration bonds, private equity, venture capital, the promise of a start-up that will earn nothing for a decade but might, someday, earn everything. Raise it, and the present value falls. This is not opinion. It is the algebra of discounted cash flow — the most basic identity in finance.

The radical proposition is not that rates matter. Everyone agrees that rates matter. The radical proposition is that central banks have no business setting them — that the interest rate, like any other price, conveys information only when it is determined by the voluntary interaction of borrowers and lenders in a free market. When the Fed or the SARB or any other central bank fixes the rate, the information is corrupted. The price of money no longer reflects the scarcity of savings, the demand for capital, the creditworthiness of borrowers, or the rate of inflation. It reflects the preferences of a committee — preferences that are, by the committee's own admission, conditioned by models of the economy that are frequently wrong and revised after the fact.

### The Bagehot Standard

Walter Bagehot, the Victorian editor of The Economist and the author of Lombard Street (1873), articulated the classical doctrine of the lender of last resort in language so precise that it ought to be carved above the entrance to every central bank:

Lend freely. At a penalty rate. Against good collateral. To solvent institutions.

Every clause is load-bearing. "Lend freely" — do not ration credit in a panic, because rationing credit in a panic is what turns a liquidity crisis into a solvency crisis. "At a penalty rate" — make the price of rescue high enough to discourage future dependence on rescue; the penalty rate is information, and the information is: you are in distress, and distress has a cost. "Against good collateral" — ensure that the central bank's balance sheet is protected and that the borrower has something of value to pledge, which is itself evidence that the borrower is worth saving. "To solvent institutions" — do not rescue the insolvent; let them fail, because failure is the mechanism by which bad loans are liquidated and capital is redirected to better uses.

Modern central banking has preserved Bagehot's first instruction and abandoned the other three. The Fed lends freely — at subsidised rates, against dubious collateral, to institutions whose solvency is doubtful. The penalty rate has been replaced by the subsidy. Good collateral has been replaced by mortgage-backed securities of uncertain provenance and Treasury bonds issued by a government running trillion-dollar deficits. And solvent institutions have been replaced by the doctrine of "too big to fail," which is a doctrine not of solvency but of political convenience.

The consequence is moral hazard at systemic scale. Financial institutions take risks they would not take if they bore the full cost of failure, because they know — correctly — that the central bank will rescue them at a price below the cost of their recklessness. The Greenspan Put, the Bernanke Put, the Powell Put: the terminology changes, the dynamic does not. The central bank has become not the lender of last resort but the enabler of first resort, and the financial system has adjusted its behaviour accordingly.

### Rate Suppression and Its Consequences

The consequences of sustained rate suppression are not speculative. They are documented across every episode of administered cheap money in financial history:

**Asset-price inflation.** When the discount rate is artificially low, the present value of future cash flows is artificially high. Asset prices — equities, real estate, private equity, venture capital — rise beyond what fundamentals justify. The rise is not random; it is arithmetic. Lower the denominator, and the fraction increases. The resulting "wealth effect" is celebrated as prosperity, but it is prosperity of a peculiar kind — available only to those who own assets, invisible to those who do not, and reversible when rates normalise.

**Credit-standard deterioration.** Cheap money makes bad loans temporarily profitable. A borrower who cannot service debt at 6% can service it at 2%, and a lender who cannot earn a spread on sound credits at 2% reaches for yield in unsound credits. The deterioration is gradual and, at each stage, rationalised: "the borrower's cash flows are resilient," "the collateral is well-located," "the structure includes protections." The protections, on examination, include covenants so loose that they protect nothing. This is the subject of Money of the Mind, and the pattern has repeated without variation since 1992.

**Capital misallocation.** Interest rates ration capital among competing uses. At an honest rate, only projects whose expected return exceeds the cost of capital attract funding. At a suppressed rate, projects that would destroy value at honest rates appear to create value at subsidised rates. The result is malinvestment — capital directed to uses that will prove uneconomic when rates normalise. The "zombie companies" that proliferated after 2008 — companies that earned too little to service their debt but survived because the debt was cheap enough to roll over — are the visible symptom of this misallocation.

**Risk mispricing.** When the risk-free rate is suppressed, the entire structure of risk premiums is distorted. Credit spreads compress — not because credit risk has diminished but because the alternative to taking credit risk is accepting a return below inflation. The compression of spreads signals not safety but desperation. The investor who reaches for yield in high-yield bonds, leveraged loans, or emerging-market debt because government bonds pay nothing is not making a risk assessment. He is making a Hobson's choice imposed by the central bank's rate policy.

### The South African Application

The SARB has, by the standards of developed-market central banks, maintained a relatively disciplined rate policy. The repo rate has generally been set to deliver a positive real interest rate — that is, a rate above CPI inflation. This is not an accident. It reflects the SARB's institutional commitment to inflation targeting, its awareness of the rand's sensitivity to capital flows, and the hard-won lesson that negative real rates in an emerging-market context invite currency depreciation, capital flight, and imported inflation.

The positive real rate is the SARB's most underappreciated asset. It means that South African savers are compensated — imperfectly, but meaningfully — for deferring consumption. It means that the cost of capital in South Africa tells something approximating the truth about the scarcity of investable funds. And it means that the South African banking system faces a discipline that its American and European counterparts escaped for a decade: the discipline of lending at rates that require genuine creditworthiness from the borrower.

The pressure to cut — always present, always politically motivated — should be evaluated against a simple historical test: has any emerging-market economy improved its long-run growth trajectory by suppressing its real interest rate? The record suggests the opposite. Negative real rates in emerging markets have produced currency crises (Turkey), capital flight (Argentina), inflation spirals (several episodes across Latin America and Africa), and the destruction of domestic savings. South Africa's relatively stable banking system and relatively credible monetary policy are, in significant part, a consequence of the SARB's refusal to administer rates at the levels favoured by politicians and property developers.

The relevant metrics for evaluating SARB policy are: (a) the repo rate minus headline CPI — the ex-post real rate; (b) the repo rate minus expected inflation — the ex-ante real rate; (c) the spread between SA real rates and US or European real rates — the differential that influences capital flows and the rand; and (d) the quality of credit growth in the South African banking system, measured not by volume but by lending standards, household debt-to-income ratios, and the trajectory of non-performing loans.

## How to Apply
1. **Start with the real rate.** Calculate the policy rate minus inflation (both headline and core, both ex-post and ex-ante). If the real rate is negative, money is being given away. Ask: to whom, and at whose expense?
2. **Compare to nominal GDP growth.** If the policy rate is below the rate of nominal GDP growth, monetary policy is accommodative by definition — the economy is growing faster than the cost of borrowing, which is an invitation to lever up.
3. **Ask what Bagehot would say.** Is the central bank lending freely, at a penalty rate, against good collateral, to solvent institutions? Or has it abandoned the penalty, accepted dubious collateral, and rescued the insolvent? The distance between current practice and Bagehot's prescription is a measure of the moral hazard accumulated in the system.
4. **Trace the distortions downstream.** Rate suppression does not operate in isolation. Follow it to asset prices (are they elevated relative to fundamentals?), to credit standards (are they deteriorating?), to capital allocation (are zombie companies surviving on cheap debt?), to risk premiums (are spreads compressed beyond what credit fundamentals justify?).
5. **Find the historical analogue.** John Law and the Mississippi Bubble. The Crédit Mobilier. The 1920s call-money market. The Latin American debt crisis. Japan after 1989. Subprime. Each episode of rate manipulation has a predecessor, and the predecessor illuminates the range of outcomes.

## Examples
**Situation:** A South African asset manager argues that the SARB should cut the repo rate by 200 basis points to stimulate economic growth, noting that SA's rate is far above the Fed's.
**Application:** The comparison to the Fed's rate is irrelevant. The relevant comparison is to SA's own inflation rate, SA's credit growth, and the quality of SA's new lending. A 200bp cut would push the real rate toward zero or negative territory, narrow the SA-US rate differential (weakening the rand), cheapen credit at the margin (loosening lending standards), and signal to the market that the SARB has shifted from inflation targeting to growth targeting — a shift that no emerging-market central bank has made without eventually regretting it. The historical analogues are Turkey (2021-2023, rate cuts into rising inflation, currency collapse) and Brazil (periodic episodes of political pressure on the central bank, each followed by inflation and capital flight). The SARB's positive real rate is not the problem. It is the solution — the institutional anchor that keeps SA's monetary credibility, such as it is, intact.

**Situation:** An investor asks whether US Treasury yields at 4.5% are "finally attractive" after the post-pandemic rate shock.
**Application:** The yield tells you the price. The question is whether the price compensates for the risk. Calculate: is 4.5% above or below the rate of US CPI inflation? (If above, the real yield is positive — a necessary but not sufficient condition for attractiveness.) Is it above or below the rate at which the US government's debt is compounding? (If below, the debt trajectory is unsustainable without either fiscal consolidation or inflation — neither of which is priced into a 4.5% yield.) Is the US government a AAA credit in substance or merely in rating? (The fiscal deficit, the entitlement trajectory, the political inability to consolidate — these are not AAA characteristics.) The historical analogue: British consols in the late Victorian period traded at yields that assumed the British Empire was a permanent institution. It was not. Yields reflect assumptions, and assumptions can be wrong.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat low rates as inherently stimulative.
**Why:** Low rates stimulate credit growth, not necessarily economic growth. If the credit flows to speculation, asset inflation, and zombie companies, the "stimulus" produces fragility, not prosperity. The distinction between credit quantity and credit quality is the distinction between the appearance of growth and the reality of malinvestment.

**Don't:** Assume that inflation targeting at 2% constitutes sound monetary policy.
**Why:** The 2% target is an arbitrary convention, adopted without rigorous justification and maintained by institutional inertia. It produces a systematic bias toward monetary ease (because central bankers fear deflation more than inflation) and provides intellectual cover for rate suppression (because as long as measured CPI is near 2%, the central bank declares victory, regardless of what is happening to asset prices, credit standards, or financial stability). Bagehot did not target 2% inflation. He targeted the integrity of the monetary system.

**Don't:** Ignore distributional consequences.
**Why:** Rate suppression is not a neutral policy. It transfers wealth from savers to borrowers, from the old to the young (who can lever up), from the un-leveraged to the leveraged, from the prudent to the speculative. In South Africa, where inequality is already extreme and where many households depend on interest income from savings, the distributional consequences of rate suppression are particularly severe. An honest rate policy is also a just rate policy — a point Grant makes with more frequency and force than most monetary economists.
