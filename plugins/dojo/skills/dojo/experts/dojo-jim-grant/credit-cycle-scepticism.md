# Credit Cycle Scepticism

## When to Use
- When evaluating whether credit conditions are sound, deteriorating, or in the early stages of recovery.
- When a market participant argues that low default rates prove credit quality is strong (they prove only that the cycle has not yet turned).
- When assessing the quality of new lending — covenant terms, leverage multiples, the eagerness of lenders relative to the creditworthiness of borrowers.
- When diagnosing why a financial institution, sector, or economy that appeared healthy is suddenly in distress (the answer is almost always that the credit deterioration preceded the distress by years, not months).
- When applying the framework to South Africa: household credit growth, corporate lending standards, the trajectory of non-performing loans in the SA banking system, the quality of new credit extension.

## Fails When
- **You use the credit cycle to time markets.** The credit cycle tells you where you are in the story — whether the lending standards are tightening or loosening, whether the risks are being created or recognised. It does not tell you when the turn will come. The turn can be delayed by years of monetary accommodation, by regulatory forbearance, by the sheer momentum of a credit expansion. Trying to time the turn is a different activity from diagnosing the quality of credit, and Grant does the latter, not the former.
- **You treat all credit growth as dangerous.** Credit growth in the context of tight lending standards, strong covenants, and genuine economic expansion is healthy. Credit growth in the context of loose standards, weak covenants, and reach-for-yield behaviour is dangerous. The distinction is everything. The volume of credit tells you the size of the exposure; the quality of credit tells you the probability of loss.
- **You ignore the lender.** The credit cycle is not an abstraction. It is enacted by specific institutions — banks, CLO managers, pension funds, retail investors — each with their own incentives, constraints, and blind spots. Who is lending, and why, reveals more about the cycle's maturity than any aggregate statistic.

## Core Concept

The credit cycle is the central organising dynamic of financial capitalism. It is older than central banking, older than fiat money, older than the joint-stock corporation. It was present in Renaissance Florence and in Augustan Rome. It persists because it arises from invariant features of human psychology and institutional incentive: the tendency of lenders to extend credit on progressively easier terms as confidence builds, and to withdraw it on progressively harsher terms as confidence collapses.

Grant's formulation is that **credit quality is the leading indicator — of everything.** Not GDP, which is a coincident or lagging indicator that tells you what the economy did, not what it will do. Not equity prices, which reflect the mood of the market rather than the soundness of its balance sheets. Not default rates, which are the lowest possible form of credit intelligence — by the time defaults spike, the loans that are defaulting were booked years ago, and the damage is already done. The leading indicator is the quality of new credit being extended: the terms on which lenders lend, the conditions they impose, the risks they accept, and the protections they demand.

### The Four Acts

The credit cycle proceeds through four acts, repeating with variations in detail but not in structure since the invention of modern banking:

**Act One: Recovery.** Credit has just been destroyed. The previous cycle ended in default, loss, and recrimination. The surviving lenders are chastened, risk-averse, and conservative. They demand strong covenants, ample collateral, low leverage, and demonstrable cash-flow coverage. Credit is scarce and expensive. Only the most creditworthy borrowers can obtain it. Paradoxically, this is when credit is safest — precisely because no one wants to extend it. The scars of the previous bust are fresh, and caution is the dominant instinct.

**Act Two: Expansion.** Time passes. The losses of the previous cycle fade from institutional memory (helped by the departure of the loan officers who booked them and the arrival of a new generation that did not). Confidence returns. Competition among lenders intensifies. Standards loosen — not dramatically, but at the margin. A maintenance covenant becomes an incurrence covenant. A leverage limit of 4x EBITDA drifts to 5x. The collateral package thins. Each relaxation is small, is justified by "improved market conditions," and is individually defensible. Collectively, they mark the transition from prudence to optimism. This is the period the financial press calls "a healthy credit environment" and "improved access to capital."

**Act Three: Excess.** The competition among lenders is now fierce. Covenant-lite issuance — loans with no financial maintenance covenants — surges as a proportion of total issuance. EBITDA "add-backs" (speculative adjustments to reported earnings that flatter the borrower's leverage ratio) become standard. Leverage ratios that would have been unthinkable in Act One are routine. The weakest borrowers find the easiest terms, because the lenders, desperate for yield in a world where central banks have suppressed the risk-free rate, compete to lend to anyone who will borrow. The CLO machine — collateralised loan obligations that buy leveraged loans, tranche them, and sell the tranches to investors who have never met the borrower — operates at full speed, providing a bottomless bid for new issuance. The sell-side calls this "deep and liquid markets." Grant calls it the systematic manufacture of future losses.

**Act Four: Reckoning.** A trigger — a recession, a rate shock, a sector-specific implosion, sometimes nothing more than a shift in sentiment — reveals that the loans booked in Act Three were not worth what the lenders paid. Defaults rise. Recovery rates fall (because the covenants that once protected lenders were given away in Act Three, and the collateral that once secured the loans was diluted by successive borrowings). Credit contracts. The institutions that lent most aggressively in Act Three — the ones that competed away their protections for the privilege of putting money to work — suffer the largest losses. And the cycle returns to Act One, where the survivors swear they have learned the lesson, and will learn it again, and again, and again.

### Reading the Credit Quality

The diagnostic art is reading the quality of credit in real time — during Act Two, when the deterioration is still marginal, still deniable, still invisible to anyone who looks only at default rates and headline spreads.

**Covenants.** The single most informative data point in the credit cycle is the covenant package on new leveraged loans. In Act One, loans carry maintenance covenants — quarterly tests that require the borrower to demonstrate that its leverage, interest coverage, and fixed-charge ratios remain within agreed limits. Breach the covenant, and the lender has the right to accelerate the loan, renegotiate the terms, or seize the collateral. In Act Three, maintenance covenants disappear, replaced by incurrence covenants — which are triggered only if the borrower takes a new action (a new borrowing, a dividend, an acquisition). If the borrower does nothing, the covenant does nothing. It protects the lender only against overt acts of self-destruction, not against gradual deterioration. The shift from maintenance to incurrence covenants is the canary in the credit mine.

**Leverage multiples.** In Act One, a leverage ratio of 4x EBITDA is aggressive. In Act Two, 5x is routine. In Act Three, 6x or 7x is marketed with straight faces. The progression is predictable, and each increment is justified by the argument that "this company's cash flows are resilient enough to support the leverage" — an argument that is tested only in the downturn and invariably found wanting, because the cash flows were modelled using assumptions generated during the boom.

**EBITDA adjustments.** The quality of the denominator matters as much as the numerator. In Act Three, the "EBITDA" used to calculate leverage ratios bears an increasingly strained relationship to actual operating earnings. Add-backs — adjustments for "one-time" costs, "synergies" from acquisitions not yet completed, "run-rate" revenue not yet earned — inflate the denominator, deflate the leverage ratio, and create the illusion of creditworthiness. Grant's Observer has documented episodes where the adjusted EBITDA used to market a loan exceeded the company's actual EBITDA by 30-50%. This is not accounting. It is salesmanship.

**The identity of the lender.** Who is providing the credit reveals as much as the terms on which it is provided. In Act One, credit is provided by banks that hold the loans on their balance sheets and bear the consequences of their underwriting judgements. In Act Three, credit is provided by CLO managers who buy loans, tranche the risk, sell the tranches, and retain nothing — neither the loan nor the responsibility. The separation of origination from ownership is a recurring feature of credit manias, from the 19th-century railway bond market to the 21st-century CLO market. It ensures that the person making the lending decision does not suffer the consequences of getting it wrong.

**The mood.** The hardest and most important diagnostic: the mood of the lending community. In Act One, the mood is fear. In Act Two, it is confidence. In Act Three, it is something between confidence and aggression — a restless, competitive energy driven by the need to deploy capital, to maintain returns, to keep up with the competitor who is lending at tighter spreads and looser terms. Grant describes this as the moment when the lender's greatest fear shifts from losing money to losing deal flow. That shift in fear is the moment the cycle turns from expansion to excess.

### The South African Application

South Africa's credit cycle operates under constraints that differ from the American and European experience, but the underlying dynamic is identical: lending standards loosen in booms and tighten in busts, credit quality deteriorates before losses are recognised, and the leading indicators are found in the fine print of loan agreements, not in the headline statistics.

**Household credit.** The ratio of household debt to disposable income in South Africa rose sharply in the 2000s, fuelled by low real rates (by SA standards), an expanding middle class, and aggressive retail lending by the major banks. The National Credit Act (2005) was a legislative response to deteriorating consumer lending standards — an explicit acknowledgement that the credit cycle had entered Act Three in the retail segment. The Act imposed affordability assessments, prohibited reckless lending, and required debt counselling for over-indebted consumers. It was, in effect, a regulatory covenant imposed because the market's own covenants had failed.

**Corporate credit.** SA corporate lending has generally been more conservative than its American counterpart, in part because the South African banking system is concentrated (the big four hold the bulk of corporate lending) and in part because the SARB's relatively high real rates impose a natural discipline on leverage. But the same dynamics apply at the margin: when SA corporate credit grows rapidly, the marginal loans are weaker than the average; when credit growth slows, the marginal loans are stronger. The quality of SA corporate credit is best assessed through non-performing loan ratios, provisioning levels, and the trajectory of sectoral lending — particularly to property, construction, and the SOE-adjacent sectors where political lending standards have historically been weakest.

**The SOE question.** South Africa's state-owned enterprises — Eskom, Transnet, SAA, the Land Bank — represent a parallel credit cycle in which lending standards were determined not by commercial judgement but by political imperative. The resulting losses are a case study in what happens when the credit quality diagnostic is overridden by non-commercial considerations. The SOE debt overhang is, in Grant's framework, an Act Four that was delayed for years by government guarantees and now weighs on the sovereign balance sheet.

## How to Apply
1. **Determine the act.** Where is the credit cycle — in recovery (Act One), expansion (Act Two), excess (Act Three), or reckoning (Act Four)? The act is determined not by the level of credit but by the direction and quality of new credit extension.
2. **Read the covenants.** What covenant protections are lenders demanding? Are they tightening (Act One/early Act Two) or loosening (late Act Two/Act Three)? Covenant-lite issuance as a share of total issuance is the single best quantitative proxy for the cycle's maturity.
3. **Check the leverage.** What leverage ratios are the market accepting on new issuance? Compare to the ratios accepted at the peak of the previous cycle. If current ratios match or exceed the previous peak, the cycle is in Act Three.
4. **Examine the EBITDA.** Is the "EBITDA" used to market new deals a real number or an adjusted number? How large are the adjustments? Adjustments above 20% of reported EBITDA are a flag; adjustments above 30% are an alarm.
5. **Identify the lender.** Is credit being provided by institutions that retain the risk or by structures that distribute and disperse it? The more the risk is dispersed, the less the originator cares about quality — and the more certain it is that losses will surface in unexpected places.
6. **Ignore the default rate.** The default rate is a lagging indicator — it tells you what happened to loans booked years ago, not what is happening to loans being booked today. Low default rates in Act Three are not reassuring. They are irrelevant. The losses are being created now; they will be recognised later.
7. **Watch the mood.** Is the lending community afraid of losing money or afraid of losing deal flow? The former is Act One; the latter is Act Three.

## Examples
**Situation:** A South African bank executive reports strong credit growth and low non-performing loan ratios, arguing that the banking system is in excellent health.
**Application:** Low NPL ratios tell you about the quality of loans booked in the past — they are a measure of yesterday's credit standards, not today's. The diagnostic question is: what are today's lending standards? Are affordability assessments as rigorous as they were five years ago? Are property valuations used as collateral based on market values or on optimistic appraisals? Is the bank gaining market share by competing on price (tighter spreads) or on terms (looser covenants)? If credit growth is strong and NPL ratios are low, one of two things is true: either the credit quality is genuinely strong (possible in early Act Two), or the losses have been created but not yet recognised (probable in late Act Two or Act Three). The distinction is made by examining the quality of new lending, not the performance of old lending.

**Situation:** A credit investor argues that South African corporate bond spreads are attractive because they are wide relative to developed-market spreads.
**Application:** Wide spreads are necessary but not sufficient for attractiveness. The spread compensates for credit risk — but is the credit risk stable, improving, or deteriorating? If SA corporate credit quality is stable or improving (covenant terms tightening, leverage declining, cash-flow coverage strengthening), wide spreads represent genuine value. If credit quality is deteriorating (loosening standards, rising leverage, weakening coverage), wide spreads may be insufficient — they may widen further as the deterioration becomes visible. The historical analogue: in every credit cycle, the widest spreads occur not at the moment of maximum pessimism but at the moment of maximum realised loss — which is Act Four, when the loans booked in Act Three finally default. The investor who buys on spread alone, without assessing credit quality, is buying on price without knowing the value.

**Situation:** Global leveraged loan issuance is at a record, with covenant-lite loans representing 85% of new issuance.
**Application:** This is Act Three. The 85% covenant-lite figure means that lenders have competed away their contractual protections on 85 of every 100 new loans. They retain the right to receive interest and principal but have surrendered the early-warning mechanisms (maintenance covenants) that would allow them to intervene before deterioration becomes default. The historical analogue is the pre-2007 vintage of leveraged loans, which were also overwhelmingly covenant-lite and which produced losses that exceeded every model's prediction — because the models assumed that covenant protections would limit loss-given-default, and the protections had been given away. The lesson was clear, briefly remembered, and now fully forgotten. Money of the Mind, updated.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Confuse low default rates with strong credit quality.
**Why:** Default rates are the lowest form of credit intelligence. They measure the performance of loans booked years ago, not the quality of loans being booked today. A low default rate during Act Three is not evidence of health. It is evidence that the cycle has not yet turned — which is precisely what the Act Three lending standards ensure will eventually change.

**Don't:** Treat the credit cycle as an engineering problem with a mechanical solution.
**Why:** The credit cycle is not a machine; it is a human drama. The participants — lenders, borrowers, regulators, rating agencies, investors — are not rational agents optimising an objective function. They are human beings in the grip of institutional incentive, competitive pressure, career risk, and the chronic inability to internalise lessons learned in a previous crisis while the current expansion is making them rich. Treating the cycle as a mechanical problem (as Dalio's framework tends to do) produces elegant models and insufficient wisdom. The credit cycle requires not engineering but judgement — the kind of judgement that comes from reading financial history, not from calibrating a model.

**Don't:** Assume that regulation prevents credit excess.
**Why:** Regulation can moderate the credit cycle but cannot prevent it. The National Credit Act in South Africa moderated consumer lending excess but did not prevent the SOE debt crisis. Dodd-Frank in the United States moderated bank leverage but pushed risk-taking into the shadow banking system — CLOs, private credit, and leveraged lending by non-bank institutions outside the regulatory perimeter. The credit cycle finds the path of least resistance, and the path of least resistance is always the least regulated channel.
