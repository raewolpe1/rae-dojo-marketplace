# Policy Experimentation

## When to Use
- When designing a major reform programme.
- When debating gradualism vs big-bang.
- When dealing with uncertainty about which reform will work.

## Fails When
- **Speed is genuinely essential** (acute crisis response).
- **The political window is narrow** and demands decisive action.

## Core Concept

Policy experimentation is the stance that, given uncertainty about which reforms will work in which contexts, reforms should be designed to learn. Chinese economic reform since 1978 is the classic example — gradual, sequenced, geographically piloted (SEZs first, then broader), with course corrections informed by what worked.

Key elements:

**Pilot before scaling.** Reform components are introduced in selected regions or sectors before national rollout. The pilot generates evidence, exposes implementation challenges, and allows redesign.

**Reversibility.** Reforms designed so that failed elements can be reversed without unwinding the whole reform programme. Big-bang reforms with simultaneous changes across all areas leave no diagnostic ability when things go wrong.

**Sequencing.** Reforms in different areas affect each other. Capital account liberalisation before financial sector strengthening produces crises; trade liberalisation before tax base reform produces fiscal problems. The sequence matters.

**Distributional management.** Reforms produce winners and losers. Designing for distributional management — compensation, retraining, regional support — protects the political coalition for the reform.

**Embedded learning.** Build evaluation and feedback into the reform architecture. Not "design the reform, implement, evaluate after"; rather "design with learning, modify as you go."

The contrast with "shock therapy" (the post-Communist transition advice of the early 1990s) is sharp. Shock therapy produced collapse in Russia and elsewhere where the institutional preconditions for rapid market adjustment were absent. The Chinese experimental approach produced sustained growth precisely because it learned as it went.

## How to Apply

1. **Design pilot phases** for major reforms.
2. **Sequence reforms** by complementarity and dependency.
3. **Build evaluation into the reform architecture.**
4. **Manage distribution actively** to maintain political coalition.
5. **Be willing to reverse failed elements** rather than defending the whole package.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's energy sector restructuring — unbundling Eskom, opening generation, introducing renewable IPPs, restructuring grid.

**Application:** The reform is sufficiently complex that big-bang implementation would produce chaos. Sequenced approach: stabilise legacy debt; complete the unbundling; open the renewable generation market (already done); design transmission opening with grid operator independence; pilot wheeling arrangements before national rollout; build complementary skills and supplier development. Learn from each phase, adjust the design of the next.

**Result:** A reform programme structured for learning rather than for ideological completeness.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Confuse experimentation with indecision.
**Why:** Experimentation is structured learning; it is not the absence of direction.

**Don't:** Pilot indefinitely.
**Why:** Pilots that never scale are policy theatre. Design the scaling triggers and apply them.
