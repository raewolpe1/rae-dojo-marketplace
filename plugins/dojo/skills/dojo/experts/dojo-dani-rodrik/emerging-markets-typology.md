# Emerging Markets Typology

## When to Use
- When comparing countries or making cross-country lessons explicit.
- When the question is what kind of emerging market this country is.

## Fails When
- **The typology becomes a substitute for diagnosis** of the specific country.

## Core Concept

"Emerging markets" is a category that conceals more than it reveals. The countries grouped under this label differ enormously in structural features. A useful typology distinguishes:

**Manufacturing exporters** (Korea historically, China, Vietnam, Bangladesh, Mexico in some segments). Growth driven by manufacturing for export, structural transformation, capability accumulation. Catch-up trajectory, vulnerable to global demand and competitive pressure.

**Commodity exporters** (Saudi Arabia, Russia, Brazil partially, South Africa partially, much of Sub-Saharan Africa). Growth driven by commodity prices, vulnerable to terms-of-trade swings, often experiencing the resource curse — deindustrialisation, financialisation, real exchange rate appreciation that hurts non-commodity tradeables.

**Services-led emerging economies** (India to a substantial extent, Philippines, parts of Southeast Asia). Growth driven by IT and BPO services. Productive but with less labour absorption and capability spread than manufacturing-led growth.

**Financial centres and small open economies** (Singapore, Hong Kong, increasingly Mauritius). Specialised positions in global financial flows. Not a generalisable model for most emerging economies.

**Stalled middle-income economies** (parts of Latin America, much of the Middle East, South Africa increasingly). Reached middle-income, struggling to catch up further. The middle-income trap discussion.

Each type faces different structural challenges and requires different policy responses. The orthodox checklist applies equally badly to all; the diagnostic approach differs by type.

For South Africa: a hybrid of commodity exporter (mining), stalled middle-income (manufacturing decline, structural unemployment), and concentrated financial sector. The mix has specific implications — the resource curse partial dynamics, the deindustrialisation problem, the financial sector that does not finance productive transformation.

## How to Apply

1. **Locate the country typologically** to inform the diagnostic.
2. **Use cross-country comparison within types** for relevant lessons.
3. **Avoid generic "emerging markets" framings** that miss structural variation.

## Examples

**Situation:** Comparing South Africa with Turkey and Brazil.

**Application:** All three are stalled middle-income with specific structural problems — Turkey's exhausted construction-and-consumption model, Brazil's premature deindustrialisation, South Africa's resource-curse-plus-state-capability problem. The shared pattern: failure of an earlier growth model with no replacement strategy. The shared lesson: structural transformation requires deliberate industrial policy, institutional rebuilding, and political coalition for a multi-decade project.

**Result:** A cross-country lesson grounded in shared structural features rather than abstract emerging-market generalisation.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use the typology to replace the country diagnosis.
**Why:** Even within a type, countries differ. The typology is starting point, not endpoint.
