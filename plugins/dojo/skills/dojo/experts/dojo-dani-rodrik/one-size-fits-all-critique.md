# One-Size-Fits-All Critique

## When to Use
- When standard reform checklists (Washington Consensus, good governance, OECD recommendations) are being applied to a specific country without diagnostic engagement.
- When responding to "international best practice" framings.

## Fails When
- **Some elements of orthodox advice are genuinely useful** for the specific country and context — selective acceptance is appropriate.

## Core Concept

The one-size-fits-all critique is the recognition that country-specific binding constraints, institutional contexts, and political economies require country-specific policy responses. Universal policy templates are methodologically indefensible — they cannot match country variation in what is binding, what is feasible, and what is political-economically supported.

The orthodox response has been to acknowledge variation while retaining the universal checklist as default — "reform" plus country-specific adjustments. This is insufficient. The country-specific engagement should come first, with the orthodox checklist deployed only where it matches the diagnosis.

The argument is methodological, not anti-orthodox. Specific orthodox recommendations are sometimes correct for specific countries. The error is the universalism, not the content. Fiscal discipline is often important; sometimes it is the binding constraint. Trade openness is often beneficial; sometimes it is. The work is in determining when, not in applying everywhere.

The practical implication: development policy advice should start with diagnostic engagement (growth diagnostics, binding constraints analysis) and prescribe from the diagnosis. The checklist approach should be retired.

## How to Apply

1. **Resist the checklist** as starting point.
2. **Diagnose first**, prescribe second.
3. **Accept orthodox elements that match the diagnosis** — don't reject for the sake of rejection.
4. **Document the diagnostic basis** for the recommended policies.

## Examples

**Situation:** An IMF country review recommends the standard package — fiscal consolidation, structural reforms (labour market and product market deregulation), monetary tightening, trade openness — without country-specific diagnosis.

**Application:** The recommendations may be partially correct, but the methodology is wrong. Replace the checklist with a diagnostic: which of these constraints are actually binding? What would addressing each one do? In what sequence? The output may include some orthodox elements; it will exclude others; it will add country-specific elements the checklist misses.

**Result:** A reform programme grounded in diagnosis rather than template.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Reject orthodox elements categorically.
**Why:** Some are correct. The case is for diagnosis-led selection, not for blanket rejection.

**Don't:** Use the critique to defer all reform.
**Why:** Some reforms are urgent; the case is for the right reforms, not for endless analysis.
