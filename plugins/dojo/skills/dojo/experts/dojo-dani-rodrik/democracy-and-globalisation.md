# Democracy and Globalisation

## When to Use
- When trade or investment agreements constrain democratic policy-making.
- When technocratic framings of economic policy are dismissing democratic concerns.
- When defending democratic policy space from market discipline.

## Fails When
- **The democratic preference is for clearly catastrophic policy** (hyperinflation, default, banking crisis). Some technocratic constraints are reasonable.

## Core Concept

When deep economic integration and democratic politics conflict, democracy should take priority. This is not anti-globalisation; it is pro-democracy within a globalised economy.

The argument:

**Democracy has substantive value** beyond economic efficiency. The ability of citizens to choose their economic policies, even when those choices are economically sub-optimal by some metric, is part of what democracy means. Constraining democracy in the name of economic efficiency is a values choice that should be made transparently.

**The technocratic framing of economic policy is partial.** Many "technical" economic policy questions involve distributional choices, time-horizon trade-offs, and contested theories of how the economy works. Treating these as purely technical disguises the political content and disenfranchises affected populations.

**Markets discipline national policy** under deep integration. Capital flight, sovereign bond markets, currency pressure, trade dependency — these create real constraints on democratic policy choice. The question is whether to design integration to preserve democratic space or to accept the constraint as the price of integration.

**The post-1990 hyperglobalisation model** chose to constrain democracy substantially. The resulting populist backlash across the advanced democracies is the political-economic response.

**The path forward** is to either deepen democratic governance to match integration (the EU model, deepened) or accept thinner integration that preserves national democratic space. Either is consistent with the trilemma; the current hyperglobalisation-without-democratic-deepening configuration is not sustainable.

For developing countries: integration depth is a democratic choice. The integration commitments should be made transparently, with attention to the policy space being given up.

## How to Apply

1. **Take democratic preferences seriously** even when economically sub-optimal.
2. **Surface the policy space cost** of integration commitments.
3. **Design integration to preserve democratic space** where possible.
4. **Distinguish technocratic claims** from contested policy choices.

## Examples

**Situation:** A trade agreement provision constrains the country's right to require local content in public procurement.

**Application:** This is a democratic constraint with economic rationale. The trade-off should be made transparently — local content provisions support specific developmental goals; the integration provision constrains that flexibility. The choice should be deliberated democratically, not imposed technocratically.

**Result:** A more honest negotiation in which the policy-space trade-off is explicit.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Romanticise every democratic preference.
**Why:** Some democratic preferences produce bad outcomes. The argument is for democratic priority, not for democratic infallibility.

**Don't:** Treat technocratic constraints as neutral.
**Why:** They embed contested values choices. Be explicit.
