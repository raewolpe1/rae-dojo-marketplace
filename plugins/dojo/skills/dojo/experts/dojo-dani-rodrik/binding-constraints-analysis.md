# Binding Constraints Analysis

## When to Use
- When prioritising among many possible policy reforms.
- When identifying which specific constraint is most limiting growth or investment.
- When designing a sequenced reform programme.

## Fails When
- **The constraints are systemic and inter-locked** in ways that make single-constraint identification artificial.

## Core Concept

The binding-constraints framework (a complement to growth diagnostics) asks: among the many imperfections in this economy, which one, if relaxed, would produce the largest welfare gain? The other imperfections matter, but relaxing them while the binding constraint remains will not produce growth — the binding constraint binds.

Diagnostic indicators:

**Shadow price.** The binding constraint should have a high shadow price — economic agents would pay substantially to relax it. Look for high implicit prices on credit, land, infrastructure access, skilled labour, regulatory approval.

**Movement around the constraint.** Economic activity reorganises around binding constraints. Firms substitute, relocate, vertically integrate, or change product mix to avoid the constraint. Where you see such substitution, the constraint is binding.

**Returns to relaxation.** When pilot interventions relax a constraint, do they produce substantial response? Strong response indicates the constraint was binding.

**Cross-sector and cross-region variation.** Where the constraint is more present, is economic activity lower? This is suggestive but not definitive (other factors covary).

The binding constraint can shift over time. As one is relaxed, another may become binding. The framework is iterative — diagnose, relax, re-diagnose.

The political economy: the binding constraint may not be the politically easiest to address. The economically optimal reform may be politically infeasible; the politically attractive reform may not be economically optimal. Engage both.

## How to Apply

1. **List the candidate constraints** plausibly limiting growth.
2. **Look for shadow-price evidence, substitution, returns-to-relaxation, variation.**
3. **Identify the top 1–3 binding constraints.**
4. **Engage the political economy** of addressing them.
5. **Sequence reforms** to address binding constraints first.

## Examples

**Situation:** Identifying South Africa's binding growth constraints.

**Application:** Candidates: energy infrastructure; transport infrastructure; state capability; skill matching; labour market structure; financial market access; macroeconomic stability; political certainty. Shadow-price evidence (load-shedding costs, port congestion costs, premium for capable executives, skill premia, financial inclusion gaps) suggests the binding constraints are energy/logistics infrastructure, state capability, and skill-employment matching. These should be the priority; the others should follow.

**Result:** A reform prioritisation grounded in evidence.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Avoid the prioritisation.
**Why:** Reforming everything at once spreads political capital and implementation capacity too thinly to be effective.

**Don't:** Treat the diagnostic as one-time.
**Why:** Constraints shift. Re-diagnose periodically.
