# Economics Rules: Methodology

## When to Use
- When discussing what economics as a discipline knows and how it knows it.
- When defending the use of economic models against charges of irrelevance.
- When critiquing universalist applications of single models.

## Fails When
- **The audience wants a single answer** rather than a model-selection conversation.

## Core Concept

In *Economics Rules* (2015), Rodrik argues that economics is best understood as a collection of models, not a single model. Each model is a stylised representation of a specific causal mechanism. No model captures everything; each captures something useful about specific phenomena. The discipline's strength is the variety of models available; its weakness is the tendency to treat one model (typically the perfect-market benchmark or the standard macroeconomic model) as the universal description of reality.

The methodology has several elements:

**Models are about specific mechanisms.** A model of trade liberalisation captures the static efficiency gains; it does not capture the dynamic capability effects, the political-economic backlash, or the distributional consequences. Each is captured by a different model. Using only the static-efficiency model produces incomplete policy recommendations.

**Model selection is the central skill.** Given a question, which model best captures the relevant mechanism? The skill is in matching model to question — not in applying one model universally.

**Multiple models can be true simultaneously.** Different mechanisms operate in different contexts; the discipline of model selection is identifying which is most relevant. The same trade policy can have different effects in different countries because different models apply.

**Economic theory is a library of mechanisms.** The professional economist's contribution is the ability to draw appropriate mechanisms from the library for the specific question, not the ability to apply a single master mechanism to everything.

The implications: economic policy advice should be model-explicit (which mechanism are we assuming? what other mechanisms might be more relevant?), context-sensitive (which model fits this country and question?), and humble about disciplinary expertise (selecting the right model is judgment, not derivation).

The methodology defends economics against both populist dismissal ("economists know nothing") and orthodox universalism ("economics has settled this"). The discipline has substantial knowledge; that knowledge is held in many specific models that must be matched to specific questions.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify which mechanism is at stake** in the policy question.
2. **Select the model that captures that mechanism** — not the default standard model.
3. **Be explicit about model choice** and what other mechanisms might be relevant.
4. **Combine models** where multiple mechanisms operate.

## Examples

**Situation:** A debate about minimum wage in South Africa cites textbook arguments that wage floors reduce employment.

**Application:** The textbook model captures one mechanism (competitive labour markets, employers as wage-takers). The minimum-wage literature has documented many contexts in which this model is not the dominant mechanism — monopsony power, efficiency wages, demand-side effects of higher wages. Empirically, minimum wage effects vary substantially by context, magnitude, and labour market structure. The right policy analysis selects the appropriate model for South African conditions, not the default textbook one.

**Result:** A more careful analysis that engages with the actual mechanism in the actual labour market.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Apply one model universally.
**Why:** Different mechanisms operate in different contexts. The model-selection step is essential.

**Don't:** Use model multiplicity as an excuse for indecision.
**Why:** Models can be selected and conclusions can be reached. The discipline is in the selection, not in the refusal to select.
