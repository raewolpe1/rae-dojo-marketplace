# Industrial Policy (Rodrik Version)

## When to Use
- When defending industrial policy from within the mainstream-economic tradition.
- When designing industrial policy that is intellectually defensible against orthodox critique.
- When distinguishing genuine industrial policy from rent transfers dressed up as policy.

## Fails When
- **The design lacks performance conditions and discipline mechanisms.**
- **The political coalition for the industrial policy will not support its disciplinary requirements** (withdrawing support from failed bets, applying performance benchmarks).

## Core Concept

Rodrik's industrial policy defence rests on **market failures** that pervade developing-country economies — coordination failures, learning externalities, information asymmetries, demand spillovers. These market failures imply that private investment will be sub-optimal absent policy intervention. The policy intervention need not pick specific winners; it must address the specific market failures.

The framework has three propositions:

**Industrial policy is necessary.** Market failures exist; ignoring them produces sub-optimal investment. The textbook objection that government also fails does not settle the question — both market and government failure exist, and the design question is which is worse for the specific problem at hand.

**Industrial policy is tractable.** Well-designed industrial policy can capture the gains and limit the rent-seeking. The design features that make this possible are: embeddedness without capture (policymakers engage closely with firms to identify constraints, but maintain disciplinary distance); selectivity (sector-specific not generic); performance conditions (continued support requires verifiable performance); sunset clauses (support has a defined endpoint); and accountability mechanisms (public reporting, independent review).

**Industrial policy is empirically successful.** The East Asian cases (Japan, Korea, Taiwan), the postwar European cases (France, Germany, Italy), and many contemporary examples (US semiconductors, EU clean energy, China across the board) show that industrial policy can drive structural transformation. The empirical record does not support the orthodox dismissal.

The Rodrik version is more methodologically conservative than the Chang version — it grounds the case in market failures recognisable to mainstream economics rather than in the historical-institutional argument that mainstream economics has often rejected. Both arrive at similar policy conclusions through different routes.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the specific market failures** the policy addresses — coordination, learning, information, demand.
2. **Design embeddedness without capture** — close engagement with firms, but with disciplinary distance.
3. **Specify performance conditions** — measurable, time-bound, verifiable.
4. **Build the sunset clause** into the design from the start.
5. **Establish independent review** mechanisms.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's EV transition — should the state actively support battery and component manufacturing?

**Application:** The market failures are real: coordination problems across automotive, battery, energy infrastructure, and skill development; learning externalities in capability building; demand uncertainty in the transition period. Industrial policy can address these through coordinated R&D investment, local content requirements on public procurement, infrastructure investment, skill development tied to sector needs, and FDI partnerships with binding capability transfer commitments. Performance conditions: production volumes, local content thresholds, R&D investment, employment. Sunset: 15 years.

**Result:** A design that satisfies the market-failure rationale and the disciplinary requirements.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Defend industrial policy without specifying design.
**Why:** Bad industrial policy is real and damages the case for good industrial policy.

**Don't:** Pretend industrial policy is risk-free.
**Why:** It has risks (capture, white elephants); the design must address them honestly.
