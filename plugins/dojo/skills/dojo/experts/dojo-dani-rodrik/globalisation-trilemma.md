# The Globalisation Trilemma

## When to Use
- When deep economic integration is being recommended without explicit attention to its policy-space costs.
- When debating trade agreements, capital account openness, or financial integration.
- When responding to "globalisation versus protectionism" framings.

## Fails When
- **The audience is unfamiliar with the framework** — explain before using.
- **The question is about specific sectoral trade policy** rather than the broader integration debate.

## Core Concept

The globalisation trilemma states that we cannot simultaneously achieve all three of:

**Deep economic integration** — free flow of goods, services, capital, and (less commonly) labour across borders, with regulatory harmonisation that removes "behind-the-border" barriers.

**The nation-state** — substantial national policy autonomy on macroeconomic, regulatory, industrial, and social policy.

**Democratic politics** — policy responsive to the democratically expressed preferences of national citizens.

Any two are achievable; all three are not.

**Integration + nation-state, without democracy.** Markets discipline national policy; elected governments cannot meaningfully deviate from market-preferred policies. This is the "gold standard" pre-1914 globalisation, where domestic populations were politically excluded.

**Integration + democracy, without nation-state.** Decisions are made at a supranational level that can democratically override national preferences. The EU partially in this direction; a hypothetical global democracy fully so.

**Nation-state + democracy, without deep integration.** Countries retain policy autonomy and democratic responsiveness, accepting thinner integration. The Bretton Woods system 1945–1971 — open trade, capital controls, substantial domestic policy autonomy, broadly democratic.

The 1990s–2010s pushed for hyperglobalisation (integration + nation-state) and produced the political backlash that has reshaped advanced-economy politics. The honest path forward is one of two: deepen democratic governance to the level of integration (the EU model, taken further), or accept thinner integration that preserves national democratic space (the Bretton Woods model).

For developing countries: the trilemma frames the integration choice. Each layer of integration comes with policy-space cost. The choice should be deliberate and democratically grounded.

## How to Apply

1. **When integration is on the table, identify what policy space is being given up.**
2. **Ask whether the trade-off has been democratically deliberated** or imposed.
3. **Use the trilemma to make alternative options visible** — thinner integration preserves choices that hyperglobalisation forecloses.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa is considering deeper EU integration through expanded trade agreement provisions.

**Application:** The trilemma frame: the EU's deep-integration model comes with substantial constraints on industrial policy, IP regimes, public procurement, investor protections. South Africa retains the nation-state and democratic politics; integration is the variable. Choose the integration depth that preserves the industrial policy space the country wants to retain.

**Result:** A more deliberate integration choice rather than an automatic embrace.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat integration as a one-way ratchet.
**Why:** Integration depth is a policy choice; countries can choose less integration as well as more.

**Don't:** Frame the trilemma as anti-globalisation.
**Why:** It is pro-democracy and pro-policy-space within a globalised economy. Thin globalisation is still globalisation.
