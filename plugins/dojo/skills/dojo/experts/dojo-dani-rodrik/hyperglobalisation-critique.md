# Hyperglobalisation Critique

## When to Use
- When deeper global economic integration is being recommended without explicit attention to costs.
- When responding to claims that the 1990s-2010s integration model should be deepened.
- When evaluating proposed trade or investment agreements with substantial behind-the-border provisions.

## Fails When
- **The question is about a specific narrow integration agreement** rather than the broader integration model.

## Core Concept

Hyperglobalisation is Rodrik's term for the post-1990 push to integrate global markets to unprecedented depth — open trade, capital mobility, harmonised regulation, investor protection, and limits on national policy. The argument is that hyperglobalisation has gone too far, producing democratic-political backlash without delivering equivalent welfare gains, particularly in advanced economies.

The evidence:

**Gains from deeper integration have diminishing returns.** The big gains from trade come from moving from autarky to openness; the additional gains from harmonising regulation across already-open economies are smaller. The political and democratic costs of harmonisation can exceed the marginal economic gains.

**Distributional consequences have been substantial and underweighted.** The China shock and adjacent literatures document that import competition in advanced economies imposed concentrated losses on specific workers and communities, with limited compensation. The political consequences (Brexit, Trumpism, populism across Europe) are real costs of inadequate distributional management.

**Policy-space constraints have been substantial.** Trade and investment agreements have constrained industrial policy, public health regulation, environmental regulation, and democratic policy-making in ways that the economic gains did not justify.

**Financial liberalisation has produced instability.** The 1990s push for capital account openness produced repeated crises (Asia 1997, Russia 1998, Argentina 2001, global 2008) without delivering the promised efficiency gains.

The alternative is "thin globalisation" — open trade and capital flows with substantial national policy autonomy. The Bretton Woods era 1945–1971 demonstrated this configuration's viability. Returning to something like it is achievable and would address the political-economic strains the current model has produced.

## How to Apply

1. **Distinguish integration depth from integration breadth.** Open trade need not require deep regulatory harmonisation.
2. **Evaluate proposed integration measures** against their full set of consequences — economic, distributional, political, regulatory.
3. **Defend national policy space** where it is being eroded by integration commitments.
4. **Engage the distributional question** seriously in trade and investment policy.

## Examples

**Situation:** A proposed bilateral investment treaty includes investor-state dispute settlement (ISDS) provisions giving foreign investors the right to sue governments over regulatory changes.

**Application:** This is hyperglobalisation in concrete form. The marginal economic benefit (slightly increased FDI flows, modest) does not justify the substantial constraint on democratic regulatory authority. The treaty should be opposed or renegotiated without ISDS.

**Result:** A more selective integration that retains national regulatory autonomy.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Confuse hyperglobalisation critique with anti-globalisation.
**Why:** Thin globalisation is still substantial globalisation. The critique is about depth, not about openness.

**Don't:** Imagine returning to 1945–1971 is straightforward.
**Why:** The world is different. The principles can be applied; the specifics must be designed for current conditions.
