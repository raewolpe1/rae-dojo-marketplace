# Trade and Distribution

## When to Use
- When debating trade liberalisation, expansion, or agreements.
- When responding to claims that trade is positive-sum and adjustment will follow.

## Fails When
- **The question is narrowly technical** about specific tariff design rather than the distributional framing.

## Core Concept

Trade integration produces aggregate gains but distributes them asymmetrically. Workers and communities in import-competing sectors absorb concentrated, persistent losses; consumers and workers in exporting sectors absorb diffuse, individually small gains. The textbook claim that gains exceed losses is correct on aggregate; the claim that compensation will happen is largely false.

The empirical record is now substantial. The China shock literature documents large, persistent, geographically concentrated losses in US manufacturing communities from import competition with China after 2001. Similar patterns have been documented across European economies. The "adjustment will happen" prediction of the textbook model failed empirically — workers did not smoothly transition; communities did not recover.

For developing countries, the parallel question is the effect of trade liberalisation on specific labour groups and regions. Effects vary. Liberalisation that expands labour-intensive export sectors (garments, electronics) generates substantial employment, including for low-skill women workers. Liberalisation that exposes domestic manufacturing without offsetting export expansion produces deindustrialisation and concentrated job losses.

The policy implications:

**Distributional consequences must be addressed explicitly** rather than assumed away by reference to aggregate gains.

**Adjustment policy must be funded and credible** — worker retraining, regional support, social protection for displaced workers.

**Sequencing matters** — open the export-expanding sectors first; manage the import-exposure side carefully.

**The political economy of trade is real** — the populist backlash in advanced economies reflects real distributional costs that policymakers minimised.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the populations likely to absorb costs.**
2. **Design adjustment policy** in proportion to expected losses.
3. **Sequence to expand opportunity before contracting protection.**
4. **Build political legitimacy** through credible compensation.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's expanded AfCFTA engagement raises distributional questions across sectors.

**Application:** Identify exposed sectors (textiles, basic chemicals, certain food processing) and regions. Design adjustment: retraining, regional development, social protection extension. Sequence: expand export opportunities first; manage import exposure with timing and complementary support.

**Result:** A trade engagement with distributional management built in.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume aggregate gains imply political sustainability.
**Why:** Distribution determines political sustainability; gains alone do not.

**Don't:** Treat compensation as automatic.
**Why:** It must be designed, funded, and delivered specifically.
