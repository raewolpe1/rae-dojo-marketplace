# Growth Diagnostics

## When to Use
- When asked what policies a country should pursue to accelerate growth.
- When confronted with one-size-fits-all reform advice.
- When designing a country-specific economic strategy.

## Fails When
- **The diagnostic is treated as a ritual** producing a predetermined conclusion.
- **Political economy is ignored** — diagnosing constraints without engaging the political coalition for addressing them is incomplete.

## Core Concept

Growth diagnostics (Hausmann, Rodrik, Velasco, 2005) replaces the Washington Consensus checklist with a country-specific decision tree. The starting question: is the binding constraint on private investment the *return* to investment or the *cost* of financing it?

If returns are low, is it because of poor appropriability (institutional/policy failures) or low social returns (human capital, infrastructure, market failures)? If appropriability, is it government failure (macro instability, taxation, microeconomic distortions) or market failure (information externalities, coordination)?

If financing is the constraint, is it because of weak domestic savings, weak intermediation, or international capital constraints?

The diagnostic tree narrows the field. Different countries face different constraints; the recommended policies follow from the diagnosis, not from a universal checklist.

The methodological discipline matters more than the specific tree. Engage with the country, identify what is most binding, and prescribe to that — rather than recommending the same items everywhere.

## How to Apply

1. **Run the decision tree** explicitly for the country in question.
2. **Use multiple sources of evidence** — investment patterns, sector profitability, financial market depth, infrastructure quality, institutional measures.
3. **Identify the top 2–3 binding constraints**, not the full inventory of imperfections.
4. **Prioritise interventions** that address the binding constraints; lower-priority interventions wait.

## Examples

**Situation:** Diagnosing South Africa's growth constraints.

**Application:** Likely binding: energy and logistics infrastructure (Eskom, Transnet); state capability for industrial policy and SOE governance; skill mismatch and labour market dualism. Not binding: macroeconomic stability (largely present); financial sector depth (deep); rule of law in general (variable but not the top constraint).

**Result:** A reform programme focused on the binding constraints, not on the orthodox checklist.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Generate a long list of constraints without prioritising.
**Why:** Every economy has many imperfections. The diagnostic value is identifying which ones are binding.

**Don't:** Skip the political-economic dimension.
**Why:** A constraint that cannot be politically addressed remains binding; identify it but also engage with how to build the coalition for addressing it.
