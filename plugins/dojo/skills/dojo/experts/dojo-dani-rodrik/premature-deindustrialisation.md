# Premature Deindustrialisation

## When to Use
- When analysing manufacturing employment or output trends in middle-income countries.
- When debating whether services can substitute for manufacturing in catch-up.
- When designing reindustrialisation strategies.

## Fails When
- **The country has genuinely passed the manufacturing-led phase** (Switzerland, Singapore) and is climbing into knowledge-intensive services on top of a manufacturing base.
- **The framework is applied without sectoral specificity** — different manufacturing segments have very different dynamics.

## Core Concept

Premature deindustrialisation, documented by Rodrik in "Premature Deindustrialization" (2016), is the empirical finding that developing countries since the 1980s have been reaching peak manufacturing employment shares at lower income levels than the historical pattern of today's rich countries. Where British, American, German, and Japanese manufacturing employment peaked at incomes of around $14,000 (per capita, PPP), recent middle-income countries are peaking at incomes of $8,000–10,000 or below.

The consequence is severe. Manufacturing-led growth has been the central mechanism by which countries have caught up. Manufacturing absorbs labour, raises productivity, accumulates capabilities, generates tradeable surplus, and produces dense input-output linkages. Without a sustained manufacturing phase, countries struggle to find a substitute growth engine; the alternatives — primary commodities, tourism, business process outsourcing, financial services — do not produce the same broad-based capability accumulation.

The causes are debated. Globalisation, particularly the rise of China as a manufacturing centre, has compressed the manufacturing window for later developers. Technological change (automation, robotics) has reduced manufacturing's labour intensity. Trade liberalisation has exposed domestic manufacturers to competition before they could mature. Some interpretations attribute the pattern to specific policy choices (premature liberalisation under structural adjustment); others to structural changes in the global economy that no country can fully escape.

The implications for development strategy: defending and rebuilding manufacturing capability becomes the central structural challenge. The orthodox response (services-led growth, FDI-attraction, knowledge economy) is largely inadequate. The needed response combines: selective trade protection and industrial policy in targeted sectors; investment in productive capabilities (skills, R&D, supplier networks); strategic use of regional integration (AfCFTA, MERCOSUR) as protected scale-up markets; and political-economic commitment to a multi-decade industrial project.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose whether premature deindustrialisation is present** — manufacturing share trajectory against income level.
2. **Treat it as the central structural problem** if so.
3. **Resist services-substitution arguments** — they do not produce equivalent capability accumulation.
4. **Design reindustrialisation strategies** rather than expecting manufacturing recovery without intervention.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's manufacturing share of GDP has fallen from around 24% (early 1990s) to around 12%, at income levels well below the historical manufacturing-peak pattern.

**Application:** This is severe premature deindustrialisation. The strategic priority is reindustrialisation across viable segments (automotive moving up the value chain, agro-processing for African markets, chemicals, renewable energy equipment, mining beneficiation). Tools: selective protection, AfCFTA strategy, industrial policy, infrastructure, skills. Time horizon: 15–20 years.

**Result:** Reindustrialisation as the strategic spine, not as nostalgia.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Accept "we missed the manufacturing window" fatalism.
**Why:** The window is narrower but not closed. Specific sectors with capability bases and demand trajectories are accessible.

**Don't:** Treat services as a substitute.
**Why:** Services do not produce the same capability accumulation. They complement manufacturing; they do not substitute for it.
