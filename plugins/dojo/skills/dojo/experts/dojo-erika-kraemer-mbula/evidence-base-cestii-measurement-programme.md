## THE EVIDENCE

### The argument, stated better than this dojo stated it
- **Finding:** **"It is often said that, 'what gets measured, gets done'. What is not considered often enough is that the inverse to this statement is also true, 'what does not get measured, does not get done'."**
- And the mechanism, precisely: **"Policies tend to speak and respond to those phenomena that have been subjected to measurement programmes."** Therefore: **"if the science and technology (S&T) and innovation measurement programmes installed in African countries follow those designed for developed nations, then the industrial policies in these countries will, modus ponens, tend to follow those of developed nations too, and vice versa."**
- **Caveat:** This is Kraemer-Mbula's decontextualisation critique with the causal chain filled in — **measurement → what policy can see → what policy does.** It is the sharpest available statement of why an indicator set is not a technical annexe. Use it.

### Why the standard surveys structurally cannot see it
- **Finding:** **"All national business innovation surveys implemented in Africa and elsewhere are restricted to the formal sector, targeting businesses with at least 10 employees (AIO 2010, 2014)."** The traditional measurement programmes "are not suited to developing economies on the Continent, where the majority of countries have economies that are largely informal, combined with under-developed measurement structures."
- **Caveat:** **The ten-employee threshold is the whole thing.** It is not that informal innovation is hard to detect — the instrument is *defined* to exclude the population. Your Groundbreakers segment is below the threshold of every innovation survey ever run in this country.

### The scale of what is excluded
- **Finding** (all cited by the paper to the named sources, not original to it):
  - **Over 75% of economic activities in sub-Saharan Africa take place in the informal sector** (Charmes 2016).
  - **More than half of non-agricultural jobs in sub-Saharan Africa are in the informal sector** (ILO 2011).
  - **The informal sector creates more than 90% of new jobs in low-income countries on the continent** (Benjamin, Golub & Mbaye 2015).
  - The informal economy provides on average **18% of value added in more developed economies and 41% in developing countries** (Schneider 2002).
  - **Every sixth non-agricultural worker in the world is an own-account worker or owner of an informal sector enterprise** (Williams 2014).
  - Beyond the economics, the informal sector **"contributes to the transfer of skills and experience, and growth in capabilities of marginalized people"** (Maloney 2004).
- **Caveat:** These are secondary citations at continental or global scale. **Do not apply them to the Western Cape.** They establish that the excluded population is the majority of African economic activity — which is the point — not what it is here.

### The South African indictment
- **Finding:** **"In African countries where industrial policy is more well-established, such as South Africa, SMME policy and interventions are expected to benefit and encourage the formalization of informal sector enterprises. This approach has had limited impact because of the continued bias toward larger formal sector enterprises that leaves out very small firms and the informal sector. For example, Rogerson (2004) makes a disparaging assessment of the impact of the first ten years of national SMME programmes in South Africa."**
- And: the prominence of informal economic activity **"is often described as a 'deficiency' (Marti and Ssenkubuge 2009) and thus a hindrance to industrialization. A central aim of industrial policy has thus been to reduce the size of the informal sector."** Against which: **"What has not received sufficient attention in the literature or policy is the potential for the informal sector to promote the kinds of competence building and structural transformation recognized as crucial for inclusive industrialization."**
- **Caveat:** Rogerson (2004), *Development Southern Africa* 21(5): 765–784, assesses **1994–2003**. It is twenty years old and its verdict on national SMME programmes is not automatically today's. **But it is the closest thing to a formal evaluation of the lineage your strategy sits in, and no one has cited it to you.** Get it.
- Also: **Kraemer-Mbula and Konté (2016), analysing STI policy across African countries, "show that there is minimal support for informal sector actors in this space."** **Petersen and Kruss (2019) reach the same conclusion reviewing South African innovation and inclusive development policy.**

### The instrument — four stages
- **Finding:** a **novel mixed methods methodology**, four inter-related stages: two quantitative, then two qualitative.

**Stage 1 — Area-based listing.** "Considering that business registers for the informal sector generally do not exist, the first stage is to develop such a register for the selected local study area. **An area-based method such as the small area census method (Charman et al. 2017) is most suitable.**" The authors **experimented with an adaptation of the PLACE method** (Priorities Local AIDS Control Efforts) — "an engaged, participatory approach for identifying and mapping phenomenon or actors in a local community... designed to map hotspots of activities." Two steps: **listing**, then **verification**. Purpose: "to develop an understanding of local perceptions of informality, and to generate a spatial representation of informal businesses." **Key outcome: a list of businesses with a geo-coded position and location, and an indication of economic activity.**

**Stage 2 — Innovation survey.** Face-to-face interviews with business owners. A structured instrument including close-ended questions, adapting standard business innovation surveys to the **Oslo Manual 4th edition** definition. Crucially: **"It is therefore possible to employ data collectors with relatively little expertise in innovation research, but with much knowledge of the local area, to administer the instrument."** Census or stratified sample depending on stage-1 counts and spatial heterogeneity.

**Stage 3 — Digital storytelling.** Workshops over **five days**, iterative, centred on a story participants share from their life experience. **The key research question — and this is the actual, validated, South African instrument:**

> **"Tell us a true story about a time when you decided to do something different in the way that you run your business and what happened."**

"The freedom of reflection provided by the digital storytelling workshop environment allows for the identification of the nature of changing business events, and how innovation takes place in informal sector businesses." The purpose is co-creation: **"allowing researchers and participants to co-create knowledge and bring informal sector businesses into the measurement programme."**

**Stage 4 — Semi-structured interviews** with informal businesses and other key actors in the local area, "to enable the development of a systemic picture."

- **Output:** not a framework but **"deep, broad and rich 'information packages'"** — a **database of local innovation and production systems**, "Instead of the rather prosaic 'framework-centered' approach that is almost ubiquitously adopted by decision makers."
- **Caveat:** **This dojo previously carried an invented survey question of my own construction. It has been removed.** The question above is the real one, and it is better — note that it never uses the word "innovation," asks for a story rather than a category, and locates the change in the respondent's own decision.

### The definitional moves — both unusually useful
- **Innovation:** they keep the core definition from formal surveys, taking the **Oslo Manual 4th revision (2018)**: **"a new or improved product or process (or combination thereof) that differs significantly from the unit's previous products or processes and that has been made available to potential users (product) or brought into use by the unit (process)."** **"The revised definition makes possible the measurement of innovation beyond formal sector businesses, and encompasses innovation in the informal sector, public sector and household sectors (see Gault 2018)."**
- **Informality — bottom-up:** **"In contrast to the prevailing definitions of researchers and measurement professionals, the informal sector here is defined in a bottom-up manner based on how the business owners and others in the local area describe the business (i.e. informal or formal)."** So a business **may be registered and keep formal accounts** and still be in scope if it is "similar in character to unincorporated enterprises in the informal sector in that they **operate from shipping containers or makeshift premises with limited or no access to basic amenities, and mainly serve the low-income market in the local area**." **"Consequently, there is no restriction on the size of the businesses selected."**
- **Caveat:** The bottom-up definition is a deliberate break from the SNA/ILO convention (unincorporated, unregistered, no formal accounts) and from SESE's operational rule (not VAT-registered). **It will not reconcile with your QLFS or SARS STP baselines and it is not meant to.** That is a third incompatible unit, and if it enters the document it needs the same footnote treatment as persons-vs-sites.
- **Note the Gault citation.** Fred Gault, whom this collection flagged as a Tier 3 candidate, is the author of the Oslo-4 argument that makes informal measurement possible at all. He is upstream of this instrument.

### The South African data landscape — SESE, and why it is not enough
- **Finding:** **"In South Africa, nationally representative data on informal sector businesses can be obtained through the Survey of Employers and the Self-Employed (SESE). The 2017 SESE (Stats SA 2019) refers to the informal sector as businesses that were not registered for Value Added Tax, but possibly registered for Income Tax. The SESE is modelled on the 1–2–3 survey methodology whereby informal business (self-employed persons) are identified in the first phase through the Quarterly Labour Force Survey, and the second phase, the SESE, is a survey of those informal businesses identified."**
- The **1–2–3 approach** is "arguably the best vehicle for creating nationally representative data on the informal sector" and **"the gold standard (ILO 2013)"** — enterprise-based, three phases: LFS (household) → self-employed follow-up → income and expenditure (demand side).
- **Charmes, Gault and Wunsch-Vincent (2016) recommend two "viable scenarios"**: add a few innovation questions to existing large-scale informal sector surveys, **or** conduct ad hoc sector-based studies. **"We contend that these recommendations are insufficient"** — because a local, all-sector, geographically contained approach is a third route, and because **"the nature of the informal sector displays such richness that it cannot possibly be sufficient merely to add a few questions on innovation to a SESE... It is necessary for us to get a much better understanding of how innovation takes place in small and micro enterprises first, before collecting such data on a large scale."**
- **Caveat — DIRECTLY LIVE FOR THIS PROJECT.** **Your QLFS baseline of 387,367 Western Cape enterprise operators is phase one of exactly this architecture.** SESE is phase two and it exists. Nobody has attached an innovation module to it, and this paper argues that attaching one would not be sufficient anyway. **The route the authors recommend is local, all-sector, and geographically bounded — which is a provincial-scale instrument, which is the scale you are working at.**

### THE CHARMAN CONNECTION — read this
- **Finding:** the methodology is built on **Charman, A. J. E., Petersen, L. M., Piper, L. E., Liederman, R. & Legg, T. (2017), "Small Area Census Approach to Measure the Township Informal Economy in South Africa", *Journal of Mixed Methods Research* 11(1): 36–58.** The paper cites it as the inspiration for its area-based stage and repeatedly for the spatial dynamics of the informal sector.
- And: **Charman, A. & Petersen, L. (2018), "Informal Micro-Enterprises in a Township Context: A Spatial Analysis of Business Dynamics in Five Cape Town Localities", in F. Fourie (ed.), *The South African Informal Sector: Creating Jobs, Reducing Poverty*, HSRC Press, 253–284.**
- **Caveat — this is the finding of this whole exercise.** The instrument's foundational method **has already been applied in five Cape Town townships**, and **Andrew Charman is a sub-consultant on your own project.** The thing this dojo has been telling you to commission has, in its essential form, been built and run here by someone on the team. **Ask him.** That conversation is free and it is the highest-value hour available on this workstream.

### The LIPS approach — and the honest cost
- **Finding:** the framework adapts **Local Innovation and Production Systems (LIPS)**, from the Brazilian RedeSist tradition (Cassiolato, Lastres and colleagues) — **"an adaptation of a programme that was successful in Brazil during the Workers' Party administration."** LIPS emphasises: **territorial dimension** as the specific focus of analysis and policy; **link between micro-, meso- and macro-dimensions**; **diversity of actors** — economic, political and social; **interactive learning**; **coordination ('governance')** — power relations among actors; and **embeddedness** — "common identities and goals... and the articulation and adherence of production and innovation initiatives to the development of that particular territory."
  - Definition: a LIPS is **"(a) group(s) of economic, political and social agents localised in the same area, performing related economic activities, in which formal and informal interdependence and consistent linkages usually result in cooperation and learning processes, with a potential to generate the increase of productive and innovative capabilities"** (Lastres & Cassiolato 2005, 7).
  - **"The LIPS approach places the unit of analysis in the set of agents, at the collective level, going beyond the individual organizations, sectors or productive chains, establishing a close relationship between the territory and the economic activities."**
- **The framework's own diagram** places the informal production unit among: **formal financing** (commercial, development, microfinance banks) and **informal financing** (**stokvels**, loans from friends/families, VC); **formal training** (universities, TVET colleges) and **informal training** (**apprenticeships, mentoring**); **formal intermediaries** (NGOs, trade associations, incubators) and **informal** (IK practitioners, religious societies, volunteer groups) — and specifies the unit's **"Modes of innovation: Learning by DUI/STI; Product/Process innovation."**
- **Caveat:** **The framework explicitly runs on Lundvall's DUI/STI distinction.** That is not this dojo drawing an analogy — it is in their diagram. The Lundvall bridge to a South African informal-sector instrument is real and it is drawn by CeSTII.
- **The cost, stated honestly by the authors:** **"Extending innovation surveys to the informal sector, using the approach proposed, will come at an extra financial cost, but the multiplier effect of harnessing the contribution of the informal sector through inclusive industrial policy informed by measurement cannot be under-estimated."** **This is a proposal, not a completed programme.** The authors are proposing to build it. Do not cite it as a body of results.

### What it is for — the industrial policy payoff
- **Finding:** **"The focus on the local level is an unconventional approach to industrial policy but addresses the need for more spatially targeted interventions, which industrialization scholars such as Tregenna (2015, 2016) have been calling for."** The output — a database of geo-coded LIPS — **"provides a way to identify key economic activities where productivity is high, or where businesses are highly innovative, or where intervention is needed to strengthen linkages."** Also: **"The demand-driven nature of the informal sector also holds promise for locally-derived novel solutions that could be scaled, and for building niche areas that better address local demand."** And the alignment argument: **better horizontal alignment of industrial with innovation policy, and "the anchoring of these policies in local economic development plans."**
- **Caveat:** The claim that the data would drive spatially targeted industrial policy is a hope, not a result. Brazil is offered as precedent. **But note the shape: this is an argument for anchoring innovation policy in local economic development plans — which is what a provincial strategy is.**

## How to Apply

1. **Use their question, not an invented one.** *"Tell us a true story about a time when you decided to do something different in the way that you run your business and what happened."*
2. **Talk to Andrew Charman.** The small area census is his; it has run in five Cape Town localities; he is on your team.
3. **Note that QLFS → SESE is already phase 1 → phase 2** of the architecture. The gap is an innovation module, and the authors say a module alone is insufficient.
4. **Cite the ten-employee threshold** when explaining why no existing survey sees your Groundbreakers.
5. **Take the bottom-up definition seriously and footnote it.** It will not reconcile with QLFS or SARS STP. Third unit, third footnote.
6. **Get Rogerson (2004).** A disparaging assessment of ten years of national SMME programmes is not something to discover after publication.
7. **Say it is a proposal.** The authors say so. It is not a completed measurement programme.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy needs to establish whether township enterprises innovate, and has no data.

**Application:** The instrument exists and is specified in this paper: area-based listing via the small area census, an innovation survey using the Oslo-4 definition administered by locally-knowledgeable fieldworkers, digital storytelling workshops, and key-actor interviews. It is peer-reviewed, South African, DSI-funded, and designed by CeSTII — whose advisory committee Kraemer-Mbula sits on. **And the foundational stage has already been run in five Cape Town townships by Charman and Petersen (2018), and Charman is a sub-consultant on this project.** The sequence is therefore: read the paper; read Charman & Petersen (2018); ask Charman what it cost and what it found; then decide whether a Western Cape LIPS baseline is affordable. **You are one conversation from knowing.**

**Result:** An instrument, a precedent in the right city, and a person on the team who has run it.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Attribute this paper to Kraemer-Mbula.
**Why:** Mustapha, Petersen, Jegede, Bortagaray and Kruss wrote it. She is cited in it, advises the institution that produced it, and shares a co-author with it. That is the honest relationship.

**Don't:** Cite it as a body of findings about informal innovation.
**Why:** It is a **proposal for a measurement programme**. The authors are explicit that it would cost money and has not been run at scale.

**Don't:** Reconcile the bottom-up informality definition with QLFS or SARS STP.
**Why:** It is deliberately a third unit — perception-based, size-unrestricted, and inclusive of registered firms in makeshift premises. Footnote it like the others.
