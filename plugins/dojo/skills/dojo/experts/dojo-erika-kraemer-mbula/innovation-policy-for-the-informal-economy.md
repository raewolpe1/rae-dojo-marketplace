# Innovation Policy for the Informal Economy

## When to Use
- Designing innovation instruments for informal or micro enterprises.
- When formalisation is proposed as the precondition for innovation support.
- When the innovation pillar has no instrument that touches the informal segment.

## Fails When
- **The target population is formal.** Standard instruments apply.

## Core Concept

**The argument, made with Konte:** innovation policy needs **a new framework** where the informal economy is concerned — **not an extension clause on the existing one.**

That distinction is the whole file. An extension clause says: our innovation policy exists, and we will also make it available to informal firms. But the existing framework's **unit of analysis excludes them**. Making an instrument "available" to a firm the instrument cannot act on is not inclusion; it is paperwork.

**Why every standard instrument fails here:**

| Instrument | Assumes | Informal firm has |
|---|---|---|
| R&D support | An R&D function | None, ever |
| IP support | Something protectable, and a filing budget | Neither |
| Technology transfer | Absorptive capacity, a receiving structure | Neither |
| Innovation vouchers | Ability to specify a research question, and often registration | Neither |
| Science parks | Rent, and a reason to be there | Neither |
| University collaboration | A counterparty the university recognises | Not recognised |

**"Formalise them first" is not a policy — it is the absence of one.** It defers the innovation question behind a formalisation question that (per McKenzie's evidence) mostly does not resolve: registration can be induced cheaply, tax registration cannot, and the promised benefits largely do not follow. Meanwhile the innovation is happening now, informally, unsupported.

**What a new framework would have to start from — the positive programme:**

- **Build on what we have.** Blending frontier technological advances **with local knowledge**. Not replacing local knowledge with the frontier; blending.
- **Mobilise the transformative power of the grassroots** — treat the informal manufacturer and the township workshop as **participants** in the innovation system, not as its eventual beneficiaries.
- **Put innovation at the centre of a developmental agenda that guides systemic change in the desired direction** — which requires naming the direction.

**What that implies instrumentally.** The learning channels for informal firms are **commercial relationships** — customers, suppliers, other firms — not institutions. So the instruments that could work are the ones that strengthen those channels: demanding buyers, supplier relationships with feedback, occasions where firms meet firms, and cheap bridges to a small amount of codified knowledge (a technical adviser, one lab test, an extension officer). This converges exactly with Lundvall's `innovation-policy-without-laboratories.md`, arrived at from the informality side.

**The honest position: this framework does not yet exist.** Not in South Africa, not anywhere. She has argued for it and worked on its measurement foundations. **A strategy that claims to have innovation instruments for its informal segment is claiming something nobody has built.** The defensible move is to say so, design candidates, and evaluate them.

## How to Apply

1. **Test each instrument against the informal firm.** Could it act? Most cannot.
2. **Refuse "formalise first."** It is deferral, and the evidence says the deferral does not resolve.
3. **Follow the learning channels.** They are commercial relationships, not institutions. Build there.
4. **Blend, don't replace.** Local knowledge is the base; the frontier is an input to it.
5. **Give the grassroots standing**, not beneficiary status.
6. **Be honest that the framework is unbuilt.** Design candidates and evaluate them rather than claiming coverage.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy's innovation pillar states that its instruments are "available to all enterprises including informal enterprises."

**Application:** Test the claim instrument by instrument. R&D support — an informal firm has no R&D function. IP support — nothing protectable, no filing budget. Technology transfer — no absorptive structure. Vouchers — usually require registration, and always require the ability to specify a research question. The claim of availability is formally true and operationally empty. **The pillar has no instrument for the segment**, and saying so is more useful than the sentence currently there. Then design candidates from the learning channels that do exist: a demanding buyer via provincial procurement, an extension-style technical adviser, and an occasion where informal manufacturers meet each other. Evaluate them, because nobody knows whether they work.

**Result:** An honest statement of coverage, and three candidate instruments to test.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Claim availability as inclusion.
**Why:** An instrument that cannot act on the firm is not available to it in any sense that matters.

**Don't:** Make formalisation the precondition.
**Why:** It defers the question behind one that mostly does not resolve — and the innovation is happening now.

**Don't:** Claim the informal innovation framework exists.
**Why:** It does not, anywhere. Design candidates and test them.
