# Democracy and Famines

## When to Use

- When someone argues that developing countries should prioritise economic growth over democratic freedoms.
- When evaluating the role of political institutions in preventing extreme deprivation.
- When the "Lee thesis" (authoritarian governance as prerequisite for development) is being invoked.
- When assessing whether a country's institutional arrangements provide adequate protective security.

## Fails When

- **Democracy alone is insufficient.** Democratic institutions prevent famines but do not automatically prevent chronic undernutrition, inequality, or slow-onset deprivation. The thesis is specifically about acute catastrophic failure.
- **The democracy is not functioning.** The protective effect requires a genuinely functioning democracy with a free press, not merely elections.

## Core Concept

No substantial famine has ever occurred in any independent country with a functioning democratic government and a relatively free press. This empirical regularity is one of the most robust findings in development studies, and its explanation illuminates the instrumental value of democratic freedoms.

The mechanism operates through political incentives. In a democracy, governments that fail to prevent famines face electoral consequences — they can be voted out. A free press makes it impossible to conceal food crises. Opposition parties have electoral incentives to publicise government failures. These political incentives create powerful motivation for governments to act quickly when food crises emerge.

In contrast, authoritarian governments face no such incentive. China's Great Leap Forward famine of 1958–1961 killed an estimated 30 million people — the largest famine in history — partly because the absence of a free press and political opposition allowed the government to persist with disastrous policies uncorrected for years. The Soviet Union, Ethiopia under Mengistu, North Korea — all experienced devastating famines under authoritarian rule.

India provides an instructive comparison with China. India has been less successful than China in achieving economic growth and reducing chronic undernutrition. But India, with its functioning democracy and free press, has not experienced a famine since independence in 1947 — despite lower per capita income than China for most of the period. The protective function of democracy operates specifically against catastrophic failure, even when it does not prevent chronic deprivation.

This thesis illustrates the broader point about democracy's instrumental value. Political freedoms are not merely intrinsically valuable; they create practical mechanisms — information flow, political accountability, opposition scrutiny — that protect populations from the most extreme forms of deprivation.

## How to Apply

1. **Assess the information function.** Does the society have a free press capable of investigating and publicising food crises? Information suppression is a precondition for famine in all but the most extreme supply-failure cases.
2. **Assess the accountability function.** Do political leaders face consequences for failing to prevent mass deprivation? Electoral accountability is the primary mechanism.
3. **Distinguish acute from chronic failure.** Democracy prevents catastrophic acute failure (famine) but does not automatically prevent chronic deprivation (persistent undernutrition, poor health outcomes). Both require attention.
4. **Do not wait for development before democratising.** The protective function of democracy operates at all income levels.

## Examples

**Situation:** An African Union policy discussion considers whether democratic governance or economic growth should be prioritised in development strategy.
**Application:** The democracy-famine thesis shows this is a false choice. Democratic governance and economic growth are not alternatives — they reinforce each other. Democratic freedoms provide protective security (preventing catastrophic failure), constructive value (public reasoning about priorities), and instrumental value (information flow, accountability). The empirical record shows that authoritarian growth models are fragile — they can produce impressive statistics while concealing massive suffering.
**Result:** Development strategy should treat democratic governance and economic growth as complementary, not sequential. The South African experience — democratic governance combined with social protection (grants, free healthcare) preventing acute deprivation even during economic stagnation — illustrates the protective function.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Overextend the thesis to claim democracy prevents all deprivation.
**Why:** The thesis is specifically about acute catastrophic failure (famine). Chronic undernutrition, inequality, and slow-onset deprivation persist in many democracies, including India. Democracy is necessary but not sufficient for addressing all forms of unfreedom.

**Don't:** Attribute the absence of famine in democracies solely to wealth.
**Why:** The mechanism is political, not economic. Poor democracies (India, Botswana) have avoided famine while wealthier authoritarian regimes (China, the Soviet Union) experienced devastating ones. The free press and electoral accountability, not income level, are the operative factors.
