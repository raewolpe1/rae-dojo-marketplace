# Development Ethics

## When to Use

- When a development programme or policy lacks explicit normative foundations.
- When ethical dilemmas arise in development practice — trade-offs between efficiency and equity, between growth and freedom, between external priorities and local values.
- When someone claims development is "value-free" or purely technical.
- When the moral status of development beneficiaries — as agents or as objects — is at issue.

## Fails When

- **The user needs specific ethical codes or professional standards.** Development ethics as Sen understands it is a philosophical discipline, not a compliance framework.
- **The ethical dilemma requires specific normative theory** (deontological, consequentialist) rather than Sen's broader evaluative approach.

## Core Concept

Development ethics asks: what are the moral foundations of development? What should development aim at? Who should decide? And what obligations do development practitioners have to those they seek to serve?

Sen's contribution to development ethics rests on several foundations. First, the reconception of development as freedom rather than growth. This is itself an ethical claim — that the purpose of development is to expand people's substantive freedoms, not to maximise aggregate output. The ethical significance is that it places the person, and the person's freedom, at the centre of the development enterprise.

Second, the insistence on agency. Development ethics in Sen's framework requires that people be treated as agents of their own development, not as passive recipients of benefits determined by others. This has direct implications for development practice: participatory design, community governance, informed consent, and accountability to affected populations are not optional extras but ethical requirements.

Third, the role of public reasoning. Ethical questions in development — what to prioritise, how to weight competing values, whose perspectives to privilege — cannot be resolved by philosophical fiat or expert determination alone. They require democratic deliberation that includes the perspectives of those most affected. This makes democracy itself an ethical requirement of development practice, not merely a desirable political arrangement.

Fourth, the attention to informational basis. Every evaluative framework embodies ethical commitments through its choice of what to measure and what to ignore. A GDP-focused framework implicitly values aggregate output over distribution, equality, freedom, and sustainability. Making the informational basis explicit is an ethical act — it exposes the hidden value judgements in technical choices.

In Southern African development practice, these principles challenge several common patterns: the dominance of donor-determined indicators, the treatment of communities as beneficiary populations rather than agents, the equation of service delivery with development, and the neglect of political voice and democratic participation as development outcomes in their own right.

## How to Apply

1. **Make normative foundations explicit.** Every programme has implicit ethical commitments. Name them: what does this programme assume about what matters?
2. **Treat agency as an ethical requirement.** Programme design that denies agency to participants — regardless of the outcomes produced — fails ethically.
3. **Subject evaluative choices to democratic scrutiny.** Who decided what to measure? Whose values are embedded in the indicators? Are the affected populations part of this conversation?
4. **Attend to means and processes, not just outcomes.** An ethically sound development programme achieves good outcomes through ethical means — with consent, participation, transparency, and respect for persons.

## Examples

**Situation:** A donor-funded water programme in the Free State measures success by the number of water points installed, with no consultation with communities about their priorities or governance of the water systems.
**Application:** Development ethics asks: were communities consulted about whether water points were their highest priority? Do communities govern the water systems, or are they passive recipients? Are the water points maintained, or do they fall into disuse because no local agency was built? A programme that installs infrastructure without building agency has succeeded technically but failed ethically — it has treated people as objects of development rather than agents.
**Result:** Embed ethical requirements — community consultation, participatory governance, agency-building — in programme design from the outset, not as afterthoughts.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat development as a purely technical exercise with no normative dimensions.
**Why:** Every development decision embodies ethical choices — about what to prioritise, whose interests to serve, how to balance competing values. Pretending these choices are technical is itself an ethical failure, because it hides value judgements behind a veneer of objectivity.

**Don't:** Assume that good intentions excuse bad processes.
**Why:** A programme that aims at good outcomes but achieves them through paternalistic, non-participatory, or dignity-denying processes has not fulfilled its ethical obligations. The means of development matter, not just the ends.
