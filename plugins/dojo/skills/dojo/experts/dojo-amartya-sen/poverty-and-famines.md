# Poverty and Famines

## When to Use

- When analysing any food crisis, famine, or acute hunger event — the entitlement approach is the foundational analytical framework.
- When challenging the assumption that famine equals food shortage.
- When designing food security programmes — to ensure they address entitlement failures, not just food supply.
- When evaluating social protection systems for their capacity to prevent entitlement collapse.

## Fails When

- **Total food system collapse.** In siege conditions, complete drought with no trade, or total infrastructure destruction, food availability may be the binding constraint. Even here, distributional entitlement failures determine who starves first.
- **Chronic undernutrition.** The entitlement approach was developed primarily for acute famine. Chronic undernutrition involves different mechanisms, though entitlement analysis is still relevant.

## Core Concept

The entitlement approach to poverty and famines establishes that starvation is a function of entitlement failure, not of food availability decline. A person starves when their entitlement set — the set of alternative commodity bundles they can command through trade, production, own-labour, or transfer — fails to include any bundle with adequate food.

Entitlements derive from four sources: trade-based entitlements (what you can buy with what you own), production-based entitlements (what you can grow or produce), own-labour entitlements (what you can earn by selling your labour), and transfer entitlements (what you receive from the state, community, or family). Famine occurs when one or more of these channels collapses for a significant portion of the population.

The Bengal famine of 1943 killed approximately three million people. Food availability per capita in Bengal was not significantly lower than in previous non-famine years. The famine was caused by a combination of wartime inflation that destroyed the purchasing power of rural labourers, speculative hoarding, and administrative failures — all entitlement failures, not supply failures. People starved in the presence of food because they could not command it.

This has profound implications. If famines are caused by aggregate food shortage, the policy response is to increase food supply. If famines are caused by entitlement failures, the policy response is to protect entitlements — through employment programmes, social transfers, price stabilisation, and democratic accountability. These are very different interventions.

The entitlement approach also illuminates chronic poverty. Poor people are poor not because there are insufficient resources in the economy but because their entitlement mappings — the rates at which they can convert their endowments into commodities — deliver an inadequate bundle. A landless labourer in Limpopo is poor because the wage rate, employment availability, and prices of essential goods combine to produce an entitlement set that falls below what is needed for basic functionings.

## How to Apply

1. **Map entitlement channels.** For the affected population, identify the primary entitlement sources: wages, own production, trade, and transfers. Which channels are failing, and why?
2. **Track entitlement exchange rates.** A food crisis may result from food price inflation (trade entitlement failure), unemployment (own-labour entitlement failure), crop failure (production entitlement failure), or withdrawal of social transfers (transfer entitlement failure).
3. **Disaggregate by occupation and class.** Different groups face different entitlement vulnerabilities. Pastoralists, landless labourers, smallholder farmers, and urban informal workers have different entitlement structures and different failure modes.
4. **Design interventions around entitlement protection.** Employment guarantee schemes protect own-labour entitlements. Cash transfers protect trade entitlements. Crop insurance protects production entitlements. Each addresses a different failure mode.

## Examples

**Situation:** A drought in the Eastern Cape has reduced maize production by 30%. Media reports frame this as a food crisis caused by drought.
**Application:** The entitlement approach asks: who has lost entitlements, and through which channels? Smallholder farmers who depend on own production have suffered a production entitlement failure. But farm labourers who have lost employment have suffered an own-labour entitlement failure that is distinct from the production loss. Urban consumers face higher food prices — a trade entitlement failure. The drought is the trigger, but the pattern of deprivation is determined by the entitlement structure, not by the aggregate production decline.
**Result:** Interventions should be differentiated: input support and insurance for smallholders (production entitlements), employment programmes for labourers (own-labour entitlements), food price stabilisation or targeted transfers for urban consumers (trade entitlements).

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Start famine analysis with aggregate food production data.
**Why:** Food availability per capita is neither necessary nor sufficient for famine. The Bengal famine, the Ethiopian famine of 1973, and the Bangladesh famine of 1974 all occurred without significant food availability decline. Start with entitlement mapping.

**Don't:** Treat food aid as the default famine response.
**Why:** If the famine is caused by entitlement failure rather than food shortage, food aid may be less effective than entitlement-protecting interventions — employment programmes, cash transfers, price stabilisation — that address the underlying cause.
