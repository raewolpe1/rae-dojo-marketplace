# Capability vs Resources

## When to Use

- When a programme or policy equates resource delivery with well-being improvement.
- When evaluating whether equal resource allocation constitutes equitable treatment.
- When the Rawls-Sen debate is directly relevant to the evaluative question.
- When conversion factors — personal, social, environmental — mediate the translation of resources into capabilities.

## Fails When

- **Resource data is genuinely the best available proxy.** In data-constrained environments, resource-based measures may be all that is feasible. The framework still applies conceptually even when capability measurement is not possible.
- **The question is specifically about Rawlsian institutional design** rather than individual-level evaluation.

## Core Concept

Rawls's theory of justice uses primary goods — rights, liberties, opportunities, income, wealth, and the social bases of self-respect — as the metric for assessing advantage. The difference principle holds that inequalities in the distribution of primary goods are just only if they benefit the least advantaged.

Sen's critique centres on the conversion problem: primary goods are the means to freedom, not freedom itself. The same bundle of primary goods yields very different levels of capability for different people, because conversion depends on personal characteristics (disability, illness, age, cognitive capacity), social conditions (gender norms, discrimination, class hierarchies), and environmental factors (climate, infrastructure, epidemiological environment).

A disabled person requires more resources than an able-bodied person to achieve the same level of mobility. A woman in a patriarchal society requires more resources — and different kinds of support — to achieve the same level of economic participation as a man. A person living in a malaria-endemic region faces health capability constraints that income alone cannot address. Equal primary goods, therefore, can coexist with radical inequality in capabilities.

This is not a marginal refinement of Rawls. It changes the evaluative space fundamentally. Instead of asking "do people have equal resources?", the capability approach asks "do people have equal freedoms?" These questions can yield very different answers — and very different policy prescriptions.

The conversion problem is especially acute in development contexts, where personal, social, and environmental variation is extreme. South Africa — with its legacy of spatial inequality, gendered violence, HIV prevalence, and disability rates — presents conversion challenges that make resource-based evaluation particularly misleading. Equal per-capita transfers to a household in Sandton and a household in a deep-rural Eastern Cape village represent radically different capability impacts.

## How to Apply

1. **Track capabilities, not just resources.** When evaluating resource-allocation programmes (grants, transfers, infrastructure), measure what people can actually do with the resources, not just whether they received them.
2. **Identify the binding conversion factors.** For the population in question, which personal, social, and environmental factors most affect the conversion of resources into capabilities? Gender norms? Disability? Distance from services? Disease burden?
3. **Adjust for conversion variation.** Equal resource allocation is not necessarily equitable. People with greater conversion disadvantages may need more resources to achieve the same capabilities.
4. **Distinguish the Rawls-Sen debate precisely.** Sen respects Rawls's project but argues the evaluative space is wrong. Primary goods are not freedoms; the conversion gap is not a marginal complication but a fundamental objection.

## Examples

**Situation:** The South African National Health Insurance (NHI) aims to provide equal access to healthcare for all citizens. An evaluation measures success by whether the same per-capita health expenditure is available to all.
**Application:** Equal per-capita expenditure does not yield equal health capabilities. A person with HIV/AIDS requires higher expenditure than a healthy person to achieve the same health capability. A person in a remote rural area faces higher transport costs and lower facility quality. A woman facing gender-based violence has health needs that standard per-capita expenditure fails to address. The capability approach would measure whether people can actually *be healthy* — not just whether the same resources are notionally available.
**Result:** Supplement resource-equality metrics with capability-equality metrics: actual health outcomes, effective access to care (not just nominal availability), and attention to conversion factors that create systematic inequality in the translation of health expenditure into health capability.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Dismiss Rawls's project entirely.
**Why:** Sen's critique is of the evaluative space (primary goods vs capabilities), not of Rawls's broader commitment to justice, fairness, and concern for the least advantaged. Sen shares many of Rawls's moral commitments while arguing for a different informational basis.

**Don't:** Treat conversion factors as mere "complications" that can be addressed by topping up resources.
**Why:** Some conversion disadvantages — disability, chronic illness, entrenched discrimination — cannot be fully compensated by additional resources. They require structural changes in the social and institutional environment. The conversion problem is not a technical adjustment; it is a fundamental challenge to resource-based evaluation.
