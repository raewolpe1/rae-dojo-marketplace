# Capability vs Utility

## When to Use

- When an evaluation framework uses utility (happiness, satisfaction, preference-fulfilment) as the measure of well-being.
- When the adaptive preferences problem is relevant — when deprived populations report high satisfaction.
- When debating between welfarist evaluation and capability-based evaluation.

## Fails When

- **Subjective well-being data is being used as one input among many.** The critique is of exclusive reliance on utility, not of all use of subjective data.
- **The context is consumer welfare in affluent settings** where adaptive preferences may be less severe.

## Core Concept

Utilitarian welfarism judges social states exclusively by the utilities (happiness, satisfaction, or preference-fulfilment) of the individuals in those states. Sen identifies three fundamental problems.

First, **adaptive preferences**. People adapt their expectations and desires to what they believe is feasible. A woman denied education in a patriarchal society may report high life satisfaction because she has internalised the social norms that constrain her. A bonded labourer may express contentment because he has no conception of an alternative life. Using their reported utility as evidence that they are well-off is a profound evaluative error — it mistakes the absence of frustrated desire for the presence of genuine well-being. Deprivation can be self-concealing when it shapes the preferences of the deprived.

Second, **distributional indifference**. Classical utilitarianism cares about the sum total of utility, not its distribution. A policy that massively benefits the well-off while marginally harming the poor can be utility-maximising if the gains of the well-off are large enough. This feature makes utilitarianism unsuitable as an evaluative framework for development, where distributional concerns are paramount.

Third, **neglect of rights, agency, and process**. Utility is a mental state — it says nothing about how it was produced. A person made happy through manipulation, false consciousness, or denial of agency registers the same utility as one who achieves happiness through free choice and full information. The process by which outcomes are achieved — whether through participation or imposition, through agency or dependency — is invisible to utilitarian evaluation.

The capability approach evaluates in a different space: the substantive freedoms people have to achieve functionings they have reason to value. This space does not suffer from adaptive preferences (capabilities are assessed objectively, not through reported satisfaction), captures distribution (whose capabilities are expanding?), and is sensitive to agency and process (are people choosing their own paths?).

## How to Apply

1. **Do not use satisfaction surveys as the primary outcome measure.** They can be useful supplements but are unreliable as the primary metric of well-being, particularly for deprived populations.
2. **Track objective capabilities alongside subjective well-being.** Health status, educational attainment, freedom from violence, political participation — these are not subject to the adaptive preferences problem.
3. **Disaggregate.** Aggregate satisfaction can conceal severe capability deprivation of specific groups (women, disabled, minorities) who have adapted to their deprivation.
4. **Question high satisfaction in deprived populations.** If a severely deprived group reports high satisfaction, this is more likely evidence of adaptive preferences than genuine well-being.

## Examples

**Situation:** A livelihoods programme in rural Limpopo reports that 85% of participants are "satisfied" with the programme, despite limited evidence of capability expansion.
**Application:** High reported satisfaction among participants who remain in severe capability deprivation (poor health outcomes, limited education, restricted mobility, little political voice) suggests adaptive preferences rather than genuine well-being. Participants may have adjusted expectations to what the programme offers because they have no experience of what a fully functioning programme could deliver. Relying on satisfaction data alone would lead to the conclusion that the programme is successful when capability analysis suggests otherwise.
**Result:** Supplement satisfaction data with direct capability measures. Where satisfaction is high but capabilities remain severely constrained, investigate the possibility of adaptive preferences.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Dismiss all subjective data as useless.
**Why:** Subjective well-being data captures something real — people's own experience of their lives. The critique is of using it as the *sole* or *primary* metric, not of using it at all. Combined with objective capability measures, subjective data is informative.

**Don't:** Assume that all departures between subjective satisfaction and objective capability indicate adaptive preferences.
**Why:** People may have genuine reasons for valuing aspects of their lives that objective measures miss — community belonging, spiritual fulfilment, cultural identity. The adaptive preferences argument should be used with care, not as a universal override of reported experience.
