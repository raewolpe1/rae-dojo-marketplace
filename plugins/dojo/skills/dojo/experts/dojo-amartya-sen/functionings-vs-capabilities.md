# Functionings vs Capabilities

## When to Use

- When an evaluation team conflates what people achieve with what they are free to achieve.
- When designing indicators — to ensure they capture both the achievement and the freedom dimension.
- When assessing whether a programme has genuinely expanded options or merely directed people toward particular outcomes.
- When the Sen-Nussbaum distinction matters: both use "capabilities" but with different emphasis on this distinction.

## Fails When

- **Data constraints make capability measurement impossible.** In practice, most evaluations can only observe functionings. The distinction remains conceptually important even when only functionings are measured, but you should not pretend to measure capabilities when you are measuring functionings.
- **The context makes choice irrelevant.** For basic survival functionings (being alive, being nourished at subsistence level), the distinction between functioning and capability may have little practical significance — the relevant question is whether the functioning is achieved at all.

## Core Concept

Functionings are the various states of being and doing that a person achieves: being well-nourished, being educated, being employed, participating in community life, being free from avoidable disease. They are the constituents of a person's life — what a person actually manages to do or be.

Capabilities are the real freedoms or opportunities that a person has to achieve functionings. The capability set represents the range of functioning combinations available to a person — the alternative lives they could lead. Capability is about freedom; functioning is about achievement.

The distinction is essential because two people can have the same achieved functioning for entirely different reasons. A person who fasts for religious reasons and a person who starves from poverty are both not eating. Their functioning (going without food) is identical. Their capabilities are radically different — one has the freedom to eat and chooses not to; the other has no such freedom. Evaluating only the functioning misses everything that matters about these two situations.

This has profound implications for development evaluation. A programme that achieves high school attendance through compulsory attendance laws has improved the functioning (children attend school) but may not have expanded the capability (the freedom to be educated) if the schools provide no genuine learning. Conversely, a programme that provides genuine educational opportunity but faces low take-up has expanded capability even if the functioning is not widely achieved — though this should prompt investigation into why the capability is not being exercised.

The capability set also captures the value of choice itself. Having options — even options one does not exercise — has intrinsic value. A person who has many career options but chooses one is in a different position from a person who has no options and ends up in the same career.

## How to Apply

1. **Specify functionings clearly.** For each dimension of interest, state the relevant functioning: being nourished, being educated, being able to move freely, being able to participate in governance.
2. **Ask whether the functioning is chosen or imposed.** High employment rates may reflect genuine economic opportunity or lack of any alternative to poorly paid work. High school attendance may reflect educational capability or compulsion without learning.
3. **Where possible, measure capability sets directly** — through questions about available options, barriers to choice, and perceptions of freedom. Where not possible, flag that you are measuring functionings and acknowledge the gap.
4. **Disaggregate by conversion factors.** The same resources yield different capability sets for different people. A capable programme evaluation tracks how gender, disability, location, and social position mediate the conversion.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African social grant programme reports that 98% of eligible children receive the Child Support Grant. An evaluation declares the programme "successful."
**Application:** Receipt of the grant is a resource (means), not a functioning or capability. The relevant functionings are: being adequately nourished, being able to attend school regularly, having access to healthcare. The relevant capability question is whether the grant expands real freedoms — can caregivers actually convert the R510 monthly into these functionings given food prices, transport costs, school fees, and healthcare access? In remote rural areas with poor infrastructure, the conversion may be severely constrained compared to peri-urban areas.
**Result:** Evaluate the programme not by grant receipt (an input) but by the functionings and capabilities it enables, disaggregated by location, household composition, and caregiver characteristics.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat functionings as sufficient for evaluation and ignore the capability dimension.
**Why:** A society where everyone achieves the same functionings because they have no choice is very different from one where people achieve similar functionings from a wide capability set. Totalitarian regimes can produce impressive functioning statistics while offering no freedom.

**Don't:** Dismiss capability measurement as impractical and default to income.
**Why:** Practical difficulty does not justify conceptual surrender. Even when capability sets cannot be directly measured, the framework disciplines the evaluation — it forces you to ask about choice, agency, and conversion, even if the answers are approximate.
