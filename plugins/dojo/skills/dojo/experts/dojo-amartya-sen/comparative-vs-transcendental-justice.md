# Comparative vs Transcendental Justice

## When to Use

- When a client or funder insists on defining the "ideal outcome" before any intervention can be assessed.
- When the debate between Rawlsian institutional design and Sen's comparative approach is directly relevant.
- When paralysis results from the inability to specify the perfect arrangement.

## Fails When

- **Constitutional founding moments** where some transcendental reasoning about ideal institutions is genuinely necessary.
- **The comparison is trivial** and does not require philosophical framing.

## Core Concept

The transcendental approach to justice, associated with Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Kant, and Rawls, seeks to identify the characteristics of perfectly just institutions. It asks: what social arrangements would be chosen under conditions of fairness? The comparative approach, which Sen associates with Smith, Condorcet, Wollstonecraft, Marx, and Mill, asks: which of the available alternatives is less unjust?

Sen argues the comparative approach is both more useful and more defensible. The transcendental approach faces three problems. First, the identification-ranking gap: knowing the ideal does not generate a ranking of non-ideal alternatives. You cannot say that an arrangement closer to the ideal in some respects is necessarily more just overall. Second, the plurality problem: reasonable impartial reasoning may yield multiple competing conceptions of ideal justice with no procedure for choosing among them. Rawls himself acknowledged this possibility but did not resolve it. Third, the realisation focus: justice must ultimately concern the lives people actually lead, not merely the institutions that govern them. Institutions are important instrumentally but the focus must be on "realised justice" — what actually happens to people.

The comparative approach avoids these problems. It does not require identifying the ideal. It works with partial orderings — clear cases where one situation is less unjust than another. It focuses on realised outcomes. And it admits of plurality — reasonable people may agree that slavery is unjust without agreeing on the ideal society.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify manifest injustices** in the situation at hand — deprivation, discrimination, denial of freedoms — that all reasonable perspectives would acknowledge.
2. **Compare available alternatives** for their capacity to reduce these injustices, using evidence and public reasoning.
3. **Accept partial orderings.** You do not need to rank all alternatives; identifying clear improvements is sufficient for action.
4. **Do not wait for the ideal.** Policy and programme design can proceed on the basis of comparative assessment.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African municipal planning process stalls because stakeholders cannot agree on the ideal distribution of services across wards.
**Application:** Comparative justice suggests identifying the most manifest injustices — wards with no sanitation, no access to emergency services, or extreme overcrowding — and prioritising these without requiring agreement on the ideal service distribution. All reasonable perspectives would agree that wards with no sanitation are worse off than wards with some sanitation; this comparative judgement is sufficient for prioritisation.
**Result:** The planning process can proceed by addressing clear cases of deprivation first, even without resolving all distributional disagreements.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Dismiss all institutional analysis as "transcendental" and therefore useless.
**Why:** Sen's critique is of transcendental *exclusivity* — the view that only ideal institutional design matters. Institutional analysis remains important; Sen's point is that it must be supplemented by attention to realised outcomes and comparative assessment.
