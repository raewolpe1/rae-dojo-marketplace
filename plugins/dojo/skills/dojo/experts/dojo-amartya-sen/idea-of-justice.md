# The Idea of Justice

## When to Use

- When someone demands a complete theory of the just society before addressing manifest injustice.
- When comparing alternative policies or programmes and asking which is more just.
- When debating whether justice requires ideal institutional design or practical injustice reduction.
- When public reasoning and democratic deliberation are being sidelined in favour of expert determination of "the right answer."

## Fails When

- **Institutional design questions genuinely require transcendental reasoning.** Constitutional design may benefit from thinking about ideal institutions, though even here comparative reasoning is essential.
- **The user wants Rawls specifically.** Sen engages Rawls respectfully but fundamentally disagrees about method. If the user needs Rawlsian analysis, route to Rawls.

## Core Concept

*The Idea of Justice* (2009) argues that justice should be understood comparatively — as the reduction of manifest injustice — rather than transcendentally — as the identification of perfectly just institutions.

The transcendental approach, exemplified by Rawls, asks: what would perfectly just institutions look like? Sen argues this approach has three fundamental problems. First, identifying the ideal does not help rank non-ideal alternatives. Knowing that the Mona Lisa is the most beautiful painting does not help you choose between a Picasso and a Dalí. Second, reasonable people exercising impartial reasoning may disagree about what the perfectly just arrangement would be — the uniqueness required by transcendental theories is implausible. Third, the approach focuses on institutions rather than the lives people actually lead. Just institutions are necessary but not sufficient — outcomes depend on actual behaviour, not just institutional design.

The comparative approach asks a different question: given the available alternatives, which reduces injustice more? This question is answerable — through public reasoning, evidence, and the kind of partial ordering that social choice theory makes possible — even when the transcendental question is not. We can say with confidence that the abolition of slavery was a reduction in injustice without having identified the perfectly just society. We can say that expanding women's access to education reduces gender injustice without solving every philosophical puzzle about the ideal society.

Public reasoning is central. Justice is not determined by a philosopher in isolation but through open, impartial scrutiny that includes the perspectives of those affected. Sen draws on Adam Smith's "impartial spectator" — the requirement to consider how our judgements look from the perspective of any fair-minded observer, not just our own community.

## How to Apply

1. **Compare available alternatives** rather than measuring against an ideal. Which policy, programme, or institutional arrangement reduces manifest injustice more?
2. **Identify manifest injustices** that all reasonable perspectives would recognise — severe deprivation, discrimination, denial of basic freedoms — without requiring agreement on the ideal.
3. **Include the perspectives of the affected.** Justice assessment requires public reasoning that incorporates the voices of those who experience the injustice, not just expert determination.
4. **Accept incomplete rankings.** You may not be able to rank all alternatives completely, but partial orderings that identify clear improvements over clear injustices are sufficient for action.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African policy debate about whether to prioritise housing delivery or healthcare expansion. Some argue that without resolving the fundamental question of distributive justice, any allocation is arbitrary.
**Application:** The comparative approach does not require resolving the complete theory of distributive justice. It asks: given current conditions, which intervention would reduce manifest injustice more? If thousands of people are dying from treatable conditions while housing, though inadequate, provides basic shelter, then healthcare expansion addresses a more urgent injustice. This comparative judgement can be made through public reasoning without a complete theory of ideal resource allocation.
**Result:** Proceed with the intervention that addresses the most manifest injustice, informed by public reasoning and evidence, without waiting for the resolution of all philosophical disagreements about justice.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Demand a complete theory of justice before addressing any injustice.
**Why:** The demand for completeness becomes a recipe for paralysis. We can act on clear cases of injustice — slavery, famine, gender discrimination — without having solved every philosophical puzzle about the ideal society.

**Don't:** Exclude the perspectives of affected populations from justice assessment.
**Why:** Justice determined solely by experts or philosophers fails the impartiality test. The perspectives of those who experience deprivation and discrimination are essential inputs to reasoned assessment.
