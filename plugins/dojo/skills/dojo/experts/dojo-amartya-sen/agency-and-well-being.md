# Agency and Well-Being

## When to Use

- When a development programme is being evaluated and the question is whether participants are agents or recipients.
- When designing M&E frameworks that need to capture both well-being improvement and agency expansion.
- When critiquing paternalistic approaches that improve outcomes while denying voice and choice.
- When assessing women's empowerment — agency is the core dimension of empowerment.

## Fails When

- **Survival is the immediate priority.** In emergency contexts, providing for well-being without full agency may be necessary and justified.
- **Agency is conflated with all positive outcomes.** Agency is a specific concept — the capacity to act and bring about change — not a synonym for "good things happening."

## Core Concept

Sen distinguishes between two aspects of a person that any adequate evaluative framework must recognise. The well-being aspect concerns a person's personal advantage — how well-off they are, assessed in the space of capabilities. The agency aspect concerns a person's capacity to act, to pursue goals they have reason to value, to participate in social and political life, and to bring about change in the world.

These aspects are related but distinct. A programme that improves a person's health, nutrition, and housing (well-being) while giving them no voice in programme design, no choice about the form of assistance, and no role in governance (agency) has addressed one aspect while neglecting the other. A person whose well-being is entirely provided by others — with no scope for self-directed action — is in a fundamentally different position from a person who achieves the same well-being through their own agency.

The agency distinction has several implications. First, development must be concerned not only with improving outcomes but with expanding people's capacity to shape their own lives. Second, the process by which outcomes are achieved matters, not just the outcomes themselves. Third, women's empowerment cannot be reduced to improved outcomes for women — it must include expanded agency: decision-making power, political participation, economic independence, and voice.

In the South African context, the distinction between agency and well-being illuminates the social grant system. Grants improve well-being (recipients can buy food, pay for transport, access services). But do they expand agency? This depends on whether recipients gain capacity for self-directed action or remain dependent. The answer is empirically complex — research suggests grants can be enabling (freeing time and resources for job search, small enterprise, community participation) or dependency-creating, depending on design and context.

## How to Apply

1. **Track agency alongside well-being.** Include indicators of decision-making, voice, participation, and self-directed action — not just outcomes like income, health, and education.
2. **Assess the process of development.** Are people participating in programme design and governance? Are they exercising genuine choice? Are they agents in their own development?
3. **Disaggregate agency by gender.** Women's agency — economic independence, decision-making power, freedom from violence, political participation — is a critical dimension that aggregate measures often miss.
4. **Design for agency, not just delivery.** Programmes that build capacity for self-directed action are different from programmes that deliver outcomes to passive recipients.

## Examples

**Situation:** An early childhood development programme in KwaZulu-Natal measures success through child nutrition outcomes and developmental milestones.
**Application:** These are well-being measures for children — important but incomplete. The agency dimension asks: do caregivers (predominantly mothers) exercise meaningful agency in the programme? Do they participate in decisions about the programme's design? Has their capacity for self-directed action expanded — in child-rearing, in economic activity, in community governance? A programme that improves child outcomes while treating mothers as passive recipients of expert instruction has addressed well-being but not agency.
**Result:** Supplement child outcome measures with caregiver agency indicators: participation in programme governance, decision-making autonomy, economic agency, and voice in community affairs.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat agency as an optional extra that can be addressed after well-being is secured.
**Why:** Agency is not a luxury — it is an irreducible aspect of the person. A well-fed, well-housed person with no capacity for self-directed action is not fully developed.

**Don't:** Conflate agency with satisfaction or consent.
**Why:** A person may express satisfaction with a programme while having no meaningful agency within it — adaptive preferences again. Agency requires actual capacity to act, not merely acquiescence.
