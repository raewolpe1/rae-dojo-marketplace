# Capability Approach Foundations

## When to Use

- When introducing the capability approach for the first time to a client, funder, or evaluation team.
- When justifying why an evaluation should track capabilities rather than income or utility alone.
- When someone asks "what should we measure?" and the answer requires a conceptual reorientation.
- When critiquing an existing framework that uses the wrong evaluative space.

## Fails When

- **The user expects a turnkey measurement instrument.** The capability approach is a framework for evaluation, not a ready-made survey. Operationalisation requires deliberation about which capabilities matter in context.
- **You present it as purely abstract.** The approach has been operationalised in the Human Development Index, the Multidimensional Poverty Index, and countless programme evaluations. Its abstractness is deliberate and principled, not a weakness.

## Core Concept

The capability approach proposes that the appropriate evaluative space for assessing human well-being and advantage is the space of capabilities — the substantive freedoms people have to achieve functionings they have reason to value.

The approach rests on three distinctions. First, the distinction between means and ends: income, commodities, and resources are means to well-being, not well-being itself. Evaluating advantage by looking at means misses the central question — what can people actually do and be? Second, the distinction between functionings and capabilities: functionings are the states of being and doing that constitute a person's life (being nourished, being educated, participating in community life); capabilities are the freedoms to achieve those functionings. A person who chooses not to eat (fasting) and a person who cannot eat (starving) have the same functioning but radically different capabilities. Third, the distinction between achievement and freedom: evaluating only what people achieve ignores the freedom dimension — whether they had genuine choice.

The informational basis of the capability approach is thus richer than utilitarian welfarism (which uses only utility), Rawlsian justice (which uses primary goods), or income-based approaches (which use monetary resources). It asks: what are people actually free to do and be, and what constrains those freedoms?

A crucial feature is the emphasis on human diversity. People differ in their ability to convert resources into capabilities due to personal characteristics (age, disability, health), social conditions (gender norms, discrimination, class), and environmental factors (climate, infrastructure, epidemiology). The conversion problem means that equal resources do not yield equal capabilities.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the relevant capability dimensions** for the population and context, through deliberation with stakeholders — not from a fixed universal list.
2. **Track functionings and capabilities separately** where possible. A programme may improve achieved functionings (more children in school) without expanding capabilities (if attendance is coerced rather than chosen).
3. **Attend to conversion factors.** Disaggregate by gender, disability, location, and other characteristics that affect the conversion of resources into capabilities.
4. **Assess both the opportunity aspect** (what options are available) and the **process aspect** (whether people exercise genuine choice and agency).

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African municipality's development plan tracks household income, housing delivery, and school enrolment as its key development indicators.
**Application:** Income tracks means, not ends. Housing delivery tracks an output, not a capability — a house without water, electricity, or safety does not constitute the capability to be adequately sheltered. School enrolment tracks attendance, not educational capability — a child enrolled in a dysfunctional school with unqualified teachers is not developing educational capability. The capability approach would ask: can residents actually be healthy (not just have a clinic nearby), actually be educated (not just be enrolled), actually participate in community governance (not just live in a democracy)?
**Result:** Supplement output indicators with direct capability measures: learning outcomes, health status, effective access to services, subjective and objective agency, participation in decision-making.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Present a fixed list of capabilities as "the" capability approach.
**Why:** Sen deliberately resists specifying a canonical list, arguing that the content of capabilities must be determined through public reasoning. Presenting a fixed list conflates Sen's open framework with Nussbaum's specified version — a significant philosophical difference.

**Don't:** Reduce the capability approach to the HDI.
**Why:** The Human Development Index operationalises three dimensions of capability (health, education, income) for cross-country comparison. It is a useful but crude summary measure, not the capability approach itself. The approach is far richer than any single index can capture.
