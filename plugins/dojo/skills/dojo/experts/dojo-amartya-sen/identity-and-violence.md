# Identity and Violence

## When to Use

- When identity-based conflict — ethnic, religious, linguistic — is being discussed in development contexts.
- When the "clash of civilisations" thesis is invoked to explain conflict.
- When someone reduces a population to a single identity category ("the poor," "the Zulus," "the Muslims").
- When social cohesion programmes need a conceptual framework for understanding identity.

## Fails When

- **Identity-based discrimination is real and requires acknowledgement.** The point is not that identity categories are irrelevant but that individuals are not reducible to any single identity.
- **Structural analysis is needed.** Identity analysis complements, not replaces, structural analysis of inequality and power.

## Core Concept

*Identity and Violence: The Illusion of Destiny* (2006) argues that the reduction of human identity to a single dimension — civilisational, religious, ethnic — is both intellectually false and a major source of violence in the contemporary world.

Every person has multiple identities: nationality, ethnicity, religion, language, class, gender, profession, political belief, and many more. The relative importance of each identity depends on context and choice. A South African woman may be simultaneously Zulu, Christian, a nurse, a trade union member, a mother, an ANC supporter, and a jazz lover — and the significance of each identity varies across situations. No single identity defines her.

The "illusion of singular identity" occurs when one identity is treated as the person's defining characteristic — and all other identities are subordinated or ignored. This illusion is cultivated deliberately by those who benefit from communal mobilisation: politicians who mobilise along ethnic lines, religious leaders who claim authority over "their" community, and intellectuals like Samuel Huntington who partition humanity into "civilisations" that supposedly determine values and behaviour.

The consequences are violent. Communal violence requires the cultivation of singular identity — the idea that "we" (defined by one identity) are fundamentally different from "them." Once this illusion is established, violence follows because the full humanity of the "other" — their multiple identities, their individual complexity — has been erased.

The antidote is the recognition of plural identity and the exercise of reasoned choice about which identities matter, and how much, in any given context. This is not relativism — it is the application of reasoning to identity, the refusal to accept identity as destiny.

## How to Apply

1. **Resist singular categorisation.** When designing programmes or analyses, do not reduce populations to single-identity categories. "The poor," "rural communities," "the youth" — each hides enormous internal diversity.
2. **Recognise choice in identity.** Identities are not given; their relative importance is subject to reasoned choice. Programmes that acknowledge and support plural identity are more likely to build social cohesion than those that reinforce singular categorisation.
3. **Interrogate identity mobilisation.** When identity-based conflict arises, ask who benefits from the cultivation of singular identity and how the illusion is being manufactured.
4. **Design for cross-cutting identities.** Social programmes, community development initiatives, and governance structures that create connections across identity boundaries reduce the salience of any single identity.

## Examples

**Situation:** A community development programme in a South African municipality with Zulu, Sotho, and Coloured residents designs separate interventions for each "community."
**Application:** This approach reinforces singular ethnic identity and may deepen communal divisions. Residents have multiple identities — ethnic, occupational, generational, religious, residential — and many of these cross-cut ethnic boundaries. A programme designed around shared concerns (water access, safety, education quality) rather than ethnic categories creates cross-cutting connections that reduce the salience of ethnic identity.
**Result:** Design programmes around shared capability deficits rather than ethnic identity categories. Create governance structures that bring residents together across identity boundaries.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Dismiss identity as irrelevant because people have multiple identities.
**Why:** Identity categories are socially real and consequential. Racial discrimination in South Africa, caste discrimination in India, gender discrimination everywhere — these operate through identity categories and must be analysed as such. The point is not that identity doesn't matter but that persons are not reducible to any single identity.

**Don't:** Accept the "clash of civilisations" as an analytical framework.
**Why:** The thesis assigns people to civilisational boxes and treats those boxes as determinative of values and behaviour. This is empirically false — more variation exists within "civilisations" than between them — and politically dangerous, as it legitimises the singular identity illusion at the global scale.
