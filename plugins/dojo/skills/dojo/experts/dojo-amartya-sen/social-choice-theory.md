# Social Choice Theory

## When to Use

- When someone argues that rational collective decision-making is impossible (misinterpreting Arrow's theorem).
- When the question involves how to aggregate diverse individual assessments into a collective evaluation — including in M&E contexts where multiple stakeholders have different value priorities.
- When debating whether interpersonal comparisons of well-being are possible or admissible.

## Fails When

- **The user needs practical electoral design.** Social choice theory informs the philosophical foundations of collective choice but does not prescribe specific voting mechanisms.
- **The context requires precise cardinal measurement.** Social choice theory works with ordinal rankings and partial orderings — it does not solve problems that require exact interpersonal utility measurement.

## Core Concept

Social choice theory, as developed in *Collective Choice and Social Welfare* (1970, expanded 2017), extends Kenneth Arrow's framework to demonstrate that coherent collective judgements are possible — contrary to the nihilistic interpretation of Arrow's impossibility theorem.

Arrow's theorem shows that no social welfare function can simultaneously satisfy four seemingly reasonable conditions (unrestricted domain, Pareto principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship) when only ordinal preference rankings without interpersonal comparisons are available. The common — and mistaken — conclusion drawn from this is that rational social choice is impossible.

Sen's contribution is to show that relaxing the informational constraint transforms the problem. If we permit interpersonal comparisons of well-being — even partial, rough ones — the impossibility dissolves. We do not need exact utility measurement; we need only the kind of approximate interpersonal comparison that ordinary moral reasoning uses constantly (recognising that a starving person has more urgent needs than a well-fed one). Partial orderings — incomplete but consistent rankings — can be coherent and informative even when a complete ranking is not achievable.

This has far-reaching implications for development evaluation. It means that collective assessments of well-being, poverty, and inequality are not condemned to incoherence. It means that the evaluative framework we use — its informational basis — determines what collective judgements are possible. And it means that requiring complete, precise rankings is unnecessary; partial orderings that identify clear cases of injustice or deprivation are both achievable and useful.

## How to Apply

1. **Do not demand complete rankings.** In evaluation, it is often possible to identify clear cases of deprivation or injustice without achieving a complete ordering of all alternatives. Partial rankings that say "A is clearly worse than B" without ranking every option are valuable.
2. **Permit interpersonal comparison.** When assessing poverty, inequality, or programme impact, allow rough interpersonal comparisons — a severely malnourished child is worse off than a well-nourished one, regardless of reported preferences.
3. **Make the informational basis explicit.** Different evaluation frameworks permit different collective judgements. Specify what information your framework uses and what it excludes.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African national planning commission faces disagreement among experts about which dimension of poverty — income, health, education, safety — should be weighted most heavily in the national development indicators.
**Application:** Social choice theory suggests that a complete ranking of all possible weighting schemes is neither necessary nor achievable. However, partial orderings are possible: all reasonable weighting schemes would rank a province with low income, low life expectancy, and low educational attainment below a province with better outcomes on all three. Focus on the robust comparisons — the cases where all reasonable weighting schemes agree — rather than demanding a single "correct" set of weights.
**Result:** Design the indicator framework to highlight robust comparisons (clear cases of multi-dimensional deprivation) rather than producing a single composite index with contested weights.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Invoke Arrow's theorem to argue that all collective evaluations are arbitrary.
**Why:** Arrow's result is about a specific informational constraint (ordinal preferences without interpersonal comparison). Relaxing that constraint — which is justified in development contexts — opens the space for coherent collective judgement.

**Don't:** Demand complete precision in interpersonal comparisons before allowing any comparative judgement.
**Why:** Partial comparisons — "this person is clearly worse off than that one" — are sufficient for many evaluative purposes and are routinely made in moral reasoning.
