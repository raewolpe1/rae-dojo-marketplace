# Gender and Missing Women

## When to Use

- When conducting gender analysis in development programmes or policies.
- When demographic data reveals gender-based capability inequalities.
- When intra-household allocation is relevant — who controls resources, who benefits.
- When evaluating women's empowerment programmes.

## Fails When

- **The analysis is purely demographic without connecting to capability analysis.** The missing women finding is powerful but must be connected to the broader capability framework.
- **The context is one where sex ratios are roughly equal** and the missing women analysis is not directly relevant — though gender capability inequality may still be severe.

## Core Concept

In 1990 Sen estimated that more than 100 million women were "missing" worldwide — women who would have been alive had they received the same nutrition, healthcare, and social support as men. The estimate, later refined to over 100 million with regional breakdowns, revealed the demographic consequence of systematic gender discrimination in health, nutrition, and care.

The mechanism is sex-selective neglect rather than, primarily, sex-selective abortion or infanticide (though these contribute in some regions). Girls and women in many societies receive less food, less healthcare, and less care during illness than boys and men in the same household. The cumulative effect of this systematic neglect manifests in skewed sex ratios — particularly in South Asia, China, and parts of North Africa and West Asia.

The missing women analysis illustrates several capability approach principles. First, aggregate statistics conceal gender-specific deprivation. A household that appears above the poverty line may contain severely deprived women and girls. Second, intra-household allocation matters — who controls and benefits from household resources is invisible to household-level data. Third, the functioning-capability distinction is critical: the relevant deprivation is not that women "choose" less healthcare but that they lack the capability to access it due to discriminatory social norms.

Women's agency is the antidote. Where women have economic independence, political participation, property rights, and voice in household decisions, gender-based capability inequality narrows. Women's education is particularly powerful — it improves health outcomes for women and children, reduces fertility, increases economic participation, and expands political voice. The connection between women's agency and broader development outcomes is one of the most robust empirical findings in development economics.

## How to Apply

1. **Disaggregate all indicators by sex.** No development evaluation should present only aggregate data. Sex-disaggregated data reveals patterns invisible in aggregates.
2. **Track intra-household allocation.** Who makes spending decisions? Who accesses healthcare? Who eats first? Household-level data misses these critical within-household inequalities.
3. **Measure women's agency directly.** Decision-making power, economic independence, freedom from violence, political participation, and mobility — these are the dimensions of women's agency.
4. **Connect women's agency to development outcomes.** Women's education reduces child mortality. Women's economic participation reduces poverty. Women's political participation improves public goods provision. These connections should be explicit in programme theories.

## Examples

**Situation:** A nutrition programme in Mpumalanga reports improved household food security but has not disaggregated data by gender.
**Application:** Household-level food security improvement may mask persistent gender-based malnutrition if food allocation within the household favours men and boys. In contexts where women eat last and least, household food security does not guarantee women's nutritional adequacy. The missing women analysis directs attention to sex-specific nutrition outcomes and intra-household allocation patterns.
**Result:** Disaggregate nutrition data by sex and age. Measure individual nutrition status, not just household food security. Investigate intra-household allocation patterns. Include women's agency indicators — does the programme strengthen women's decision-making power over food allocation?

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat gender analysis as a compliance checkbox rather than an analytical framework.
**Why:** Gender analysis in the capability approach is not about counting women in programme activities. It is about tracking whether the programme expands women's capabilities — their substantive freedoms to be healthy, be educated, be economically active, be politically heard, and be safe.

**Don't:** Assume that economic growth automatically closes gender capability gaps.
**Why:** Economic growth can coexist with persistent and even widening gender inequality. South Africa has relatively high female labour force participation but severe gender-based violence, limited women's safety, and persistent earnings gaps. Growth does not substitute for targeted attention to women's capability deprivation.
