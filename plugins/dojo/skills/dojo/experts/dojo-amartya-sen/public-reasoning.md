# Public Reasoning

## When to Use

- When development priorities are being set by technocrats or external funders without meaningful participation of affected populations.
- When debating whether "experts know best" or whether values must emerge from democratic deliberation.
- When designing evaluation frameworks and the question is who decides what counts as success.
- When someone argues that cultural values are fixed and immune to reasoned scrutiny.

## Fails When

- **Deliberative conditions are absent.** Public reasoning requires basic conditions — freedom of expression, access to information, absence of coercion. Where these are absent, the process is compromised.
- **Urgency precludes deliberation.** In emergency situations, waiting for full deliberation may cost lives.

## Core Concept

Public reasoning is the process by which societies, through open and informed deliberation, determine their values, priorities, and the content of development. It is not merely instrumental to good outcomes — it is constitutive of justice itself. A just arrangement is one that can withstand the scrutiny of open, impartial reasoning that includes the perspectives of all affected parties.

Sen draws on Adam Smith's "impartial spectator" device — the requirement to subject one's judgements to the scrutiny of any fair-minded observer, not just members of one's own community. This is crucial because parochial reasoning — judgement confined to one's own group — can be systematically biased. Open public reasoning requires exposure to perspectives from outside one's immediate community.

Public reasoning performs three functions in Sen's framework. First, it is how societies determine the content of capabilities that matter — which functionings are valuable, how they should be weighted, what trade-offs are acceptable. This is why Sen resists fixing a canonical capability list: the content must emerge from democratic deliberation, not philosophical fiat. Second, public reasoning is the mechanism through which evaluative frameworks are scrutinised and improved — through the discipline of justifying one's evaluative choices to others. Third, public reasoning has constructive value — through the process of deliberation, people form and refine their values, discover common ground, and develop the shared understandings needed for collective action.

The argumentative tradition is not confined to Western democracy. Sen emphasises the rich traditions of public reasoning in Indian history (Buddhist councils, Mughal emperor Akbar's interfaith dialogues), African palaver and indaba traditions, and other non-Western contexts. The claim that democracy is a "Western" value imposed on other cultures is both historically false and politically self-serving — it is typically made by authoritarian rulers, not by the populations they govern.

## How to Apply

1. **Create conditions for informed deliberation.** Public reasoning requires access to information, freedom of expression, and the absence of coercion. These are preconditions, not luxuries.
2. **Include perspectives of the affected.** Those who experience deprivation and injustice must be part of the deliberation, not merely represented by proxy.
3. **Subject evaluative frameworks to public scrutiny.** The choice of indicators, the weighting of dimensions, the definition of success — all should be open to democratic contestation.
4. **Draw on diverse traditions of reasoning.** Public reasoning is not confined to parliamentary procedure. Traditional deliberative processes — indaba, imbizo, community assemblies — can be vehicles for public reasoning.

## Examples

**Situation:** A donor-funded programme in South Africa's Eastern Cape defines its success indicators without consulting communities — measuring school enrolment, clinic utilisation, and household income.
**Application:** Public reasoning would question whether these indicators capture what communities value. Deliberation with affected populations might reveal that safety from violence, access to water, and meaningful employment are higher priorities than the indicators chosen. The indicators reflect donor priorities, not community valuations — a failure of public reasoning.
**Result:** Establish deliberative processes — community meetings, participatory indicator development, regular feedback mechanisms — that allow affected populations to shape the evaluative framework.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat expert-determined indicators as neutral and objective.
**Why:** All indicator choices embody value judgements about what matters. These value judgements should be subject to democratic scrutiny, not hidden behind a veneer of technical objectivity.

**Don't:** Dismiss deliberative processes as impractical in development contexts.
**Why:** The Southern African tradition of community deliberation — imbizo, indaba, community meetings — provides ready vehicles for public reasoning. The institutional infrastructure exists; what is often missing is the willingness of development agencies to subject their evaluative frameworks to community scrutiny.
