# Middle Manufacturing

## When to Use
- When selecting priority sectors or value chains.
- When asking what the next tier of industrial capability would be.
- When basic manufacturing is already reasonably served.

## Fails When
- **The question is national industrial strategy at macro level.** Route to Rodrik (binding constraints) or Lin (comparative advantage). Sutton's unit is the firm and the product line.

## Core Concept

**The finding across all five economies: capabilities are well developed across a broad range of basic manufacturing. Continued growth will require a broadening in the range of activity into middle manufacturing.**

**What "middle" means operationally.** Not high technology. Not frontier innovation. The tier above basic processing and assembly: components, intermediate goods, engineered products, things with tighter tolerances, more complex supply chains, and more demanding buyers. It is where value added rises and where the capability requirement steps up — and it is precisely the tier that is empty in these economies.

**Why it is empty.** Two reasons, and they are the same reason. The **capability window** for a middle-manufacturing product line has a threshold *b* that domestic firms sit below, so they are not in the market. And the **adjacent-line logic** means nobody arrives there by growing within basic manufacturing — you move laterally into a neighbouring line, and there is no neighbour.

**Sutton's route into it, which is the whole policy conclusion of the series:**

> Broadening into middle manufacturing → requires improving the **effectiveness of manufacturing multinationals** → requires **strengthening national investment agencies** to international best practice.

Note the shape of that chain. The route to the middle tier runs **through the multinationals**, because they are already above the threshold in the relevant product lines and are the only mechanism by which the capability arrives in the territory at all. The investment agency is the instrument that operates the mechanism. That is a duller and more specific conclusion than any industrial policy document reaches, and it is grounded in fieldwork on five economies.

**The complementary constraints.** The Ethiopia volume also identifies: **import substitution needing to be nurtured on a level playing field with export projects**, which were receiving preferential treatment; **access to medium-term finance for growth**; and **availability of land for industrial use**. These are real, and note where they sit — as constraints on firms *already at the frontier* attempting the next tier, not as the general answer to why firms are small.

**The South African difference, which must be stated.** South Africa has middle manufacturing. It has automotive components, chemicals, engineered products, and a corporate sector with real capability. The Sutton diagnosis for Ethiopia — the middle tier is empty — does not transfer. What transfers is the **method**: identify where the capability window is empty in *this* economy, ask what the route into it is, and ask whether that route runs through multinationals, traders, or something else. The answer will be different. The question is the same.

## How to Apply

1. **Locate the empty tier.** Where in the product-line space does the province have no firms above threshold?
2. **Ask what the route in is.** Growth from below (rare), lateral move from an adjacent line (usual), trade-to-manufacturing (Ethiopian), or multinational transfer (Sutton's conclusion).
3. **Follow the multinational chain** where the route runs through it — and then audit the investment agency, because that is the instrument.
4. **Do not import the Ethiopian diagnosis.** SA has a middle tier. Find the actual gap.
5. **Note the second-order constraints** — medium-term finance, industrial land, the export/import-substitution playing field — as frontier constraints, not general ones.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial strategy selects priority value chains on the basis of employment share and growth rate.

**Application:** That selects on where firms already are, which is the basic tier by construction. The Sutton question is different: where is the window empty, what would it take to enter, and who could bring the capability? Apply it to, say, agro-processing: the province has basic processing in volume. The empty tier is the engineered end — equipment, packaging technology, ingredient inputs currently imported. Ask who is above threshold in those lines globally, which of them are present or reachable, and whether the investment agency is capable of the relationship-building aftercare that turns their presence into local capability. That is a different priority list from the one growth rates produce.

**Result:** Priorities set by where capability could go rather than where it already is.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Select priorities by current employment or growth.
**Why:** That selects the tier you already have. The question is the empty one.

**Don't:** Import the "middle tier is empty" diagnosis to South Africa.
**Why:** It has one. Run the method, not the conclusion.

**Don't:** Treat medium-term finance and industrial land as the general constraint.
**Why:** In the Ethiopian mapping they bind on frontier firms attempting the next tier — not on the small firm that is below threshold everywhere.
