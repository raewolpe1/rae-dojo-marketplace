# Evidence Base — The Enterprise Map Findings

> **Provenance.** Compiled July 2026 from IGC and LSE published summaries of the Enterprise Map series and from Sutton's own pages. Verify against the primary volumes before quoting in a client deliverable. Entries marked `[CHECK]` are unverified to the primary source.

## When to Use
- **Whenever a strategy assumes firms climb from micro to large.** This is the file that contradicts it.
- When asked where capability comes from.
- When designing a growth pathway, pipeline, or graduation model.

## Fails When
- **The unit is one firm.** These are findings about routes across a population of frontier firms.

## Headline

| Assumption | Ethiopian evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Large firms grew from small manufacturers | **Two** of the largest domestic private companies did | **Contradicted** |
| The main route is organic growth | Around **half** came from import/export trading | **Contradicted** |
| Domestic firms are the job engine | New FDI stock could match all 50 leading firms' jobs | **Incomplete** |
| Capital equipment builds capability | It does not, absent capability | **Contradicted** |

## THE EVIDENCE

### The series
- **Study:** Sutton, J. and co-authors, *The Enterprise Map* series, International Growth Centre / London Publishing Partnership. Five volumes: **Ethiopia** (with Nebil Kellow, 2010); **Ghana** (with Bennet Kpentey, 2012); **Tanzania** (with Donath Olomi, 2012); **Zambia** (with Gillian Langmead); **Mozambique** (fifth volume).
- **Countries:** Ethiopia, Ghana, Tanzania, Zambia, Mozambique — five fast-growing African economies. In 2015 the IMF projected each would exceed 6% GDP growth.
- **Method:** For each country, profile the industrial sectors and roughly **50 leading firms**, which taken together represent **the frontier of national industrial capability**. Industries ranged widely — coffee, cut flowers, chemicals, engineering. Record, industry by industry, **who makes what**, and where the capabilities came from.
- **Finding:** The first systematic and comprehensive account of the industrial sectors of these countries. The fifty firms per country represent almost all the largest firms in their respective sectors.
- **Caveat:** The frontier, not the distribution. This is a study of the top of the capability window, not a sample of all firms. It says what the best can do and where they got it; it does not describe the median firm.

### The origin finding — the one that breaks the pipeline
- **Study:** *An Enterprise Map of Ethiopia* (Sutton & Kellow, 2010).
- **Country:** Ethiopia.
- **Population:** The 50 leading large and mid-size industrial firms (agri-business, manufacturing, construction).
- **Finding:** **Only two of the largest domestic private companies began life as small manufacturers.**
- **Caveat:** Ethiopia, 2010, a specific industrial history including a long period of state ownership. **Do not transfer to South Africa without checking** — SA has an industrial base, a large formal corporate sector, and a history these economies do not share. But the finding is a direct challenge to the graduation assumption and the burden of proof shifts to whoever asserts the pipeline locally.

### The trading route
- **Study:** *An Enterprise Map of Ethiopia* (2010); IGC summary.
- **Country:** Ethiopia.
- **Finding:** **Around half the leading firms emerged from the import/export (trading) sector** — where the deepest and most acute knowledge of local and international market conditions is already at hand. A **huge role is played in manufacturing by long-established trading companies that turned to manufacturing a generation after they began importing.**
- **Caveat:** The mechanism is demand-side knowledge accumulated over decades, not capital. This is why the route is invisible to enterprise policy: the trader does not look like a manufacturer-in-waiting, and the lag is a generation, which is longer than any strategy cycle.

### The FDI arithmetic
- **Study:** *An Enterprise Map of Ethiopia* (2010).
- **Country:** Ethiopia.
- **Finding:** **The current stock of new foreign direct investment projects could generate as many jobs as the fifty leading companies profiled**, combined. FDI projects spanned every sector of Ethiopian industry, led by four source countries: **China, India, Saudi Arabia and Italy**. The authors conclude that **careful monitoring and development of FDI ventures should be a key focus of policy concern**.
- **Caveat:** Ethiopia at a particular moment of FDI inflow from a very low base. The magnitude will not transfer. The structural point — that the FDI stock is a capability and jobs inventory of the same order as the entire domestic frontier — is the transferable part.

### The equipment fallacy
- **Study:** Sutton, "Competing in Capabilities: An Informal Overview"; *Competing in Capabilities* (Clarendon Lectures, OUP, 2012).
- **Country:** Tanzania (the illustrative case).
- **Intervention:** Installation of capital equipment in a firm.
- **Finding:** **If you don't start out with a firm that has the appropriate capabilities, installing capital equipment won't help.** The scarce resource most important to the process of industrial development lies in **the capabilities of firms**.
- **Caveat:** An illustrative case used to carry a theoretical point, not an RCT. But it is the same point McKenzie's capital-grant heterogeneity shows quantitatively: returns to capital are concentrated in the firms that can use it.

### The other policy findings
- **Study:** IGC / LSE research impact summaries of the series.
- **Countries:** All five.
- **Finding:** Capabilities are **well developed across a broad range of basic manufacturing**. Continued growth **will require a broadening in the range of activity into middle manufacturing**. This in turn requires **improving the effectiveness of manufacturing multinationals**, which requires **strengthening national investment agencies** to bring them into line with international best practice. Other issues identified in the Ethiopia volume: **import substitution needing to be nurtured on a level playing field with export projects** (which were receiving preferential treatment); **access to medium-term finance for growth**; and **availability of land for industrial use**.
- **Caveat:** These are the authors' policy conclusions from the mapping, not experimental findings. Note that finance and land appear — but *after* capability, and as constraints on firms already at the frontier, not as the general answer.

## How to Apply

1. **Confront the pipeline assumption with the number.** Two of fifty. Then ask for the local equivalent, which nobody has.
2. **Look for your traders.** If the Ethiopian route holds even partially, the growth manufacturers of 2040 are currently importers and distributors with deep demand-side knowledge — and no enterprise programme is looking at them.
3. **Read the FDI register as a capability inventory.** Who is here, what can they make, what could they teach, who supplies them?
4. **Put capability before capital in every diagnostic sequence.**
5. **State the transfer problem.** Ethiopia is not South Africa. Use the findings to shift the burden of proof, not to settle the question.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy's theory of change runs micro → small → medium → large, with support at each tier.

**Application:** The only systematic firm-level evidence from comparable African economies says this route produced two of Ethiopia's fifty frontier firms, and that roughly half arrived instead from trading. The strategy is funding the rarest route and ignoring the commonest. Two recommendations: state the assumption explicitly and cite what evidence exists for it locally (there will be none, which is itself the finding); and open a workstream on the trading-to-manufacturing route — importers and distributors with twenty years of market knowledge who could be moved into production.

**Result:** A strategy that funds the routes firms actually take.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Present the graduation pipeline as established.
**Why:** Two of fifty, in the only systematic African firm-level study of the question.

**Don't:** Transfer the Ethiopian magnitudes to South Africa.
**Why:** Different industrial history, different corporate sector, different FDI base. The structure travels; the numbers do not.

**Don't:** Read the series as a study of small firms.
**Why:** It profiles the frontier — the fifty leading firms. It says nothing directly about the median enterprise.
