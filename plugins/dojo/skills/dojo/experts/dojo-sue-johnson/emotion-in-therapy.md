# Emotion in Therapy

## When to Use
- When someone wants to understand how EFT works with emotion.
- When the distinction between primary and secondary emotion needs to be explained.
- When someone questions whether "talking about feelings" can produce real change.

## Core Concept
Emotion is not a decorative element in EFT — it is the mechanism of change. Johnson draws on modern emotion theory (particularly the work of Les Greenberg and Antonio Damasio) to argue that emotions are adaptive action tendencies — they orient us toward what matters, organise our behaviour, and communicate our needs to others. In the context of attachment, emotions are the primary signalling system between partners. When I express sadness, I pull you toward me. When I express anger, I push you away. The emotional signal determines the relational response.

EFT distinguishes between several types of emotion. Secondary emotions are reactive, surface-level responses that obscure the underlying experience — the anger that covers fear, the irritation that masks loneliness, the numbness that protects against overwhelming grief. Primary emotions are the core adaptive responses to the situation — the fear of losing the attachment bond, the sadness of disconnection, the longing for closeness. Instrumental emotions are emotions used to influence others — the tears calculated to produce guilt, the anger deployed to control.

The therapeutic task is to move from secondary to primary emotion. When a partner who has been presenting anger accesses the fear underneath it, the relational dynamic shifts. Anger pushes the other partner away; fear pulls them toward. The same person, expressing a different emotional layer, generates a completely different response in their partner. This is not about being "nice" or "soft" — it is about being accurate. The anger is real but it is not the whole truth. The fear is also real, and it is the part that, when expressed, creates the possibility of connection.

Johnson calls this process "emotion coaching" — the therapist helps the client notice, access, articulate, and express their primary emotional experience in the presence of their partner. The therapist is not asking "how do you feel?" as a generic prompt — the therapist is tracking the moment-to-moment emotional process and guiding the client deeper: "Stay with that. What happens inside you right now?"

## How to Apply
1. **Learn to distinguish secondary from primary emotion.** Anger in a relationship context is almost always secondary — a protest against disconnection. Underneath it is fear, sadness, or longing. Numbness is almost always protective — a shutdown response to overwhelming emotional pain.
2. **Follow the emotion, do not redirect it.** When a client begins to access primary emotion, stay with it. Do not reassure. Do not interpret. Do not move to problem-solving. Let the emotion emerge and be witnessed.
3. **Use reflection and heightening.** Reflect the emotion to help the client hear it themselves. Heighten it to make it vivid enough to register with the partner. "So underneath all the anger, there is this place where you feel... like a small child standing at a closed door. Is that it?"
4. **Create the enactment.** Once the primary emotion is accessible, help the client express it directly to their partner. "Can you tell him that? Can you tell him about the closed door?"

## Examples
**Situation:** A man in couples therapy presents rage about his wife's friendships with male colleagues. He has been told by previous therapists that he is controlling and needs to manage his jealousy. He feels pathologised.
**Application:** EFT would not pathologise the rage or try to manage it cognitively. The therapist would validate the rage as real while exploring what is underneath it. "The anger makes sense — something is happening that feels threatening. But I wonder what is underneath the anger. If we set the anger aside for just a moment — I know that is hard — what else is there?" Perhaps he accesses fear: "I am afraid she will find someone better." Or longing: "I just want to know that I am her person." These primary emotions change everything. The wife who was defending herself against his jealous anger can respond to his fear. The relational dynamic reorganises — not because the jealousy was resolved cognitively but because the emotional signal changed.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat all emotional expression as equally therapeutic.
**Why:** Venting secondary emotion — raging, complaining, listing grievances — does not produce change. It reinforces the cycle. The therapeutic task is to move beneath the secondary emotion to the primary attachment emotion.

**Don't:** Rush past emotion to insight.
**Why:** Understanding why you feel afraid is less transformative than actually feeling the fear in the presence of your partner and having them respond. EFT is an experiential model — the experience of the emotion, not the understanding of it, is what heals.
