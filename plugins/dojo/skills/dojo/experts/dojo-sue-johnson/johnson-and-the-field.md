# Johnson and the Field

## When to Use
- When someone wants a balanced assessment of EFT — its evidence, contributions, limitations, and place in the therapeutic landscape.
- When someone is deciding whether EFT is the right approach for them.
- When criticisms of EFT need to be engaged honestly.

## Core Concept
Emotionally Focused Therapy has become one of the most widely practised and researched couples therapy models in the world. Its trajectory from a clinical innovation in the 1980s to a global training and certification system reflects both the model's genuine power and the broader field's need for an empirically supported, humanistic approach to couple distress.

**Evidence base.** EFT has the strongest research base of any couples therapy model. Multiple randomised controlled trials have demonstrated effectiveness, with recovery rates of 70-75% and effect sizes significantly larger than those of behavioural couples therapy. Uniquely, EFT outcomes tend to hold or improve at follow-up — couples continue to get better after therapy ends. Process research has identified the specific change events (particularly the softening) that predict outcome. Neuroimaging research (particularly Coan's work) has provided biological evidence for the attachment mechanisms EFT targets. Studies have demonstrated effectiveness across diverse populations — heterosexual and same-sex couples, cross-cultural samples, couples dealing with chronic illness, depression, PTSD, and infidelity.

**Contributions.** Johnson's central contribution was integrating Bowlby's attachment theory with experiential therapy and systems theory to create a coherent, applicable clinical model. Before EFT, couples therapy was dominated by behavioural approaches that treated relationships as negotiation systems and focused on communication skills and conflict resolution. Johnson reframed relationship distress as an attachment problem — an emotional, not cognitive, phenomenon — and built a therapy that worked at the level where the distress actually operates. This was a paradigm shift in the field.

**Limitations and criticisms.** EFT's claim to the strongest evidence base, while defensible, has been contested — some researchers note that the comparison base is limited and that effect sizes vary across studies. The model has been criticised for its relative lack of attention to power dynamics, cultural context, and the structural inequalities that shape relationships. Its applicability to couples where one partner is abusive is debated — Johnson argues that attachment insecurity underlies most intimate partner violence, while critics argue that treating abusive dynamics through an attachment lens risks minimising the abuser's responsibility. The training and certification structure has been criticised as expensive and hierarchical. Some clinicians find the model's emotional intensity difficult to manage without extensive training.

**Johnson's place.** Johnson occupies a distinctive position as both a rigorous researcher and a passionate populariser. Her academic work meets the standards of empirical psychology; her popular books (Hold Me Tight, Love Sense) bring attachment science to general audiences with clarity and emotional power. Her intellectual allies include John Bowlby (posthumously), Les Greenberg (her early collaborator, now working independently on emotion-focused therapy for individuals), James Coan, and the broader attachment research community. Her relationship to John Gottman — the other dominant figure in relationship research — is collegial but conceptually distinct: Gottman is primarily a researcher who observes and predicts; Johnson is primarily a clinician who intervenes and transforms.

## How to Apply
1. **Evaluate EFT on its merits and limitations.** It is the most rigorously researched couples therapy model with demonstrated effectiveness across populations and presenting problems. It is not the only valid approach and its evidence base, while strong, is not beyond challenge.
2. **If considering EFT therapy, seek a certified therapist.** The depth of the model — particularly Stage 2 restructuring — requires competent training. The International Centre for Excellence in Emotionally Focused Therapy (ICEEFT) certifies therapists through a structured programme.
3. **Integrate EFT with other approaches where appropriate.** EFT can be combined with individual trauma therapy, somatic approaches, and psychoeducation. It does not demand exclusivity.

## Examples
**Situation:** A couples therapist trained in the Gottman Method has heard that EFT produces better outcomes but is sceptical of the heavy emphasis on emotion and vulnerability. He wants to know whether the evidence justifies retraining.
**Application:** An honest assessment would acknowledge that Gottman's research has produced invaluable observational data about what predicts relationship success and failure — the Four Horsemen, the 5:1 ratio, repair attempts. But Gottman's intervention model (the Sound Relationship House) has a thinner evidence base for producing change than EFT does. EFT's strength is precisely what makes the clinician uncomfortable — it goes to the emotional depth that behaviour-based approaches often skirt. The recommendation would be to learn EFT as a complement, not a replacement — Gottman's diagnostic framework is excellent for understanding the surface dynamics; EFT provides the tools for restructuring the emotional foundation underneath them.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present EFT as proven beyond challenge.
**Why:** The evidence is strong but not unassailable. Overclaiming invites justified scepticism and damages credibility.

**Don't:** Dismiss other couples therapy models as inferior.
**Why:** Gottman, Behavioral Couples Therapy, Integrative Behavioral Couples Therapy, and others have contributions and evidence of their own. EFT is arguably the strongest model but it is not the only valid one.
