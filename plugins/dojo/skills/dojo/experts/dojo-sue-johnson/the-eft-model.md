# The EFT Model

## When to Use
- When someone wants to understand what Emotionally Focused Therapy is and how it works.
- When someone is considering EFT and wants to know what they would experience.
- When the three-stage structure of EFT needs to be explained.

## Core Concept
Emotionally Focused Therapy is a short-term, structured approach to couples therapy developed by Sue Johnson and Les Greenberg in the 1980s. It integrates attachment theory (Bowlby), humanistic-experiential therapy, and systems theory into a model that treats relationship distress as fundamentally an attachment problem — a failure of the emotional bond between partners.

The model rests on three premises. First, adult romantic love is an attachment bond with the same biological logic as infant-caregiver attachment. Second, relationship distress arises when attachment needs are unmet and partners become trapped in rigid negative interaction cycles. Third, change occurs not through insight or skill-building alone but through new emotional experiences — moments where partners access their deeper attachment emotions and use them to reach for each other in new ways.

EFT proceeds through three stages and nine steps. Stage 1, De-escalation (Steps 1-4), focuses on identifying the negative cycle, accessing the attachment emotions underlying each partner's position, and reframing the problem as the cycle rather than the partner. Stage 2, Restructuring the Bond (Steps 5-7), is the heart of the model — here the withdrawn partner re-engages and the critical partner softens, creating new bonding events that restructure the attachment. Stage 3, Consolidation (Steps 8-9), integrates the changes, creates new narratives, and addresses practical problems from the platform of a more secure bond.

The typical course is 8-20 sessions, though complex cases may require more. EFT has the strongest evidence base of any couples therapy model, with recovery rates of 70-75% in clinical trials and effects that hold or increase at follow-up.

## How to Apply
1. **Understand the logic before the technique.** EFT is not a set of interventions — it is a way of seeing. The therapist tracks attachment emotions, follows the process, and creates safety for new experiences. Technique serves the process, not the other way around.
2. **Learn to see the cycle.** Every distressed couple has one. The surface content varies infinitely but the underlying patterns are remarkably consistent: pursue-withdraw, attack-attack, withdraw-withdraw.
3. **Follow the emotion.** EFT moves from secondary emotion (anger, numbness, irritation) to primary emotion (fear, sadness, longing). The primary emotions are the ones that reorganise the interaction.
4. **Trust the model's sequence.** De-escalation must precede restructuring. Pushing for vulnerability before the cycle is de-escalated risks harm.

## Examples
**Situation:** A couple has been in therapy for four months with a therapist who taught them communication skills. They can use "I-statements" in session but revert to fighting at home. They are frustrated and considering ending therapy.
**Application:** The communication skills failed because they addressed the surface without reaching the attachment level. EFT would begin by identifying their cycle — perhaps she criticises and he defends, then she escalates and he withdraws. The therapist would help them see that this pattern, not each other, is the enemy. Then the therapist would slow the process down and go underneath the surface anger and defensiveness to the attachment fears driving each partner's position — her terror that she does not matter to him, his terror that he will never be enough. When those emotions emerge and are expressed to each other in session, something shifts that no communication technique can produce.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Describe EFT as "learning to communicate better."
**Why:** EFT is not a communication model. It is an attachment model. The communication changes that result from EFT are by-products of a more secure bond, not the mechanism of change.

**Don't:** Treat the three stages as rigid sequential phases.
**Why:** While the overall arc moves from de-escalation to restructuring to consolidation, the process is iterative. Couples revisit earlier stages, particularly when attachment injuries surface or new stressors activate the old cycle.
