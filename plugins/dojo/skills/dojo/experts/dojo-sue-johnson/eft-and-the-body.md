# EFT and the Body

## When to Use
- When someone describes physical responses to relational distress.
- When the physiological dimension of attachment needs to be understood.
- When someone asks about what happens in the body during connection and disconnection.

## Core Concept
Attachment is not an abstract psychological construct — it is a biological reality with observable physiological correlates. Johnson draws on neuroscience to ground EFT's clinical insights in the body. The attachment system operates through the autonomic nervous system, the limbic system, and the neuroendocrine system. When the attachment bond is secure, the body is regulated — the parasympathetic nervous system predominates, cortisol levels are lower, oxytocin is released during contact, and the immune system functions optimally. When the attachment bond is threatened, the body goes into survival mode — the sympathetic nervous system activates, cortisol surges, the prefrontal cortex goes offline, and the person responds from the fight-flight-freeze circuits of the amygdala.

This is why relationship distress is not merely psychological — it is physiological. The pursuer's racing heart, clenched jaw, and elevated voice are not choices — they are the body's threat response to perceived abandonment. The withdrawer's numbing, flat affect, and avoidance of eye contact are not choices either — they are the body's shutdown response to perceived failure and overwhelm. EFT works because it addresses these physiological states, not just the cognitive or behavioural surface.

James Coan's Social Baseline Theory and his fMRI research with hand-holding provide compelling neuroimaging evidence for the physiological impact of attachment. When a person faces a mild threat (electric shock) while holding a stranger's hand, neural threat activity decreases modestly. When holding a spouse's hand, threat activity decreases significantly. When holding the hand of a securely attached spouse, threat activity drops to near-baseline. The secure attachment bond literally regulates the brain's threat response. This is not metaphor — it is neuroscience.

Johnson uses somatic awareness in clinical practice by tracking physiological cues in session — changes in breathing, voice quality, body posture, facial expression, tears, muscle tension — as signals of the underlying emotional process. When a client's voice drops and their breathing slows, something important has shifted. When a client's shoulders rise and their jaw clenches, the protective system has activated. The body tells the therapist what the client may not yet have words for.

## How to Apply
1. **Track the body in session.** Watch for somatic signals — shifts in posture, breathing, voice tone, facial expression. These are often the first indicators of emotional change.
2. **Name the body's experience.** When a client is in the grip of attachment distress, help them notice what is happening physically: "What is happening in your body right now? Where do you feel that?"
3. **Use touch as an attachment signal.** Physical contact — holding hands, placing a hand on a partner's arm — can activate the attachment system in a positive direction. In session, encouraging partners to reach physically as well as emotionally can deepen the restructuring.
4. **Normalise physiological reactivity.** Help clients understand that their physical responses to disconnection are not pathological — they are the body's alarm system responding to a genuine threat to the attachment bond.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man describes a familiar experience: when his wife begins to criticise him, he feels a sensation of tightness in his chest, his hearing seems to dull, and he experiences a strange sense of distance — as if he is watching the interaction from behind glass. He has been told he "stonewalls" and needs to stay present, but he says he cannot control it.
**Application:** EFT would recognise this as a physiological shutdown — the dorsal vagal response in polyvagal terms, the freeze response in attachment terms. His body is protecting him from emotional overwhelm by numbing and distancing. This is not a choice and telling him to "stay present" without addressing the underlying physiology is like telling someone whose hand is on a stove to stop flinching. The therapist would slow the process, help him notice the moment the shutdown begins — "Stay with me here. When she said that, I saw something happen. Your eyes changed. What happened inside you?" — and gradually help him remain in the emotional experience for slightly longer before the shutdown takes over. Over time, as the cycle de-escalates and his wife's approach becomes less threatening, his nervous system can tolerate more emotional contact without shutting down.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat somatic tracking as a technique to be added.
**Why:** In EFT, body awareness is integrated into the emotional process, not a separate intervention. The therapist tracks the body as part of following the emotion, not as a distinct exercise.

**Don't:** Overclaim the neuroscience.
**Why:** Coan's research and other neuroimaging studies are compelling but the field is young. Use the neuroscience to support and illustrate the clinical model, not as proof of claims that exceed the evidence.
