# Attachment Theory in Adults

## When to Use
- When someone wants to understand why adult romantic relationships carry such emotional intensity.
- When someone asks about attachment styles in the context of romantic love.
- When A.R.E. (Accessibility, Responsiveness, Engagement) needs to be explained.

## Core Concept
John Bowlby established that human beings are wired for attachment from birth — the infant's survival depends on maintaining proximity to a caregiver who provides safety, comfort, and a secure base for exploration. Sue Johnson's central contribution was recognising that adult romantic love follows the same logic. The romantic partner becomes the primary attachment figure — the person we turn to for safety, the person whose absence creates distress, the person whose responsiveness determines whether we feel secure enough to explore the world.

This is not metaphor. The same neurobiological systems that govern infant-caregiver bonding — oxytocin, vasopressin, the limbic system — govern adult romantic bonding. Separation from an attachment figure activates the same panic circuits regardless of whether the person is two years old or forty-two. The protest behaviours of an anxious partner — calling repeatedly, demanding reassurance, escalating conflict — are structurally identical to the protest behaviours of an infant whose caregiver has left the room.

Johnson identifies three key dimensions of secure attachment, summarised as A.R.E.: Accessibility — can I reach you? Responsiveness — can I rely on you to respond to my emotional needs? Engagement — do I know you value me and will stay close? When all three are present, the bond is secure. When any are disrupted, attachment distress follows — and the negative cycle begins.

Attachment styles — secure, anxious, avoidant, disorganised — are not personality types. They are relational strategies learned in early relationships and carried into adult bonds. They describe how a person manages attachment distress, not who they are. They can shift in either direction — a person with a secure history can become anxiously attached in a relationship with an unresponsive partner, and a person with an anxious history can earn security through a relationship with a consistently responsive one.

## How to Apply
1. **Normalise the intensity.** When someone is consumed by relationship distress, help them understand that the intensity is proportional to the biological significance of the bond. This is not overreaction — it is the attachment system doing exactly what it evolved to do.
2. **Assess A.R.E.** When evaluating a relationship, ask about accessibility, responsiveness, and engagement rather than communication skills or conflict frequency. These predict satisfaction better than any other variable.
3. **Reframe attachment needs as healthy, not pathological.** The cultural message that needing your partner is weakness or codependency is attachment-shaming. Correct it directly.
4. **Understand style as strategy, not destiny.** Attachment strategies are adaptations that made sense in the original context. They can be updated when the relational context changes.

## Examples
**Situation:** A woman in her thirties describes herself as "too needy" in relationships. She checks her phone constantly for texts from her partner, feels anxious when he is busy, and worries that she drives people away with her intensity.
**Application:** Johnson would reframe this immediately. The woman is not "too needy" — she is experiencing normal attachment anxiety in a context where her A.R.E. needs are not being reliably met. Her phone-checking is protest behaviour — a strategy to maintain connection with an attachment figure whose responsiveness is uncertain. The question is not "how do I need less?" — it is "what is happening in this relationship that makes the bond feel insecure?" Perhaps her partner is emotionally avoidant. Perhaps there have been attachment injuries. Perhaps her early attachment history sensitised her system to disconnection cues. All of these can be worked with. None of them require her to pathologise her fundamental need for connection.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use attachment styles as labels that define people.
**Why:** Saying "he's avoidant" as if it is a fixed trait misses the relational nature of attachment. He may be avoidant in this relationship because his reaching has been met with criticism, while he was secure in a previous relationship where his partner was responsive.

**Don't:** Treat independence as the goal of healthy attachment.
**Why:** The goal is not independence — it is secure dependence. From a platform of secure attachment, people explore more, take more risks, and function more autonomously. Independence without connection is not strength — it is often avoidant coping.
