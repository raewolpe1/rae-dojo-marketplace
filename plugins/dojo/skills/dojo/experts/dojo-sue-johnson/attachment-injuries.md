# Attachment Injuries

## When to Use
- When someone has experienced a betrayal, abandonment, or violation of trust in their relationship.
- When a couple is stuck because one partner cannot move past a specific incident.
- When the standard EFT process stalls because an unresolved injury keeps resurfacing.

## Core Concept
An attachment injury is a specific incident in which one partner fails the other at a moment of critical need — a moment when the attachment system is fully activated and the partner's response defines the safety of the bond. Johnson distinguishes attachment injuries from everyday disappointments or even significant conflicts. An attachment injury is a wound to the attachment bond itself. It answers the question "Can I count on you?" with a devastating "No."

Common attachment injuries include: a partner's emotional or physical absence during a medical crisis, miscarriage, or bereavement. Infidelity. Abandonment during a period of intense vulnerability. Siding with a third party — a parent, a friend, an ex — against the partner at a moment when loyalty was essential. The defining feature is not the severity of the act in objective terms but its meaning within the attachment bond. A partner who checks their phone during a conversation about a miscarriage may create an attachment injury as devastating as an affair — because the message received is the same: "You are not important enough for me to be present."

Attachment injuries have a distinctive clinical signature. They do not fade with time. They are replayed repeatedly, often with the same emotional intensity as the original event. They block the standard EFT process — the couple may de-escalate around most topics but cannot approach the injury without the cycle erupting at full intensity. The injured partner cycles between anger and grief. The injuring partner typically feels overwhelmed, defensive, or numb — they may have apologised many times but the apology never lands.

Johnson developed the Attachment Injury Resolution Model (AIRM) to address these wounds within EFT. The process involves: the injured partner expressing the pain of the injury and its impact on the attachment bond; the injuring partner acknowledging the injury and its impact without defensiveness; the injuring partner expressing genuine empathy, remorse, and accountability; the injured partner risking a reach for comfort; and the injuring partner responding with emotional presence and reassurance.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the injury.** Listen for the incident that keeps resurfacing. It is usually specific, dated, and carries an emotional charge disproportionate to its apparent severity.
2. **Validate the persistence of the wound.** The injured partner is not "holding a grudge" — the injury is unhealed because the attachment bond was damaged at its core. The wound persists because the relational safety has not been restored.
3. **Do not rush to forgiveness.** Premature forgiveness — forgiveness demanded or forgiveness offered before the injury has been fully processed — seals the wound over without healing it. It will resurface.
4. **Guide the injuring partner toward genuine accountability.** This is not an apology. It is a visceral acknowledgment of the other's pain: "I see what I did to you. I see how it changed everything. I am here now."

## Examples
**Situation:** A couple is stuck in therapy. They have de-escalated on most issues and even had some warm moments. But every few weeks, the wife returns to the time her husband did not come to the hospital when she was diagnosed with breast cancer — he had a work deadline and said he would come "as soon as he could." He came six hours later. This was three years ago. He has apologised dozens of times. She cannot let it go.
**Application:** This is an attachment injury. She was at maximum vulnerability — terrified, alone, facing a life-threatening diagnosis — and he was not there. His absence answered her most fundamental attachment question with "No, you cannot count on me." His subsequent apologies do not heal the injury because they are cognitive — "I know I should have been there" — not emotional. He has not fully entered her experience of that moment. The AIRM process would involve helping him access not his guilt but his grief — grief at having failed her, at having been the kind of person who chose a deadline over his dying wife. When he can sit with that grief and express it to her, not as a defence but as genuine anguish — "I cannot believe I did that to you. I left you alone at the worst moment of your life. I am so sorry" — and when she can see his pain as real, she can risk reaching for him. And if he responds — holds her, stays present, weeps with her — the injury begins to heal.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the injury as a negotiating position.
**Why:** The injured partner is not wielding the injury as a weapon — they are trapped in it. Treating it as a tactic dismisses the genuine attachment wound and reinforces the cycle.

**Don't:** Equalise the injury.
**Why:** "Well, you hurt me too" is the injuring partner's protective response, but it blocks healing. Attachment injuries have a directionality — one partner failed the other at a specific moment of need. That must be addressed before other grievances can be explored.
