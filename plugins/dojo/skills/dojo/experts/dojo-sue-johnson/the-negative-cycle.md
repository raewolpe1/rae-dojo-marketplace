# The Negative Cycle

## When to Use
- When someone describes a repetitive fight pattern in their relationship.
- When the pursue-withdraw dynamic needs to be explained.
- When someone is trying to understand why they keep having the same argument about different topics.

## Core Concept
The negative cycle is the central concept in EFT's understanding of relationship distress. It is the rigid, self-reinforcing interaction pattern that couples fall into when their attachment bond is insecure. The cycle is not caused by the topic of the argument — it is caused by the attachment insecurity underneath. The topic changes; the cycle remains the same.

The most common pattern is pursue-withdraw (or demand-withdraw). One partner, typically the more anxiously attached, responds to disconnection by pursuing — criticising, complaining, demanding, escalating. The other partner, typically the more avoidantly attached, responds to the pursuit by withdrawing — shutting down, going silent, leaving the room, numbing out. Each partner's strategy is a logical response to their own attachment distress, but it activates the other partner's deepest fear. The pursuer's escalation confirms the withdrawer's belief that they cannot get it right. The withdrawer's retreat confirms the pursuer's belief that they do not matter. The cycle feeds itself.

Other patterns exist. Attack-attack (also called mutual blame or mutual pursuit) involves both partners escalating simultaneously, each matching the other's intensity. Withdraw-withdraw (also called mutual avoidance or frozen distance) involves both partners retreating into numbness — the surface is calm but the emotional connection is dead. Johnson considers withdraw-withdraw the most dangerous pattern because it signals that both partners have given up on reaching the other.

The cycle has three layers. The surface layer is the observable behaviour — the criticism, the stonewalling, the door-slamming. The middle layer is the secondary emotion — the anger, the frustration, the irritation that both partners feel and express. The deepest layer is the primary attachment emotion — the fear, the loneliness, the longing, the shame that neither partner typically shows. EFT works by moving through these layers from surface to depth.

## How to Apply
1. **Map the cycle explicitly.** Ask: when you fight, who typically starts? What does that look like? What does the other person do in response? What happens next? Draw it out — a literal loop — so both partners can see it.
2. **Externalise it.** Name the cycle as something that happens to both of them, not something either of them does to the other. "The cycle has taken over again." "There it is — your old dance."
3. **Identify each partner's position.** Who pursues? Who withdraws? These positions can shift — the same person may pursue in one domain and withdraw in another — but there is usually a dominant pattern.
4. **Connect the surface to the depth.** When the pursuer criticises, what is the fear underneath? When the withdrawer goes silent, what are they protecting against? The cycle only begins to dissolve when the deeper layer is visible.

## Examples
**Situation:** A couple reports that they fight about everything — housework, finances, parenting, time together, time apart. They cannot identify a single issue that, if resolved, would fix the relationship. They feel hopeless.
**Application:** Johnson would say the content does not matter because it was never the real problem. The real problem is the cycle. The therapist would ask them to describe what happened during their most recent fight — not the topic but the sequence. She said something critical. He defended himself. She escalated. He went quiet. She followed him into the next room. He said "I need space." She said "You always do this." He left the house. She called him four times. He did not pick up. This is the cycle. It has nothing to do with whether the dishwasher was loaded correctly. It has everything to do with whether she can reach him and whether he can ever feel safe enough to stay. Naming this — "your fight about the dishwasher was actually about whether you can count on each other" — is the first step toward de-escalation.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assign blame for the cycle.
**Why:** The pursuer is not "too much" and the withdrawer is not "too cold." Both are responding to attachment distress with the strategies they learned. Blaming either partner reinforces the cycle.

**Don't:** Try to fix the cycle by addressing the content.
**Why:** Resolving the dishwasher argument does not fix the attachment insecurity. The same cycle will replay around the next topic within days. The cycle itself must be addressed.
