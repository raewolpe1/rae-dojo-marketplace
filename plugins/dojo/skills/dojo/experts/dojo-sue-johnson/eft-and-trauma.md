# EFT and Trauma

## When to Use
- When individual trauma is showing up in a couple's interaction.
- When PTSD, complex trauma, or developmental trauma is complicating the attachment bond.
- When a therapist or couple asks how EFT addresses trauma.

## Core Concept
Trauma and attachment are deeply intertwined. Trauma disrupts the capacity for secure bonding — it teaches the nervous system that proximity to others is dangerous, that vulnerability leads to harm, that emotional needs will be exploited or ignored. When a trauma survivor enters a romantic relationship, their attachment system is simultaneously activated (the partner is the attachment figure, the source of safety) and dysregulated (intimacy triggers trauma responses because closeness has been associated with danger).

Johnson argues that EFT is particularly suited to working with trauma in relational context because the model operates at the intersection of attachment and emotion — precisely the territory that trauma disrupts. The EFT approach to trauma in couples therapy involves several key adaptations. First, the therapist creates extraordinary safety — more than typical de-escalation requires. The traumatised partner's protective system is highly sensitised, and the therapeutic pace must respect this. Second, the therapist helps the non-traumatised partner understand the trauma responses — the hypervigilance, the emotional flooding, the dissociation, the avoidance of intimacy — as adaptive survival strategies rather than personal rejection. Third, the therapist works with the traumatised partner to gradually access the attachment emotions underneath the trauma responses — the longing for safety that has been buried under layers of protection.

Research by Johnson and her colleagues, including Susan Johnson and Scott Woolley, has demonstrated that EFT can reduce PTSD symptoms in traumatised individuals through couples therapy — not just improve the relationship but actually reduce the trauma response. This makes neurobiological sense: if the romantic partner can become a reliable source of safety, the nervous system recalibrates. Secure attachment provides the antidote to the isolation and helplessness that maintain traumatic responses.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess for trauma before beginning EFT.** Trauma may not present directly — it shows up as avoidance of intimacy, explosive reactions to minor triggers, dissociation during conflict, or an inability to access vulnerable emotions.
2. **Psychoeducate the non-traumatised partner.** Help them understand that the trauma responses are not about them — the withdrawal, the startle, the emotional shutdown are the nervous system's protective strategies. This reduces the partner's personalisation and increases their capacity to be a safe haven.
3. **Slow the pace significantly.** Traumatised partners need more time in de-escalation. Pushing for vulnerability before safety is established risks retraumatisation.
4. **Work with the body.** Trauma lives in the nervous system. The therapist must track physiological signs of dysregulation — changes in breathing, muscle tension, voice quality, eye contact — and titrate the emotional intensity accordingly.

## Examples
**Situation:** A couple in their forties. She has a history of childhood sexual abuse. He is loving and committed but increasingly frustrated because she withdraws from physical intimacy. She feels guilty and broken. He feels rejected and helpless. Their cycle: he initiates, she freezes, he becomes hurt and withdraws, she feels she has failed again.
**Application:** EFT would work with this couple by first externalising the cycle and naming the trauma's role in it. Her freeze is not rejection — it is a trauma response activated by the vulnerability of physical intimacy. His hurt withdrawal is not selfishness — it is an attachment protest in response to her unavailability. The therapist would help him understand that her body is protecting her, not rejecting him. She would be helped to distinguish between present safety and past danger — "He is not the person who hurt you. Your body is responding to an old threat." Gradually, with sufficient safety and at the pace her nervous system can tolerate, she could begin to access the longing underneath the freeze — "I want to be close to you. I want to want you. My body just... shuts down." If he can receive this without taking it personally — "I am here. We will go at whatever pace you need" — the relationship becomes a healing context rather than a retraumatising one.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Force the pace with traumatised couples.
**Why:** Pushing for emotional or physical intimacy before the traumatised partner's nervous system is ready is retraumatising. Safety must be built incrementally.

**Don't:** Replace individual trauma therapy with couples therapy.
**Why:** Some trauma presentations require individual work — particularly severe dissociative responses or trauma that the person is not yet ready to share with their partner. EFT can be the relational container but may need to work alongside individual therapy.
