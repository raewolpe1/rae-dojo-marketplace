# Consolidation

## When to Use
- When someone asks what happens after the core change events in EFT.
- When a couple has experienced restructuring and needs to integrate the changes.
- When the question is about sustaining gains after therapy ends.

## Core Concept
Stage 3 of EFT — Consolidation — comprises Steps 8 and 9. It is the stage where the new bond is tested, integrated, and applied to the practical realities of the couple's life. If Stage 2 is the moment of transformation, Stage 3 is the embedding of that transformation into daily life.

Step 8 involves facilitating the emergence of new solutions to old practical problems. The critical insight is that problems that were intractable during attachment insecurity often resolve naturally once the bond is secure. The couple who fought endlessly about finances was not really fighting about money — they were fighting about whether they could count on each other. Once the attachment bond is reorganised, the financial conversation changes entirely. The partners can negotiate from a position of trust and collaboration rather than from protective positions of demand and withdrawal.

Step 9 involves consolidating the new positions and the new cycle — creating a narrative of the journey from distress to security, reinforcing the new patterns of interaction, and preparing the couple to recognise and interrupt the old cycle when it inevitably resurfaces under stress. The old cycle does not disappear. It remains a groove in the relational system. But the couple now has a new pattern that is stronger, and they have the awareness and the emotional skills to recognise when they are slipping back and to choose the new pattern instead.

Consolidation also includes anticipatory work — identifying the situations most likely to trigger the old cycle and rehearsing, in session, how the couple will handle them differently. Johnson emphasises that this is not cognitive planning but emotional preparation — the couple practises turning to each other rather than against each other in the face of stress.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the new story.** Help the couple articulate their journey: where they started, what they discovered, what shifted, and where they are now. The narrative consolidates the emotional learning.
2. **Return to previously intractable problems.** Test the new bond against the old conflicts. Can they now discuss finances, parenting, in-laws, or sex from a different position? If so, the restructuring has generalised.
3. **Anticipate regression.** Normalise it. The old cycle will return under stress — illness, job loss, transition, grief. The difference is that the couple now recognises it and has the capacity to interrupt it.
4. **Plan for maintenance.** Some couples benefit from booster sessions. All couples benefit from ongoing relational practice — the daily habits of connection that maintain a secure bond.

## Examples
**Situation:** A couple has been through eighteen sessions. The withdrawer re-engaged. The pursuer softened. They feel closer than they have in years. Now they need to address the practical reality that his job requires significant travel and she has been carrying the household and parenting alone. In earlier therapy, this would have triggered the cycle — she would pursue with resentment, he would withdraw with guilt.
**Application:** In consolidation, the therapist would invite them to discuss the travel issue from their new relational position. The conversation looks entirely different. He can say, "I know the travel is hard for you and I feel guilty about it — I want to figure this out together" instead of defending or withdrawing. She can say, "I miss you when you are gone and sometimes I feel overwhelmed" instead of criticising or listing grievances. They can problem-solve — perhaps he reduces travel, perhaps they hire help, perhaps they build in reconnection rituals — because the attachment question has been answered. She knows he cares. He knows he is enough. The practical problem becomes manageable because the emotional problem has been resolved.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat consolidation as merely administrative.
**Why:** Stage 3 is active therapeutic work. The new bond needs to be tested against real-life stressors, and the couple needs to build the narrative and practices that sustain the change.

**Don't:** Assume the old cycle is permanently gone.
**Why:** The old pattern is not eliminated — it is superseded. Under sufficient stress, it will resurface. The couple needs to expect this and know how to respond when it happens.
