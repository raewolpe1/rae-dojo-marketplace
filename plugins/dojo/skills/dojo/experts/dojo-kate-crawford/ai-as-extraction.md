# AI as Extraction

## When to Use

- When a conversation treats AI as disembodied software and needs grounding in material reality
- When reviewing strategy documents, proposals, or policies that frame AI purely as innovation opportunity
- When introducing the critical-AI framework to an audience unfamiliar with Crawford's work

## Core Concept

The central argument of Kate Crawford's work is deceptively simple: artificial intelligence is neither artificial nor intelligent. It is not artificial because it is made entirely from natural materials — lithium, cobalt, copper, rare earths, silicon, water, and fossil-fuel energy — extracted from the earth through industrial processes with profound environmental and human costs. It is not intelligent because what passes for intelligence is the product of massive human labour: the workers who mine the minerals, assemble the hardware, label the training data, moderate the outputs, and maintain the infrastructure.

Crawford reframes AI as an extractive industry. Like oil, like mining, like industrial agriculture, AI takes resources from some places and people and concentrates value in others. The lithium comes from the Atacama; the cobalt comes from the DRC; the labelled data comes from workers in Nairobi and Manila; the electricity comes from power grids everywhere. The value accrues to a handful of corporations headquartered in a few American cities. This is not a side-effect of AI — it is the structure of AI.

For development practitioners, this reframing is consequential. When a government "adopts AI," it is not simply installing software. It is plugging into a global extractive infrastructure with specific material dependencies, labour relations, and power asymmetries. An AI-enabled social protection system is not just an algorithm — it is a supply chain that begins in a mine and ends in a classification decision that determines whether a family receives assistance. The extraction lens forces the question that innovation narratives suppress: who pays the material cost of this system, and where are they located relative to those who benefit?

## How to Apply

1. **Start with the material question.** Before analysing any AI system, ask: "What is this made of?" Trace the minerals, energy, water, and labour required. As Crawford insists: "AI is not an abstraction — it is an industry with a supply chain, and that supply chain has a geography."

2. **Name the extraction pattern.** Identify the flow of resources: from where to where, from whom to whom. Map the geographic distance between cost-bearing and value-capture. In development contexts, this often maps onto familiar centre-periphery dynamics — which is the point.

3. **Audit the innovation narrative.** When a document says "AI-powered" or "AI-enabled," substitute "extraction-dependent" and see what changes. This is not rhetorical sabotage — it is analytical hygiene. The question is whether the document can survive the substitution or whether it depends on the abstraction for its persuasiveness.

4. **Connect to longer histories.** AI extraction is not novel. It is the latest form of patterns that include colonial resource seizure, enclosure of commons, and labour exploitation. Locating AI within these longer histories prevents the error of treating it as uniquely responsible for structural problems it inherits and extends.

## Examples

**Situation:** A bilateral development agency publishes a strategy titled "AI for Sustainable Development." The document frames AI as a tool for achieving SDGs — climate monitoring, disease prediction, agricultural optimisation — and allocates $200 million over five years.

**Application:** Crawford's extraction lens reveals what the strategy omits. The "AI for climate" programme requires compute infrastructure whose carbon footprint may offset the monitoring gains. The disease-prediction models require training data labelled by low-wage workers whose labour conditions contradict SDG 8 (Decent Work). The agricultural optimisation platform runs on hardware whose mineral supply chain intersects with SDG 15 (Life on Land) violations in mining regions. None of these costs appear in the strategy's theory of change.

The critical move is not to reject the strategy but to complete it — to insist that the material costs of AI infrastructure are budgeted alongside the projected benefits. A strategy that claims AI will advance sustainable development while ignoring the unsustainable extraction that AI requires is not a strategy. It is a marketing document. Crawford's framework provides the audit methodology: trace the supply chain, name the costs, locate them geographically, and ask whether the projected benefits justify them and for whom.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Don't treat extraction as metaphor.** When Crawford says AI is extractive, she means it literally — minerals are removed from the earth, water is consumed, labour is exploited. Using "extraction" as a loose metaphor for any power asymmetry dilutes the argument's force. Stay material.

- **Don't make AI the cause of extraction.** Extraction predates AI by centuries. Presenting AI as the origin of resource seizure, labour exploitation, or environmental destruction is historically illiterate and analytically unhelpful. AI extends and accelerates existing patterns — that is the claim, and it is powerful enough without inflation.
