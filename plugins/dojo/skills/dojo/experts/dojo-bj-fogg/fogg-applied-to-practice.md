# Fogg Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When the user wants actionable takeaways from Fogg's work, not just theory.
- When designing behaviour change interventions — personal, team, or programme level.
- When the question is "what should I actually do differently?"

## Core Concept
Fogg's practical toolkit comprises six elements that work as a system.

First, B=MAP as a diagnostic. Before designing any behaviour change intervention, diagnose which element is missing or weak: motivation, ability, or prompt. The diagnosis determines the intervention. Most practitioners default to motivation (more training, more awareness, more inspiration) when the problem is ability or prompts.

Second, behaviour crafting as a selection method. Do not default to the obvious behaviour. Generate a swarm of possible behaviours that could serve the aspiration, evaluate them on effectiveness and ease, and select the golden behaviours — those that are both impactful and achievable.

Third, tiny habits as the implementation method. Shrink the selected behaviour to its smallest viable version. Anchor it to an existing reliable routine. Write the habit recipe: "After I [anchor], I will [tiny behaviour]." Celebrate immediately.

Fourth, ability chains as a design tool. When a behaviour is too hard, identify the specific ability bottleneck — time, money, physical effort, cognitive effort, or routine fit — and address that specific factor rather than trying to boost motivation.

Fifth, prompt design as an architecture tool. Embed prompts in existing workflows, physical environments, and shared infrastructure. Prefer action prompts (anchored to existing behaviours) over context prompts (external reminders) over person prompts (internal memory).

Sixth, celebration as a wiring mechanism. The positive emotion immediately after the behaviour is what converts deliberate action into automatic habit. It is not optional, not decorative — it is the mechanism.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose first.** "Run B=MAP. Which element is missing? Do not skip this step — it prevents wasting effort on the wrong intervention."
2. **Select the right behaviour.** "Generate options. Evaluate on effectiveness and ease. Choose golden behaviours."
3. **Shrink it.** "Whatever you chose, make it tiny. Thirty seconds or less. Ridiculously small."
4. **Anchor it.** "Find the reliable existing routine. Write the recipe."
5. **Celebrate.** "Immediately after. Every time. Not optional."
6. **Let it grow.** "Do not prescribe escalation. Trust the natural expansion."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme director asks "We are launching a new community health programme. How do I use behaviour design to improve adoption of the health practices we are promoting?"
**Application:** Fogg would say: "Six steps, applied to your programme. First, run B=MAP on each health practice you are promoting. For each one, ask: do community members want to do this (motivation)? Can they do it given their resources and context (ability)? Is there a reliable cue in their daily life to trigger it (prompt)? This diagnosis will likely reveal that most of your practices have adequate motivation — people want to be healthy — but face ability barriers or lack prompts. Second, for each practice, generate a swarm of behaviours. Do not assume the textbook version is the right one. How could this practice be done differently in this specific context? What is the easiest version that still achieves the health benefit? Third, shrink each behaviour to its tiniest version. Not 'wash hands with soap for twenty seconds before every meal' but 'rinse hands with water before the first meal of the day.' Start there. Fourth, find anchor moments in the community's daily routines. What do they already do reliably? Cooking the first meal, fetching water, arriving at the market. These are your prompts. Fifth, design for celebration. How can the person feel good immediately after performing the behaviour? Community health workers who praise the behaviour — genuinely, not patronisingly — provide social celebration. Sixth, let growth happen. Once the tiny version is established, people will naturally expand it. Your programme's job is to plant seeds, not demand full trees."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Apply all six elements simultaneously to twenty different behaviours.
**Why:** Even with a systematic toolkit, behaviour design requires focus. Start with one or two priority behaviours, design well, establish them, then expand.

**Don't:** Skip the diagnostic and jump to solutions.
**Why:** Without B=MAP diagnosis, you will default to motivation-based interventions — which are the least reliable. The diagnosis is the step that makes everything else effective.
