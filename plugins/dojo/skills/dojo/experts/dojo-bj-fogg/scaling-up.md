# Scaling Up

## When to Use
- When a tiny habit is established and the person wants to expand it.
- When someone is sceptical that starting tiny can produce meaningful results.
- When the natural growth trajectory needs explaining or supporting.

## Core Concept
Scaling in the Tiny Habits framework is not a separate phase — it is a natural consequence of habit formation. Once a tiny behaviour is reliably happening and wired through celebration, it tends to grow on its own. The person who starts with one sentence finds themselves writing a paragraph. The person who starts with two pushups finds themselves doing ten. This growth is organic, not prescribed.

Fogg is explicit: do not set growth targets. Do not say "this week one sentence, next week two sentences, the week after a paragraph." Prescribed escalation reintroduces the motivation dependency that the tiny approach was designed to eliminate. Instead, let the person expand naturally as the behaviour feels increasingly easy and automatic. Some days they will do the minimum; some days they will do more. Both are fine. The minimum maintains the habit; the expansion builds the practice.

There are two growth patterns. The first is organic expansion — the behaviour simply gets larger because the person feels like doing more. The second is habit multiplication — the person adds new tiny habits anchored to the established one, creating a chain or "recipe cluster" that grows the overall practice without any single behaviour becoming burdensome.

The key insight is that consistency matters more than intensity. A person who writes one sentence every day for a year has written 365 sentences — roughly a chapter. A person who writes a page on Monday and nothing for the rest of the week has written 52 pages but has no habit. The tiny daily practice produces more output and — critically — produces the automaticity that makes the practice sustainable indefinitely.

## How to Apply
1. **Do not prescribe growth.** "Let the behaviour expand naturally. On days you do the minimum, that is fine — you maintained the habit."
2. **Celebrate the minimum, not the expansion.** "The celebration is for doing the behaviour, not for doing more. This prevents the minimum from feeling like failure."
3. **Add new tiny habits rather than inflating existing ones.** "Instead of making one behaviour larger, add a second tiny behaviour anchored to the first."
4. **Trust the process.** "The tiny version feels inadequate at first. After a month, you will see that consistency has produced more than sporadic ambition ever did."

## Examples
**Situation:** A junior consultant started a tiny habit of writing one learning from each project in a notebook. After three weeks, it is automatic. She asks "How do I turn this into a proper learning journal?"
**Application:** Fogg would say: "You already have. The mistake would be to impose a structure — 'now write a full page with three sections.' That turns an automatic behaviour back into a demanding one. Instead, let it grow naturally. Some days you will write one line. Some days a paragraph. Some days you will sketch a diagram. Do not prescribe the format — let your curiosity guide the expansion. What you can do is add a second tiny habit. After you write your one learning, flip to the front of the notebook and add one keyword to a running index. That tiny addition — one word — makes the journal searchable, which makes it useful, which increases your motivation to write in it. Now you have two tiny habits that together create a functional learning system: a daily entry and a running index. Neither takes more than a minute. But in six months you will have a searchable knowledge base built entirely from tiny daily actions."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Set escalation schedules.
**Why:** "Week 1: one sentence. Week 2: two sentences. Week 3: a paragraph." This reintroduces the motivation dependency. If the person has a bad week and cannot manage the prescribed escalation, they feel like they have failed — and failure feelings erode habits.

**Don't:** Dismiss the minimum as insufficient.
**Why:** On low-energy days, doing the tiny version is a victory, not a failure. The minimum maintains the neural pathway. Treating the minimum as inadequate converts a success into a disappointment.
