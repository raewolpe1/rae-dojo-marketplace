# Fogg and Persuasive Technology

## When to Use
- When understanding Fogg's intellectual history — his work preceded Tiny Habits.
- When examining how technology platforms use behavioural principles to shape user behaviour.
- When the connection between tech design and everyday habit formation needs exploring.

## Core Concept
Before Tiny Habits, Fogg was known for founding the field of "captology" — the study of computers as persuasive technologies. His 2003 book *Persuasive Technology* and his Stanford courses trained a generation of Silicon Valley designers in applying behavioural principles to technology products. Several of his students went on to build features at companies like Instagram, Facebook, and Google — features explicitly designed to make products more engaging, more habit-forming, and harder to stop using.

The connection to B=MAP is direct. Social media notifications are prompts. Infinite scroll reduces the effort (ability) required to continue consuming. Variable rewards exploit motivation. The same model that helps an individual build a healthy tiny habit can be — and has been — used to design addictive technology.

Fogg's contribution to persuasive technology rests on a key insight: technology can be designed to change behaviour through the same mechanisms that operate in face-to-face persuasion, but at scale and with persistence that no human persuader could match. A phone notification fires a prompt every time. An app can be redesigned to reduce friction (ability barriers) to near zero. The technology never gets tired of prompting.

This intellectual history matters for two reasons. First, it demonstrates the power of B=MAP — if the model can drive billions of daily behaviours through tech products, it is empirically validated at scale. Second, it raises the ethical questions that Fogg has increasingly engaged with: if you can design behaviour, who decides what behaviour to design, and for whose benefit?

Fogg has publicly distanced himself from the manipulative applications of his work, stating that behaviour design should serve the person, not the designer. His pivot from persuasive technology to Tiny Habits can be read as a turn toward empowering individuals to design their own behaviour rather than having their behaviour designed for them by platforms.

## How to Apply
1. **Understand the model's power.** "B=MAP works at scale — technology proves this. The same principles that make apps addictive can make healthy habits automatic."
2. **Recognise design when you see it.** "Every notification, every default setting, every ease-of-use improvement is behaviour design. Understanding the model helps you see when you are being designed for."
3. **Apply tech lessons to non-tech contexts.** "Reduce friction, embed prompts in the workflow, make the desired action the default — these principles work in programme design, not just app design."
4. **Engage the ethics.** "The power to design behaviour creates the responsibility to design it for the person's benefit, not against their interests."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme designer asks "Can we use these same techniques in our mobile health app to increase medication adherence?"
**Application:** Fogg would say: "Yes, and this is exactly the kind of application I want to see. Apply B=MAP systematically. Prompt: a notification timed to the person's actual medication schedule, not a generic daily reminder. Better still, anchor it to something they already do — 'this notification appears when you finish logging your morning meal, because we know you eat breakfast every day at about this time.' Ability: make logging the medication a single tap, not a multi-screen process. Pre-populate the medication name, the dose, the time. One tap to confirm. Celebration: after the tap, show a brief positive message — a streak counter, a green checkmark, a gentle acknowledgement. That positive feedback is the celebration mechanism. The difference between this and manipulative tech is the direction of benefit. Social media uses these techniques to keep you scrolling for the platform's benefit. Your app uses them to help someone take their medication for their own benefit. Same model, different ethics."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present persuasive technology as inherently good or bad.
**Why:** The model is a tool. Notifications can remind someone to take medication or can exploit someone's attention for ad revenue. The ethics depend on whose interests are being served.

**Don't:** Disconnect Fogg's tech work from his habit work.
**Why:** They share the same theoretical foundation — B=MAP. Understanding one illuminates the other. The tech work demonstrates the model's power; the habit work demonstrates its personal application.
