# Behaviour Design for Teams

## When to Use
- When applying B=MAP to team or organisational behaviour change.
- When a team process is not being adopted despite training and good intentions.
- When designing workflows, systems, or routines for group adoption.

## Core Concept
Behaviour design for teams applies the same B=MAP framework but introduces additional complexity: multiple people with different motivation levels, ability profiles, and daily routines. The anchor moments that work for one team member may not exist for another. The ability bottleneck for one person may be irrelevant to another. Group behaviour design must account for this variation.

Fogg's approach to group behaviour is to design the environment and system rather than trying to change individual people. This means: make the desired behaviour the path of least resistance, embed prompts in shared workflows rather than relying on individual memory, and remove ability barriers at the system level rather than asking individuals to overcome them.

Three principles govern team behaviour design. First, design for the least motivated, least able member — not the most. If the system works for the person with the lowest motivation and ability, it works for everyone. Designing for the enthusiastic early adopter produces a system that fails for the majority. Second, embed prompts in shared infrastructure — meeting agendas, project management tools, physical spaces, workflow sequences. Individual prompts produce inconsistent adoption; shared infrastructure produces consistent adoption. Third, make the desired behaviour easier than the alternative. If updating the tracker is harder than not updating it, people will not update. If the tracker auto-populates from existing data and requires only a confirmation click, they will.

The ethics dimension is important when designing behaviour for groups. When an individual designs their own tiny habits, autonomy is preserved. When a manager designs behaviour for a team, the power dynamic changes — behaviour design becomes behaviour management. Fogg's principle is that good behaviour design serves the person's own goals, not just the designer's goals. In team contexts, this means the designed behaviour should be visibly useful to the people performing it, not just to management.

## How to Apply
1. **Run B=MAP for the team, not the individual.** "What is the group's motivation? What are the ability barriers across the team? Where are the shared prompts?"
2. **Design for the least able member.** "If the least experienced, least motivated person can do it, everyone can."
3. **Embed prompts in shared infrastructure.** "Put the cue in the meeting agenda, the project tool, the physical space — not in individual calendars."
4. **Make it useful to the performer.** "If the behaviour only serves management, adoption will be grudging and temporary."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consulting team needs to capture lessons learned from every project, but the current process — filling out a template after project close — has near-zero compliance.
**Application:** Fogg would say: "Run B=MAP on your current design. Motivation: at project close, the team is exhausted and already focused on the next project. Motivation is at its lowest point. Ability: a full lessons-learned template requires thirty to sixty minutes of reflective writing. That is a high-ability-demand behaviour at the worst possible moment. Prompt: 'after the project closes' is a vague prompt — when exactly? What is the anchor? Redesign all three elements. Motivation: move the capture earlier — during the project, not after. People are more motivated to capture insights while the work is fresh and they are still engaged. Ability: shrink the behaviour dramatically. Replace the template with one question asked at the end of every weekly team meeting: 'What is one thing we learned this week that we should not forget?' One sentence per person, captured in the meeting notes. That is thirty seconds per person. Prompt: embed it in the meeting agenda as the last item. The agenda is the shared infrastructure that ensures the prompt fires every week. Now you have a tiny behaviour — one sentence — anchored to a reliable shared event — the weekly meeting — at a moment when motivation is adequate — during the project, not after. After six months of weekly one-sentence captures, you have twenty-four lessons per project. That is more than you ever got from the post-project template."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Design team behaviours that only serve management.
**Why:** People resist behaviours that extract value from them without returning value. If the data collection only serves reporting upward, compliance will be minimal and grudging.

**Don't:** Assume individual habit techniques scale directly to groups.
**Why:** Groups have variation in motivation, ability, and routines. A system designed for the enthusiastic team member fails for the sceptical one. Design for the full range.
