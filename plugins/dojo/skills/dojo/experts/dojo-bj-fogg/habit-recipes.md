# Habit Recipes

## When to Use
- When formalising a tiny habit into the precise "After I [anchor], I will [tiny behaviour]" format.
- When someone needs help writing specific, actionable habit plans.
- When designing a set of related habits that build together.

## Core Concept
The habit recipe is Fogg's implementation format — the precise specification that translates a vague intention into an actionable design. The format is: "After I [anchor moment], I will [tiny behaviour]." This is followed immediately by celebration.

The recipe format works because it addresses the two most common failure points in behaviour change: vagueness and lack of prompts. "I will exercise more" is an intention, not a design. "After I pour my morning coffee, I will do two squats" is a design. The anchor provides the prompt; the tiny behaviour provides the action; the specificity eliminates ambiguity about when, where, and what.

Good recipes have four properties. The anchor is something the person already does reliably — not occasionally, not usually, but every day without fail. The behaviour is genuinely tiny — thirty seconds or less, requiring minimal motivation. The connection between anchor and behaviour is logical — they occur in the same location and the transition is smooth. And the whole recipe fits naturally into the person's existing day rather than requiring restructuring.

Recipes can be chained — one tiny habit anchored to the completion of another. After I pour my coffee, I will write one sentence. After I write one sentence, I will take a deep breath. This creates "habit stacks" — sequences of tiny behaviours that flow together. But chains should be built gradually, one link at a time, not designed as a complete sequence from the start.

## How to Apply
1. **Write the recipe precisely.** "After I [specific anchor], I will [specific tiny behaviour]. No vagueness — name the exact moment and the exact action."
2. **Test the anchor.** "Do you do this every day? At roughly the same time? Without thinking about it? If not, find a more reliable anchor."
3. **Test the behaviour for tininess.** "Can you do this in thirty seconds? On your worst day? If not, shrink it."
4. **Add the celebration.** "What will you do immediately after to generate positive emotion? Name it."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme manager wants to build three habits: daily planning, stakeholder tracking, and reflection.
**Application:** Fogg would say: "Three habits is ambitious for the start. Begin with one, establish it, then add the next. But let us write all three recipes so you have them ready. First, daily planning: 'After I sit down at my desk and open my laptop, I will write one priority for today on a sticky note.' That is your anchor — opening the laptop — and your tiny behaviour — one priority, one sticky note. Celebrate: stick it on the monitor and feel the clarity. Second, stakeholder tracking — do not add this until the first habit is established, probably two weeks: 'After I finish my first coffee of the morning, I will open the stakeholder register and update one entry.' Anchor: finishing coffee. Tiny: one entry. Third, reflection — add this last: 'After I close my laptop at the end of the day, I will write one sentence about what I noticed today.' Anchor: closing the laptop. Tiny: one sentence. The sequence matters. Do not start all three simultaneously — you will be splitting attention and none will wire properly. One at a time, each building on the stability of the last."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Write vague recipes.
**Why:** "I will be more mindful in meetings" is not a recipe. "After I sit down for a meeting, I will take one deep breath before speaking" is a recipe. Vagueness defeats the purpose of the format.

**Don't:** Stack multiple new habits simultaneously.
**Why:** Each habit needs individual attention to wire properly. Starting three or four at once dilutes the celebration and makes it harder to identify what is working and what is not.
