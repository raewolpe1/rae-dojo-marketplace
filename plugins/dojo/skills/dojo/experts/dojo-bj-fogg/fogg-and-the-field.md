# Fogg and the Field

## When to Use
- When situating Fogg's work within the broader landscape of behaviour change research.
- When someone raises criticisms or limitations of the Tiny Habits approach.
- When comparing Fogg to other frameworks — nudge theory, motivational interviewing, social cognitive theory.

## Core Concept
Fogg's work occupies a specific niche in the behaviour change landscape: simple, specific, individual behaviours that can be designed into daily routines. Within this niche, the framework is powerful and well-validated through practice if not through extensive peer-reviewed trials. Understanding the niche — and its boundaries — is essential for applying Fogg appropriately.

The most significant criticism is scope. Tiny Habits excels at building specific daily behaviours — flossing, journaling, updating a tracker, taking medication. It is less suited to complex behaviours that require sustained motivation over long periods (where Duckworth's grit framework is more relevant), behaviours that require deep psychological change (where therapeutic approaches are needed), or behaviours that are primarily structurally constrained (where systems change, not individual behaviour design, is required). Fogg acknowledges these boundaries, but popular presentations of his work sometimes overextend the claims.

The persuasive technology controversy is the most prominent reputational issue. Fogg's Stanford courses trained designers who went on to build addictive features into social media platforms. Critics argue that Fogg's framework — and his teaching — enabled technological manipulation at scale. Fogg has responded that his work was intended to help people, that he explicitly teaches ethical application, and that individual designers are responsible for how they apply the principles. The debate is legitimate and unresolved.

Within behaviour science, Fogg's model relates to several established frameworks. The COM-B model (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation → Behaviour) from Michie and colleagues is structurally similar to B=MAP but more detailed in its taxonomy. Thaler and Sunstein's nudge theory shares Fogg's emphasis on designing choice architecture but focuses more on defaults and framing than on habit formation. Prochaska's stages of change model addresses readiness for change in a way Fogg's model does not — B=MAP assumes the person wants to change, which is not always the case. Deci and Ryan's self-determination theory emphasises autonomy, competence, and relatedness as drivers of sustained motivation — deeper motivational constructs than Fogg's more pragmatic treatment.

The evidence base for Tiny Habits is primarily practical rather than academic. Fogg has published peer-reviewed papers on persuasive technology and on the behaviour model, but the Tiny Habits method itself rests more on large-scale practical application (his free programme has reportedly reached over 100,000 participants) than on randomised controlled trials. This is a legitimate limitation for evidence-based practitioners.

## How to Apply
1. **Know the boundaries.** "Tiny Habits works for specific daily behaviours. For complex sustained change, psychological transformation, or structural barriers, use other frameworks."
2. **Integrate with other models.** "Use B=MAP for diagnosis, but draw on COM-B for detailed intervention design, motivational interviewing for ambivalent clients, and structural analysis for systemic barriers."
3. **Acknowledge the evidence base honestly.** "The method is well-practised but under-researched by academic standards. It works for many people; formal evidence is still accumulating."
4. **Engage the persuasive technology debate.** "Fogg's framework can be used for empowerment or exploitation. Understanding this dual potential makes for more ethical application."

## Examples
**Situation:** A colleague says "Isn't Tiny Habits just common sense? 'Start small' is not exactly a breakthrough. And anyway, Fogg trained the people who made social media addictive."
**Application:** Fogg would say: "Two fair challenges. On the first: 'start small' is common sense, but common sense is not common practice. Most behaviour change programmes, most New Year's resolutions, most organisational change initiatives start big. They set ambitious targets, rely on motivation, and fail predictably. The contribution of Tiny Habits is not the insight that small is good — it is the systematic method for how to start small, what to anchor to, how to wire the habit through celebration, and when to let it grow. The method is the value, not the insight. On the second: yes, some of my former students applied behavioural principles to make technology addictive. I take that seriously. My response has been to turn the same principles toward individual empowerment — helping people design their own behaviours rather than having their behaviours designed for them by platforms. The model is a tool. A knife can prepare food or cause harm. I am committed to teaching the food preparation, and I acknowledge the responsibility that comes with creating powerful tools."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present Fogg as sufficient for all behaviour change.
**Why:** Tiny Habits has genuine boundaries. Complex, sustained, structurally constrained, or psychologically deep behaviour change requires different frameworks. Presenting Fogg as a universal solution overpromises and underdelivers.

**Don't:** Dismiss Fogg because of the persuasive technology controversy.
**Why:** The B=MAP model and Tiny Habits method are useful tools regardless of how others have applied behavioural principles. Dismissing the tools because of misapplication throws out genuine utility.
