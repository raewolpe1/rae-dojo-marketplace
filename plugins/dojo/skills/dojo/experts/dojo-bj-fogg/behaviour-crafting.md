# Behaviour Crafting

## When to Use
- When someone has a goal or aspiration but has not identified specific behaviours.
- When the obvious behaviour is failing and alternatives need exploring.
- When the "swarm of behaviours" approach — generating many options — is appropriate.

## Core Concept
Fogg distinguishes between aspirations and behaviours. An aspiration is a general desire — "get healthier," "be more productive," "improve my team's performance." A behaviour is a specific action — "walk for ten minutes after lunch," "write one priority on a sticky note each morning," "ask one question in every team meeting." Most behaviour change fails because people try to act on aspirations directly rather than translating them into specific, designable behaviours.

Behaviour crafting is the process of moving from aspiration to behaviour. Fogg's method is to generate a "swarm" — a large set of possible behaviours that could serve the aspiration. For "get healthier," the swarm might include: walk after lunch, take the stairs, eat one vegetable at dinner, drink water before coffee, stretch for two minutes after sitting for an hour, go to bed fifteen minutes earlier, and dozens more. The point is to explore the space rather than defaulting to the first behaviour that comes to mind.

From the swarm, Fogg recommends selecting behaviours using two criteria: effectiveness (will this actually move me toward my aspiration?) and ease (can I realistically do this given my current life?). The sweet spot — the "golden behaviours" — are those that score high on both. A behaviour that is effective but impossible will not happen. A behaviour that is easy but ineffective wastes effort. The golden behaviours are both impactful and achievable.

This process matters because the obvious behaviour is often not the best behaviour. "Exercise" defaults to "go to the gym" for most people, but going to the gym may be low-ease (requires travel, equipment, time) when "walk after lunch" achieves similar health benefits with much higher ease. Behaviour crafting opens the design space.

## How to Apply
1. **Clarify the aspiration.** "What do you actually want? State it as a general desire, not a specific behaviour."
2. **Generate a swarm.** "List at least ten behaviours that could serve this aspiration. Do not evaluate them yet — just generate."
3. **Plot effectiveness and ease.** "For each behaviour, ask: how effective is this? How easy is it for me specifically?"
4. **Select golden behaviours.** "Choose behaviours that score high on both. These are your best candidates for tiny habits."
5. **Shrink to tiny.** "Take your golden behaviours and find their tiniest viable versions."

## Examples
**Situation:** An M&E specialist wants to "stay current with evaluation methodology" but never finds time to read journals or attend webinars.
**Application:** Fogg would say: "You are stuck on two behaviours — reading journals and attending webinars — and both are high on effectiveness but low on ease. They require blocks of uninterrupted time you do not have. Let us generate a swarm. What else could serve 'staying current'? Follow three evaluation researchers on social media and glance at your feed once daily. Subscribe to one evaluation digest email and read the headlines. Listen to one evaluation podcast episode during your commute. Read one abstract per day — just the abstract, not the paper. Ask one colleague per week what they have read recently. Attend one conference per year and take detailed notes. Join an online evaluation community and check it weekly. Now plot these on effectiveness and ease. Reading one abstract per day: moderate effectiveness, high ease. Following researchers on social media: moderate effectiveness, very high ease. Listening to a podcast during commute: high effectiveness, high ease — you are already commuting. These are your golden behaviours. Pick one, shrink it to tiny, anchor it, and start. You were stuck because you were trying to do the hardest version of staying current. The easy versions work too."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Skip the swarm and default to the obvious behaviour.
**Why:** The obvious behaviour is often the hardest. Exploring the full space of possible behaviours reveals options that are equally effective but much easier.

**Don't:** Select the behaviour for the person.
**Why:** The person knows their life, constraints, and preferences better than you do. Your role is to generate options and help evaluate them — the choice must be theirs.
