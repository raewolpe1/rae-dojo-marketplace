# Prompt Design

## When to Use
- When a behaviour is not happening despite the person being motivated and able.
- When designing cues that reliably trigger desired behaviours.
- When existing prompts (reminders, alarms, notifications) are being ignored.

## Core Concept
Fogg identifies three types of prompts. Person prompts are internal — something in your mind reminds you. These are unreliable because the mind is busy and forgetful. Context prompts are external — sticky notes, phone alarms, notification systems. These are better but suffer from habituation: people learn to ignore them. Action prompts are the most reliable — they are anchored to something you are already doing, so the existing behaviour serves as the cue for the new one. Action prompts are the foundation of the Tiny Habits method.

The critical insight is that most behaviour failures are prompt failures. People assume the problem is motivation — "I just don't want it enough" — when the real problem is that nothing cued the behaviour at the right moment. A person who genuinely intends to take their medication but forgets every morning does not have a motivation problem. They have a prompt problem.

Effective prompts have three properties. They fire at the right moment — when the person has the motivation and ability to act. They are noticeable — impossible to miss or ignore. And they are contextually appropriate — they fit the flow of the person's existing routine rather than interrupting it.

The design principle is: embed the prompt in the existing flow of the day rather than adding an external interruption. After I pour my coffee is a better prompt than a 7:15 AM phone alarm. The coffee is something you attend to; the alarm is something you dismiss.

## How to Apply
1. **Check that motivation and ability are adequate first.** "A prompt only works if the person is above the action line. Adding prompts to someone who lacks ability produces frustration."
2. **Prefer action prompts over context prompts.** "Anchor to existing behaviours. 'After I [existing routine]' is more reliable than 'when my phone buzzes.'"
3. **Make the prompt impossible to miss.** "If the prompt can be ignored, it will be. Place it in the direct path of attention."
4. **Match the prompt to the moment.** "The prompt must fire when the person has the time, energy, and ability to act. A prompt at the wrong moment is noise."

## Examples
**Situation:** A development programme sends weekly SMS reminders to community health workers to submit their reports. Compliance is low despite the reminders.
**Application:** Fogg would say: "SMS reminders are context prompts — external interruptions that compete with whatever the health worker is doing when the message arrives. They are easy to dismiss and easy to forget. Redesign the prompt as an action prompt anchored to existing workflow. When does the health worker naturally finish their last household visit? That is the anchor moment. The prompt becomes: 'after finishing my last visit, before starting the motorbike, I submit my report.' The trigger is the natural transition in their day — a moment they experience reliably every working day. No SMS required. The existing behaviour — finishing visits — cues the new behaviour — submitting the report. If you want a physical prompt as backup, put a sticker on the motorbike seat that says 'report?' so they see it at exactly the moment they transition from visits to travel."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Add more reminders when existing reminders are being ignored.
**Why:** If a prompt is being ignored, more of the same prompt will also be ignored. The problem is the prompt type or timing, not the quantity. Switch from context prompts to action prompts.

**Don't:** Assume the problem is motivation when the person simply forgot.
**Why:** Forgetting looks like not caring, but it is a prompt failure. Before questioning someone's commitment, check whether they had a reliable cue.
