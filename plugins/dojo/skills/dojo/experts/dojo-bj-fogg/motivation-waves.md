# Motivation Waves

## When to Use
- When someone blames their failure on "not being motivated enough."
- When a behaviour change plan depends on sustained high motivation.
- When the action line concept — the trade-off between motivation and ability — needs applying.

## Core Concept
Fogg treats motivation as a wave — it rises and falls unpredictably, driven by mood, energy, context, health, stress, and competing demands. This is not a flaw in people; it is how motivation works. The implication is that any behaviour change system built on the assumption of stable high motivation will fail for most people most of the time.

The action line in the Fogg Behaviour Model illustrates this. Picture a graph with motivation on the vertical axis and ability on the horizontal. The action line curves from upper-left to lower-right. Behaviours above the line happen when prompted; behaviours below the line do not. As ability increases (the behaviour gets easier), the action line drops — less motivation is needed. As ability decreases (the behaviour gets harder), the line rises — more motivation is required.

The strategic insight: you can get a behaviour above the action line by increasing motivation or by increasing ability, but increasing ability is more reliable because it stays increased. Motivation spikes and crashes; ability improvements tend to persist. This is why Fogg's primary lever is always ability — make the behaviour easier — rather than motivation — make the person want it more.

Motivation waves do have a use, however. When motivation is naturally high — after a health scare, at the start of a new year, after an inspiring event — that is the moment to do hard things that require high motivation: signing up for a course, committing publicly, setting up systems. Use motivation spikes to create infrastructure, not to sustain daily behaviour. Fogg calls this "surfing the motivation wave" — riding the peak to do setup work, then relying on ability and prompts for the daily practice.

## How to Apply
1. **Stop blaming motivation.** "Your motivation is not the problem. Your design is the problem. Redesign the behaviour so it does not require high motivation."
2. **Move the behaviour below the action line.** "Make it easier until it requires less motivation than you typically have on your worst day."
3. **Surf motivation spikes for setup tasks.** "When motivation is high, use it to create systems — sign up, buy equipment, set up the environment. Do not use it for daily behaviour."
4. **Design for low-motivation days.** "The test of a good behaviour design is whether it works on your worst day, not your best day."

## Examples
**Situation:** A team leader has tried to get her team to hold weekly learning sessions. They start enthusiastically but attendance drops after three weeks.
**Application:** Fogg would say: "Classic motivation wave. The initial enthusiasm — the spike — carried them for three weeks. When enthusiasm normalised, attendance dropped because the behaviour was above the action line at normal motivation levels. Three fixes. First, shrink the behaviour. A one-hour learning session is a high-ability-demand behaviour. Try a fifteen-minute 'one thing I learned this week' round. That is below the action line at normal motivation. Second, anchor it to something unmissable — do it as the first fifteen minutes of an existing weekly meeting, not as a separate calendar item. Third, use the initial motivation spike differently. Instead of launching the sessions during the enthusiasm peak, use the enthusiasm to set up the infrastructure: create the recurring calendar item, designate a facilitator rota, establish the format. Then launch the tiny version when enthusiasm has normalised. You want the system to work on the week when nobody feels like it — that is the design test."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Try to sustain high motivation through inspirational messaging.
**Why:** Inspiration produces motivation spikes, not sustained motivation. Each inspirational message has diminishing returns. The energy spent on motivation campaigns is better spent on making the behaviour easier.

**Don't:** Dismiss motivation entirely.
**Why:** Motivation matters — it is one of three essential elements. The point is not that motivation is irrelevant but that it is unreliable as the primary driver of sustained behaviour.
