# Fogg Behaviour Model (B=MAP)

## When to Use
- When diagnosing why a behaviour is or is not happening.
- When someone is failing to act despite apparent motivation.
- When designing an intervention and needing to identify the right lever.

## Core Concept
The Fogg Behaviour Model states that behaviour happens when three elements converge at the same moment: Motivation, Ability, and Prompt. B=MAP. Remove any one element and the behaviour does not occur. This is the foundational diagnostic tool of Fogg's entire system.

Motivation is the desire to perform the behaviour. It fluctuates with mood, energy, context, and competing priorities. Ability is the capacity to perform the behaviour — determined by time, money, physical effort, cognitive effort, and fit with existing routines. The Prompt is the cue that says "do this now" — an alarm, a visual reminder, an anchor moment, or a contextual trigger.

The model's key insight is the action line — the relationship between motivation and ability. When ability is high (the behaviour is very easy), even low motivation is sufficient. When ability is low (the behaviour is hard), high motivation is required. The action line is the threshold: above it, behaviour happens when prompted; below it, no prompt will work.

Most behaviour change programmes focus on motivation — inspiring people, educating them, frightening them. Fogg's contribution is showing that ability is a more reliable lever. Making a behaviour easier moves it above the action line permanently, while boosting motivation produces a temporary spike that fades. The strategic implication is: instead of asking "how do I motivate people to do this?" ask "how do I make this so easy that motivation barely matters?"

The prompt is the element most often overlooked. Motivated, able people still fail to act if nothing triggers the behaviour at the right moment. This is why habit formation depends on anchoring — connecting new behaviours to existing routines that serve as reliable prompts.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose the missing element.** "Is the person failing because they do not want to (motivation), they cannot manage it (ability), or they are not being cued (prompt)?"
2. **Prioritise ability over motivation.** "If you can make the behaviour easier, do that first. It is more reliable than trying to increase motivation."
3. **Design the prompt.** "If motivation and ability are sufficient but the behaviour is not happening, the problem is almost certainly the prompt."
4. **Check all three simultaneously.** "The model requires all three at the same moment. High motivation without a prompt produces nothing. A prompt without ability produces frustration."

## Examples
**Situation:** A monitoring and evaluation team has designed excellent data collection forms, trained field staff, and explained why data quality matters. But field staff still submit incomplete forms.
**Application:** Fogg would say: "Run B=MAP. Motivation: your field staff understand why data matters — you have addressed this through training. Motivation is probably adequate but not the bottleneck. Ability: how long does a complete form take? How many fields? Can it be done in the field or does it require returning to the office? If the form takes forty-five minutes and requires connectivity, you have an ability problem regardless of motivation. Solution: reduce the form to the five most critical fields for field completion. Prompt: when in the daily workflow is the form supposed to be completed? If there is no natural trigger — if 'complete the form' is a floating task — it will be forgotten. Anchor it: 'immediately after the last household visit of the day, before starting the motorbike, complete the five-field form.' The anchor — finishing the last visit — is the prompt. The tiny form — five fields — is the ability fix. You have addressed motivation already; now address the other two elements."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Default to motivation as the diagnosis.
**Why:** Most behaviour failures are ability or prompt failures disguised as motivation problems. "They just don't care enough" is almost always wrong. They probably care — they just cannot manage the behaviour or forget to do it.

**Don't:** Apply the model without considering all three elements together.
**Why:** Fixing one element while another remains broken produces no change. A brilliant prompt delivered to someone who lacks ability produces frustration, not behaviour.
