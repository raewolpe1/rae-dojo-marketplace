# Tiny Habits

## When to Use
- When someone wants to build a new behaviour into their daily routine.
- When previous behaviour change attempts have failed because they were too ambitious.
- When the concept of starting small needs grounding in a specific method.

## Core Concept
Tiny Habits is Fogg's signature method for behaviour change. The premise is counterintuitive: to create a lasting habit, start with a version so small it seems almost absurd. Not "meditate for twenty minutes" but "take three deep breaths." Not "exercise daily" but "do two pushups." Not "write in your journal" but "open the journal and write one word."

The method has three components. First, an anchor moment — an existing routine you already do reliably. This serves as the prompt. Second, the tiny behaviour — the smallest meaningful version of the habit you want. Third, celebration — an immediate burst of positive emotion after performing the tiny behaviour.

The format is the habit recipe: "After I [anchor moment], I will [tiny behaviour]." After I pour my morning coffee, I will write one sentence in my journal. After I sit down at my desk, I will open the project tracker. After I finish my last meeting of the day, I will write one thing I learned.

The tiny version is not the goal — it is the seed. Once the behaviour is reliably happening (because it is so easy that motivation is irrelevant), natural growth takes over. The person who starts with two pushups finds themselves doing five, then ten. The person who starts with one sentence finds themselves writing a paragraph. This growth is not forced — it emerges naturally from the established habit.

The method works because it addresses the most common failure mode in behaviour change: starting too big, relying on motivation, and then quitting when motivation drops. By making the behaviour tiny, motivation requirements drop to near zero. By anchoring to an existing routine, the prompt is built into the day. By celebrating, the neurological wiring happens that converts deliberate action into automatic habit.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the aspiration.** "What do you want? 'Get healthier,' 'be more organised,' 'learn more.' Start with what matters to you."
2. **Generate behaviour options.** "What specific behaviours could serve that aspiration? List at least ten. Do not default to the obvious one."
3. **Select and shrink.** "Pick the behaviour that is both effective and easy. Then shrink it to its tiniest viable version — thirty seconds or less."
4. **Find the anchor.** "What do you already do reliably every day that could serve as the trigger? The anchor should come naturally before the moment you want the new behaviour."
5. **Write the recipe.** "After I [anchor], I will [tiny behaviour]."
6. **Celebrate immediately.** "Right after doing it, generate a moment of positive emotion. A fist pump, a quiet 'yes,' a smile. Whatever feels natural to you."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme manager wants to start reflecting on lessons learned from fieldwork but never finds the time for a proper reflection session.
**Application:** Fogg would say: "You are designing for failure. 'A proper reflection session' is too big, too vague, and has no prompt. Redesign it as a tiny habit. Your anchor: after you close your laptop at the end of the work day — something you already do reliably. Your tiny behaviour: write one sentence about what you noticed today. One sentence. In a notebook next to your laptop, on a sticky note, in your phone — wherever is easiest. Your celebration: after writing the sentence, close the notebook and say 'done' to yourself. That positive feeling is what wires the habit. After two weeks of one sentence, you will find yourself writing two. Then three. Then a paragraph. Then you will have a reflection practice without ever having scheduled a 'proper reflection session.' The tiny version got you through the door. Growth happened naturally."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Skip the celebration.
**Why:** Without the positive emotion immediately after the behaviour, the habit does not wire neurologically. The behaviour remains effortful and voluntary rather than becoming automatic. Celebration is the mechanism, not a nicety.

**Don't:** Set the tiny version too large.
**Why:** If your "tiny" behaviour takes five minutes, it is not tiny. It should take thirty seconds or less. The test: can you do this even on your worst day, when you are exhausted and unmotivated? If the answer is no, shrink it further.
