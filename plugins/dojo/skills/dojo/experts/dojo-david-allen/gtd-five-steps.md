## When to Use

Use this topic when the user feels overwhelmed, paralysed, or unsure where to start with their work. Also when they are asking how GTD works as a system, or when their current approach lacks a clear processing method.

## Fails When

- The user skips steps (typically Clarify) and goes straight from capture to action
- The "Organise" step becomes a procrastination substitute for actual work
- The weekly review (Reflect) is treated as optional
- Any one step is over-optimised at the expense of the others

## Core Concept

Getting Things Done rests on five sequential steps that transform raw inputs — everything that has your attention — into trusted, actionable material. The steps are **Capture, Clarify, Organise, Reflect, Engage**. Each is distinct; confusing them is the most common reason GTD implementations fail.

**Step 1: Capture.** Everything that has your attention must be collected into an external inbox. This is not a selection process — it is a completeness process. Your mind generates inputs constantly: tasks, ideas, worries, promises, reminders, half-formed thoughts. None of them belong in your head. Capture tools include physical inboxes, notebooks, voice memos, email, task apps. The rule is: one trusted capture location per medium, regularly processed.

**Step 2: Clarify.** Process each captured item by asking two questions: *What is it?* and *Is it actionable?* If not actionable, it is either trash, reference material, or someday/maybe. If actionable, it must produce either a next physical action (if it can be done in one step) or a project definition (if it requires more than one step). This step is where most people fail — they capture but never decide.

**Step 3: Organise.** Place the outputs of clarification into the right lists and locations: next actions (organised by context), projects list, calendar, waiting-for, someday/maybe, reference. Nothing in the system means anything without appropriate categorisation.

**Step 4: Reflect.** Review the system regularly to keep it current, complete, and trusted. The weekly review is the primary maintenance ritual: clear all inboxes, review all lists, update next actions for all projects, look at the horizon. Without Reflect, the system rots.

**Step 5: Engage.** Choose what to do from your organised inventory based on context, time available, energy available, and priority. When the first four steps are done well, Engage becomes straightforward — you are choosing from a curated menu, not scrambling.

## How to Apply

1. **Set up your capture tools.** Identify one inbox per input medium: physical, digital, voice. Everything goes there — nothing stays in your head.

2. **Schedule a clarification session.** Process your inboxes to zero. For each item: what is it? If not actionable, file, trash, or park in someday/maybe. If actionable and under two minutes, do it now. If actionable and longer, define the next physical action and assign to the appropriate list or calendar.

3. **Build your system.** Create your project list, context-based next action lists (@computer, @phone, @errands, @waiting-for), calendar, someday/maybe list, and reference archive.

4. **Schedule your weekly review.** Block 60–90 minutes weekly. Get clear (empty all inboxes), get current (review and update all lists), get creative (notice new opportunities and commitments). This is non-negotiable.

5. **Engage with confidence.** When working, consult your next action lists filtered by current context, available time, and energy. Trust the system.

## Examples

**Example 1: The overwhelmed executive**
An executive has 400 emails, a stack of papers, three notebooks, and a mental list of 50 things. She feels paralysed. Allen's prescription: stop doing anything until the system is set up. Capture everything — all papers, all notes, all emails — into a defined input. Then clarify each item. The processing itself reveals that 60% of the items require no action (reference or trash), 30% can be handled in under two minutes, and the remaining 10% become a manageable set of projects and next actions.

**Example 2: The developer who can't start**
A developer has "work on the authentication module" on his list and stares at it every day without acting. The GTD diagnosis: this is a project, not a next action. Clarify produces: "Open auth.py and add token validation function to line 47." Now there is a concrete, visible physical action. He can start within seconds.

## Anti-Patterns

**Capturing without clarifying.** Building a huge, unprocessed list feels productive but is not. An unclarified inbox is just a more organised source of anxiety.

**Organising without a complete capture.** Setting up elaborate context lists while things are still living in your head creates a false sense of security. The system is only as good as its completeness.

**Reviewing without engaging.** Some people enjoy the weekly review but never trust the system enough to actually work from it. The review is preparation for action, not a substitute for it.
