## When to Use

Use when someone is overwhelmed by email or other inboxes, when inputs pile up without being processed, or when they confuse "reading" an email with "processing" it.

## Fails When

- Processing is conflated with doing (acting on everything immediately)
- Items are re-read repeatedly without decisions being made
- Inbox zero is the goal rather than the decision discipline

## Core Concept

**Clarification** is the second step of the GTD five-step workflow. Its purpose is to process every item in your collection inboxes by making a decision about each one. The goal is not a tidy inbox — the inbox's emptiness is a byproduct of the decision discipline, not the objective.

**The clarification decision tree.** Every item in an inbox passes through the same logic:

1. **What is it?** Identify what the item actually is.
2. **Is it actionable?** Does it require a physical action from you or someone else?
   - **No:** Three options — trash it (not needed), incubate it (someday/maybe list or tickler file), or file it as reference.
   - **Yes:** Define the next action.
3. **What is the next action?** Identify the next physical action required to move it forward. This is the critical step — not "think about project X" but "call Dr Mensah's office to confirm appointment."
4. **Will the next action take less than two minutes?** If yes, do it now.
5. **Am I the right person?** If someone else should do it, delegate and add a waiting-for note.
6. **If not now and not delegated:** Defer it — add it to the appropriate next-actions list or calendar.

**Inbox zero.** The term, popularised in part by Merlin Mann drawing on GTD, means that every inbox has been processed to empty — not that every task has been completed. Inbox zero means you have made a decision about every item and moved it out of the inbox to the appropriate place in your system. The inbox is not a to-do list.

**Processing versus doing.** A critical distinction: processing means making decisions about items, not doing them. During a processing session, you move items to the right places. Doing is separate. Confusing the two produces marathon "processing" sessions that are really unfocused work sessions.

**Frequency of processing.** Allen recommends processing inboxes until empty at each processing session, and scheduling those sessions rather than treating email as a constant stream. Two or three defined email sessions per day is more efficient than continuous monitoring.

## How to Apply

1. **Process from top to bottom, one item at a time.** Start with the first item. Make a decision. Move it. Move to the next item. Do not scan, sort, or shuffle — process.

2. **Never put anything back into the inbox.** An item that enters the inbox has entered the clarification process. It exits into the trash, reference, someday/maybe, calendar, next-actions list, or the waiting-for list. It does not go back to the inbox.

3. **Define actions in physical, specific terms.** Not "deal with budget" but "open budget spreadsheet and update Q3 projections." The more specific the action, the easier it is to execute.

4. **Use the two-minute rule.** If the next action takes less than two minutes, do it during processing.

5. **Process to zero.** The goal of each processing session is an empty inbox. If you run out of time, stop and schedule another session — do not leave half-processed items in the inbox.

## Examples

**Example 1: The email that stays in the inbox for a week**
An email from a colleague asks for input on a proposal. The professional reads it, thinks "I need to respond to that," and leaves it marked unread. He re-reads it four times over the next week without responding. Processing fix: during his next email session, he applies the decision tree. It is actionable; the next action is "draft three bullet-point response to Alex's proposal"; it will take eight minutes, not two; he adds it to his @computer next-actions list and archives the email. Inbox zero reached for that item.

**Example 2: The reference document**
An email arrives with a useful article about regulatory changes. Processing decision: not actionable — reference only. She saves it to her reference folder tagged "regulatory/2026" and deletes the email. Total processing time: 20 seconds.

## Anti-Patterns

**Treating the inbox as a to-do list.** Leaving items in the inbox as a reminder to act on them. This means every visit to the inbox requires re-reading everything to recall what still needs attention — a massive and repetitive overhead.

**Processing without deciding.** Reading emails or scanning items without making a decision about each one. This is not processing — it is browsing. Processing requires a decision and an action (move to trash, reference, calendar, or list) for every item.

**Batch-deleting without processing.** Declaring "inbox zero" by archiving or deleting everything. This produces a clean inbox by dumping commitments rather than managing them — the opposite of GTD.
