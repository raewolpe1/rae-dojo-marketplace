## When to Use

Use when someone feels guilty about ideas they are not pursuing, when delegated items fall through cracks, or when someone cannot distinguish between what they are doing now and what they might do eventually.

## Fails When

- The someday/maybe list is never reviewed and becomes a dumping ground
- Waiting-for items are not tracked and people do not follow up on delegated work

## Core Concept

GTD identifies two categories of commitments that are real and important but require neither immediate action nor formal project status: things you are genuinely considering doing someday, and things you have delegated and are waiting on others to complete. Both require systematic tracking; neither requires daily attention.

**The Someday/Maybe List**

The someday/maybe list is the holding area for ideas, projects, and possibilities that you are not committed to doing now but are not willing to permanently abandon. It is not a graveyard — it is an incubation list. Items on it deserve periodic review because your life and priorities change, and something that was not right for this season may be exactly right for the next.

What belongs on someday/maybe:
- Projects you want to do when you have more time, money, or capacity
- Skills you want to develop eventually
- Books to read, places to visit, courses to take
- Business ideas not yet ready for active development
- Purchases under consideration but not decided

The failure mode is treating someday/maybe as permanent deferral. Items should be reviewed at every weekly review. The question for each item: has anything changed that makes this more or less viable now? Does it belong on the active projects list? Should it be deleted? Is it still genuinely incubating?

The someday/maybe list is also a release valve. When you have more ideas than you can pursue, committing a genuine possibility to someday/maybe — trusting the review process to surface it at the right time — allows your mind to let go of it without feeling like you are abandoning it. This is psychologically significant. The mind clings to uncaptured possibilities. The list gives them a home.

**Subtypes of someday/maybe.** Many practitioners maintain separate lists or sections within someday/maybe for distinct categories: books to read, travel to consider, business ideas, skills to develop. These are personal organising choices. The discipline — regular review and honest pruning — applies to all of them.

**The Waiting-For List**

The waiting-for list tracks all commitments that require action from someone else before you can move forward. It is the systematic answer to the question "what have I delegated that I need to follow up on?"

What belongs on waiting-for:
- Tasks you have delegated to colleagues or direct reports
- Information requested from others
- Deliverables due from vendors or contractors
- Decisions pending from managers or clients
- Replies awaited from any communication

The failure mode is tracking delegated items mentally. Mental tracking of delegated work produces anxiety proportional to the number of open items, consumes cognitive bandwidth, and results in missed follow-ups. A written waiting-for list removes this burden and makes follow-up systematic rather than dependent on memory.

**Processing waiting-for items.** During the clarification step, when an action requires someone else to act, the item goes on waiting-for rather than a next-actions list. Add the date the request was made. During the weekly review, scan the entire waiting-for list and ask: is any item overdue? Do I need to follow up? If follow-up is needed, it becomes a next action.

**Waiting-for in email.** Many practitioners use a specific label or folder in their email system to tag waiting-for items — an email thread where they are awaiting a reply. This is a legitimate implementation as long as the tagged items are reviewed regularly and follow-up actions are generated when needed.

## How to Apply

1. **Create a someday/maybe list if you don't have one.** Collect everything you have been meaning to do someday — from learning guitar to starting a side project — and move it off your active projects list and onto someday/maybe. This clarifies what you are actually committed to now.

2. **Review someday/maybe at every weekly review.** For each item: still relevant? Ready to activate? Should be deleted? Items that are never reviewed should be deleted — if you haven't looked at them in six months of reviews, they are not incubating, they are cluttering.

3. **Use waiting-for as your delegation tracker.** Every time you delegate a task, request information, or send something that requires a response, add an entry to your waiting-for list. Include what was requested, from whom, and when.

4. **Follow up systematically from waiting-for.** During your weekly review, scan the entire waiting-for list. Items that are overdue or approaching a critical date become next actions: send a follow-up email, make a call, escalate.

5. **Trust the system to hold your possibilities.** When you add something to someday/maybe, do so with the genuine intention of reviewing it. The list is useful only if the review is happening.

## Examples

**Example 1: The someday/maybe as idea incubator**
A consultant had 23 "aspirational projects" that were not actually getting any attention but were consuming guilt every time they surfaced. She moved all 23 to someday/maybe. During the next eight weekly reviews, seven were deleted as genuinely unimportant, three were activated (she now had capacity), and thirteen remained on the list. Removing them from the active projects list eliminated the guilt; the review process ensured none were forgotten.

**Example 2: The waiting-for recovery**
A manager had no tracking system for delegated tasks. He relied on memory and email search when he needed to follow up. He spent 30 minutes one Friday populating his waiting-for list from his sent email: 14 items he was waiting on, from 9 different people. He reviewed the list and found 4 items were already overdue. He sent follow-up messages immediately. Within two weeks he had a reliable delegation tracking habit.

## Anti-Patterns

**Someday/maybe as permanent deferral.** Adding items to someday/maybe with no intention of ever reviewing them. If you are not willing to review an item periodically, either commit to it (projects list) or delete it. Someday/maybe is not a place to store things you feel guilty about not doing.

**No waiting-for list.** Relying on memory, starred emails, or mental tracking for delegated work. This produces anxiety proportional to the number of open items and results in missed follow-ups. The cost of not tracking waiting-for items is reliably higher than the cost of maintaining the list.

**Waiting-for as an accusation list.** Tracking delegated items in a spirit of catching people who miss deadlines rather than as a neutral coordination tool. The list is for your reliability, not for blame management.
