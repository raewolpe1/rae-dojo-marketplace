## When to Use

Use when someone's GTD implementation has broken down, when someone tried GTD and abandoned it, or when someone feels more overwhelmed after implementing GTD than before.

## Fails When

- The person is in acute overwhelm and needs triage, not a diagnostic session
- The failure is actually a life problem (wrong job, misaligned priorities) that no productivity system can fix

## Core Concept

GTD fails in predictable ways. The failure modes are not unique to individuals — they are structural vulnerabilities in the system that appear reliably when certain practices are omitted or degraded. Identifying which failure mode is active is the first step to recovery.

**Failure Mode 1: The incomplete capture.** The person captures in some contexts (meetings, at the desk) but not others (in the car, in conversation, at night). The result is a trusted system that only captures part of what has attention. The mind continues to hold the uncaptured remainder, and the sense of overwhelm does not lift. The test: if you lie awake at night with thoughts circling, your capture is incomplete.

**Failure Mode 2: The inbox that never empties.** The person captures reliably but does not clarify. The inbox becomes a second to-do list — a large, undifferentiated mass of items that produces anxiety rather than relief. Processing to zero is not a metaphor; it is a literal discipline. Items in the inbox have not yet been decided about. Until they are, they cannot give you any relief.

**Failure Mode 3: The missing next action.** The person has a projects list but each project has no identified next physical action. When they look at the projects list, they feel stuck. They know the outcome they want but not what to do next. The result: projects stall indefinitely, the list produces guilt rather than momentum. Every project requires a next action — a physical, visible step that can be done in a specific context.

**Failure Mode 4: The abandoned weekly review.** The system works well for the first few weeks, then the weekly review is skipped once (busy week), then twice, then indefinitely. Within a few weeks the system is stale, the lists are untrustworthy, and the person has reverted to relying on their head. The weekly review is the maintenance practice. A GTD system without a weekly review is a car that was never serviced — it will eventually stop working.

**Failure Mode 5: The trusted system that is not trusted.** The lists exist but the person does not actually use them to make decisions about what to work on. They check email instead, or work on what feels urgent, or rely on memory. The system becomes a guilt-producing archive rather than an active engagement tool. Trust in the system must be earned through the experience of it working — which requires actually engaging with it consistently.

**Failure Mode 6: The over-engineered system.** The person spent so much time designing the perfect GTD system — with elaborate tags, multiple review levels, complex context hierarchies — that the system itself became a project that crowded out actual work. The system is too heavy to maintain. GTD requires only the minimum structure that allows you to trust it.

**Failure Mode 7: The context mismatch.** The person set up contexts that don't match how they actually work. They created @calls but they're always at a desk with a phone. They have @errands but they don't batch errands. The context lists produce artificial friction. Context design must reflect actual work patterns.

**Failure Mode 8: The someday/maybe burial ground.** Items on the someday/maybe list are never reviewed. The list grows indefinitely and becomes a graveyard of abandoned aspirations rather than an active incubation list. Someday/maybe requires a regular review — at minimum during the weekly review — or it becomes meaningless.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose before fixing.** Ask: Where in the five-step workflow does the breakdown occur? Is capture incomplete? Is the inbox not being processed? Are projects missing next actions? Is the weekly review not happening? The fix depends on the diagnosis.

2. **Restart with a complete capture sweep.** If the system has degraded significantly, restart with a full mind sweep — every open loop captured into a single inbox. This resets the foundation.

3. **Process to zero before adding complexity.** Before adding new contexts, new tools, or new review practices, get the inbox to zero. Until the clarification step is working, everything else is premature.

4. **Protect the weekly review.** Put it on the calendar as a recurring hard commitment. Treat it like an external appointment. A 90-minute weekly review prevents weeks of degradation.

5. **Simplify, not elaborate.** When the system feels heavy, remove elements. The minimum viable GTD system — inbox, projects list, context-based next-action lists, weekly review — is enough. Complexity should only be added when the minimum system is running smoothly.

## Examples

**Example 1: The skipped weekly review**
A project manager ran her GTD system well for two months, then skipped the weekly review during a particularly busy week. Within three weeks her projects list had no current next actions, her someday/maybe list had grown to 60 items she hadn't reviewed, and she had stopped checking her lists. She restarted by scheduling a two-hour review, processing every list, and confirming a next action for every active project. The system recovered within one week.

**Example 2: The over-engineered collapse**
A software developer spent two weeks designing a custom GTD system in Notion with nested projects, 15 context tags, automated reviews, and integrations with his calendar and task manager. By the time he finished, he was exhausted and hadn't actually worked his system. He rebuilt it in plain text: INBOX.txt, PROJECTS.txt, NEXT.txt with four context headers. The simple system ran for six months without failure.

## Anti-Patterns

**Treating the system design as productive work.** Spending significant time optimising your GTD system when you are behind on actual commitments. The system exists to serve your work, not to replace it. System maintenance should be invisible overhead, not a primary activity.

**Abandoning GTD after one failure.** A skipped weekly review or an overflowing inbox does not mean GTD has failed. Every GTD practitioner has periods of degradation. The discipline is in restarting — doing the sweep, processing to zero, restoring next actions — not in maintaining a perfect system indefinitely.

**Adding complexity to solve a discipline problem.** Believing that a more sophisticated system will solve the problem of not doing the weekly review, or not processing the inbox. The failure is almost always a discipline failure, not a system design failure. Simpler systems are easier to maintain.
