## When to Use

Use when someone has implemented GTD but reports that it is "falling apart," when the system feels stale or untrustworthy, or when someone asks how to sustain a productivity system over time. The weekly review is the answer to nearly all GTD maintenance problems.

## Fails When

- The review is treated as optional and skipped under pressure
- The review becomes only a task-checking exercise without horizon reflection
- The review is done too quickly to actually process all inboxes
- There is no protected time block for it

## Core Concept

The weekly review is the single most important practice in GTD. It is not a nice addition to the system — it is the maintenance ritual without which the entire system degrades. Allen describes it as "the master key" to making GTD work over months and years rather than weeks.

**Why weekly?** Shorter than weekly does not allow enough time for new commitments to accumulate. Longer than weekly allows the system to drift far enough from current reality that trust erodes. One week is the natural frequency of the business calendar and is short enough that no single review becomes overwhelming.

**The three phases: Get Clear, Get Current, Get Creative.**

*Get Clear* means emptying all inboxes to zero. Not sorting them — processing them. Every physical inbox, email inbox, voice note, scratch pad, and captured item must be clarified: what is it? What does it mean? What is the next action?

*Get Current* means reviewing all lists. Review the calendar for the past week (what did you not complete? does anything need follow-up?) and the next two weeks (what is coming? what needs preparation?). Review all next action lists for completeness and relevance. Review all project files: does every project have a next action? Review the waiting-for list: is there anything to follow up on? Review someday/maybe: is there anything whose time has come?

*Get Creative* means looking up — at 10,000ft (current projects), 20,000ft (areas of responsibility), 30,000ft (1–2-year goals), and beyond. Are your projects aligned with your responsibilities? Are your responsibilities aligned with your goals? What new thinking has emerged this week that belongs in the system?

**The feeling after.** After a complete weekly review, the experience Allen describes is consistent: a feeling of clarity, spaciousness, and readiness. Not because the work is done — it almost certainly is not — but because the full landscape of commitments is visible and trusted.

## How to Apply

1. **Block 60–90 minutes.** Most people need 75 minutes for a thorough weekly review when the system is current. Schedule it as a non-negotiable appointment, preferably at the same time each week. Friday afternoon or Sunday evening are common. Protect it.

2. **Get Clear first.** Process every inbox to zero before reviewing any lists. You cannot assess where you are if new, unprocessed material is still waiting. Go through physical inbox, email, notes app, voice memos, wallet scraps — everything.

3. **Get Current — calendar review.** Look at the past week: what did not happen that should have? Look at the next two to four weeks: what needs preparation?

4. **Get Current — lists review.** Scan every item on every next action list. Archive or delete items that are no longer relevant. For any project without a next action, define one now.

5. **Get Creative — horizon review.** Ask: are my projects still the right projects? Are there areas of my life I am neglecting? Has anything happened this week that changes my priorities at a higher altitude?

## Examples

**Example 1: The GTD practitioner who fell off**
A professional implemented GTD well for three months, then stopped. She reports that the system "stopped working." Allen's diagnostic question: when did you last do a weekly review? Answer: six weeks ago. The system did not fail — the maintenance stopped. A single thorough weekly review, even after a long gap, typically restores the system.

**Example 2: The professional who reviews lists but skips Get Clear**
A manager does his weekly review by scanning his next action lists, but never fully processes his email inboxes or physical inbox. After two months, his lists are current but his inboxes are an accumulating mess. The anxiety persists because he knows there are items in the inboxes that are not in the system. Get Clear is the precondition for Get Current — skipping it undermines everything.

## Anti-Patterns

**The inbox-skimming review.** Skimming inboxes rather than processing them to zero. Items that are seen but not decided on remain open loops in the brain regardless of whether they are technically in an inbox.

**The review-as-task-check.** Treating the weekly review as only a list-review without the horizon scan. You can be brilliantly organised about the wrong things. The higher-altitude review is what aligns your doing with your being.

**Skipping the review when busiest.** The busier you are, the more important the weekly review — because the system has accumulated more new material and the risk of invisible misalignment is highest. Skipping it when overwhelmed is precisely backwards.
