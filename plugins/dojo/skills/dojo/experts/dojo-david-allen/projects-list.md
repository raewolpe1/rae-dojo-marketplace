## When to Use

Use when someone conflates "task" with "project," when commitments fall through because no one tracks multi-step outcomes, or when people feel behind without being able to name specifically what they are behind on.

## Fails When

- Projects are defined so broadly they become life goals rather than actionable outcomes
- The projects list is never reviewed, making it decoration rather than a system
- Next actions are not linked to projects, leaving projects without traction

## Core Concept

A **project** in GTD is any desired outcome that requires more than one action step to complete. This is the definition that matters — not the scale, not the importance, not the duration. "Get car serviced" is a project because it requires scheduling an appointment and then dropping off the car. "Launch the new product" is also a project.

**Why the projects list is essential.** The human mind cannot hold dozens of multi-step commitments simultaneously and reliably. Without an external list, projects drop off the radar — not because they were completed, but because attention moved elsewhere. The projects list is the antidote to this forgetting. It is the complete inventory of all outcomes you are committed to completing, reviewed regularly, and connected to active next actions.

**The relationship between projects and next actions.** Every project should have at least one next physical action attached to it at all times. If a project has no next action, it is stalled — you have committed to an outcome without committing to the next step, which means the outcome will not move forward. The weekly review's primary function is to ensure every project has a next action.

**Scope and granularity.** The projects list should be comprehensive and specific. "Personal" and "Work" are not projects. "Draft Q3 strategic plan" is a project. "Research accountants" is a project. "Plan mother's 70th birthday party" is a project. The more precisely the outcome is named, the easier it is to identify the next action and to recognise completion.

**Subprojects and project support.** Large projects often contain subprojects — discrete multi-step outcomes nested within a bigger goal. These can be listed separately or tracked within project-specific reference folders. The key is that each subproject also has a next action.

**Reviewing the projects list.** The weekly review requires scanning every project and asking: is this still a commitment? What is the next action? Is that action on the next-actions lists? The projects list is not set-and-forget — it is a living inventory updated as projects are completed, added, or abandoned.

## How to Apply

1. **Do a project inventory.** List every outcome you are committed to completing — professional and personal — that requires more than one action. Take as long as needed. Most people discover 30–100 projects when they do this honestly for the first time.

2. **Name projects as outcomes, not activities.** "Call vendor" is an action. "Contract signed with vendor" is a project outcome. Name each project so that you could answer "is this done?" with a clear yes or no.

3. **Check every project for a next action.** For each item on your projects list, verify there is at least one next action on your next-actions lists. If not, add one immediately.

4. **Create project support folders as needed.** For complex projects, create a reference folder (physical or digital) to hold relevant materials — documents, notes, contact info. The folder is not the project; the project is the outcome on the list.

5. **Review the projects list weekly.** Every week, scan the complete list. Add new projects, delete completed or abandoned ones, and ensure every remaining project has a next action.

## Examples

**Example 1: The project that wasn't tracked**
A consultant said she felt "always behind on client work." When she listed her actual project commitments, she found 23 active client deliverables, only 7 of which were on any list. The other 16 were in her head, creating a persistent anxiety without a corresponding action plan. Adding them to a projects list with next actions attached immediately reduced her stress and improved her delivery rate.

**Example 2: The over-broad project**
A professional listed "improve my health" as a project. This is a goal or area of focus, not a project — there is no discrete outcome, no completion point. Replacing it with specific projects — "complete 30-day strength training program," "schedule annual physical," "research and choose nutritionist" — gave each commitment a trackable form and a next action.

## Anti-Patterns

**No projects list.** Tracking projects only in memory or in scattered notes without a consolidated list. This guarantees that projects will be forgotten or poorly managed.

**Projects without next actions.** Having a comprehensive projects list but not linking each project to a next action. This is a list of desired outcomes with no mechanism for achieving them.

**Tasks masquerading as projects.** Listing "call Jenny" or "email report" as projects. Single-action items belong on next-actions lists, not the projects list. Mixing them clutters the list and obscures true multi-step commitments.
