## When to Use

Use when someone is stuck on a task, when a project has stalled, or when a to-do list is full of vague items that never get done. The next physical action question is Allen's single most powerful diagnostic tool.

## Fails When

- The action identified is still a sub-project ("write the report") rather than a physical step
- Context prevents the action from being taken and no alternative is identified
- The action is physically correct but the required resource is missing and not tracked

## Core Concept

The **next physical action** is the single most important concept in Getting Things Done. It is the specific, visible, external, physical activity that needs to happen to move a project or task forward. Allen argues that most stuck work is stuck not because it is difficult, but because no one has defined what "doing it" actually looks like at the level of physical reality.

**Why vague tasks fail.** A task like "deal with the merger" or "sort out the website" or "prepare for the conference" is not actionable. The brain recognises that it cannot execute a vague instruction and so it deflects — the item gets skipped repeatedly, generating guilt but no progress. The moment a next physical action is defined, the path clears: the brain can visualise executing it, the body can follow, and momentum begins.

**What makes something a next physical action.** It must be:
- *Visible* — you can see it being done
- *External* — it happens in the physical world, not in your head
- *Physical* — it involves moving something (your fingers, your voice, your body, a cursor)
- *Next* — it is the immediate next step, not a later step contingent on something unresolved

Examples: "Open the draft in Google Docs," "Call Sarah on her mobile to ask about the budget dates," "Walk to the printer and retrieve the signed contract," "Type the first paragraph of the executive summary."

Not examples: "Think about the proposal," "Work on the website," "Deal with Jim," "Plan the event."

**The question that unlocks work.** When someone is stuck, Allen's first question is always: "What is the very next physical action?" Pushing toward a specific answer — past "talk to the team" to "email Marcus at m.jones@co.com and ask which format he needs by when" — almost always produces immediate forward movement.

**Next actions belong in context lists.** Once defined, a next action is placed on the appropriate context list: @computer, @phone, @errands, @office, @waiting-for. This way, when you are at a computer, you can review all computer-executable actions without being distracted by actions requiring phone or travel.

## How to Apply

1. **Audit your current task list.** Look at each item and ask: is this a next physical action or is it a project or vague intention? Anything with more than one step is a project. Move it to the projects list and define its next action.

2. **Apply the question when stuck.** For any item that has sat undone for more than a week, sit with it and ask "what is the very next physical action?" Push until the answer is a specific, visible step you could start within 60 seconds.

3. **Make it contextual.** Once you have your next actions, distribute them to context lists. You do not need to think about @phone actions when you are at your desk, and vice versa.

4. **Update after each completion.** The moment a next action is completed, the corresponding project must get a new next action. If you complete "email Sarah" and the project is now waiting for her reply, the new action goes on the waiting-for list: "Waiting: Sarah's reply on budget dates, expected by Friday."

5. **Pre-define next actions during weekly review.** Every project without a next action is a stalled project. The weekly review exists partly to ensure every project always has an identified next action.

## Examples

**Example 1: The stalled report**
A consultant has "write quarterly report" on his list for three weeks. The GTD diagnosis: that is not a next action. Running the question: "What's the very next physical action?" Answer: "Open the Q2 data folder and review the sales figures." That item goes on @computer. He opens his laptop, sees it, does it in ten minutes. The report is no longer stuck.

**Example 2: The awkward conversation**
A manager has "deal with performance issue — James" on her list. She avoids it daily. Next physical action: "Schedule 30-minute 1:1 with James for this week — open calendar and send invite." That is something she can do in 90 seconds. The avoidance was driven not by difficulty but by undefined action.

## Anti-Patterns

**Multi-step actions masquerading as single steps.** "Draft the proposal" sounds specific but may contain: gathering data, choosing a format, writing an outline, drafting, editing. The actual next action might be: "Open a new document and write the proposed scope in one paragraph."

**Next actions without context.** A flat list of 80 next actions with no context assignment is overwhelming. The context is what makes the action executable at the right time.

**Defining next actions without doing them.** Some GTD practitioners spend more time defining actions than taking them. The clarification step serves the engagement step — not the other way around.
