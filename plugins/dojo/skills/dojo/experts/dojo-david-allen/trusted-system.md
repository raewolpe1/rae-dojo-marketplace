## When to Use

Use when the user reports chronic mental background noise — the nagging feeling that something is being forgotten, or the inability to be fully present in any task. Also when they are sceptical that an external system could replace mental tracking.

## Fails When

- The system is not complete (some commitments still live only in the head)
- The system is not reviewed regularly (the weekly review is skipped)
- The system is too complex to maintain consistently
- The user does not trust their own system and keeps re-checking mentally

## Core Concept

The fundamental promise of GTD is this: if you have a trusted external system that captures 100% of your open loops and you review it regularly, your mind can relax completely. This state is what Allen calls **mind like water**.

The metaphor comes from martial arts. Water responds to any force with exactly the right counter-force — no more, no less — and then immediately returns to stillness. The mind can work the same way. A thrown pebble produces proportional ripples, then calm. But only if there is nothing else already disturbing the surface. Every uncaptured commitment is a stone already sitting in the water. Every unreviewed system is debris accumulating at the edges.

**Why the mind holds on.** The brain is not irrational when it recycles half-formed tasks. It recycles them because it does not trust that the information is safe elsewhere. The moment you have an external system that you trust completely, the brain receives what Allen calls a "handshake" — a signal that it no longer needs to keep the item in working memory. Without this handshake, the mind will keep reminding you regardless of what your task app contains.

**What "trusted" requires.** Three things. First, the system must be **complete** — every open loop, commitment, and intention must be in it. A system that holds 90% of your commitments forces your brain to maintain the other 10%, which contaminates the whole. Second, the system must be **current** — regularly reviewed and updated so that nothing in it is stale, completed but unchecked, or no longer relevant. Third, it must be **accessible** — available when and where you need it, with the right level of context to act on.

**The paradox of control.** People assume that a detailed external system will make them feel more constrained. The opposite is true. A complete trusted system produces a feeling of freedom — because it defines the edges of your world clearly and lets you engage fully with whatever is in front of you without ambient guilt about what is not.

## How to Apply

1. **Do a complete mind sweep.** Sit down with a pad and capture every single open loop in your life: every project, every promise, every worry, every thing you have been meaning to do. This usually produces 50–200 items. The act of externalising everything is itself the first handshake with your brain.

2. **Process and organise.** Take every captured item through the clarify step until the system contains only well-defined projects with next actions, well-defined reference material, and dated calendar entries.

3. **Conduct your first weekly review.** Check every item in the system. Confirm it is current, correctly categorised, and has a next action assigned. This review cements the brain's trust in the system.

4. **Test the handshake.** Notice how you feel after a complete capture and processing session. Most people report an immediate reduction in background anxiety — not because any work was done, but because the brain now knows the inventory is safe.

5. **Maintain the trust.** The weekly review is how trust is sustained. Each review renews the handshake. Skip it for two weeks and the brain begins reclaiming storage, which is why falling off the system feels sudden even though the erosion is gradual.

## Examples

**Example 1: The midnight-worrying professional**
A consultant wakes at 2 a.m. cycling through a mental list of things she might be forgetting. This is not an anxiety disorder — it is a symptom of an incomplete external system. Her brain does not trust the to-do list because it is partial. The GTD intervention: a full mind sweep to get every open loop out of her head, followed by processing. After one complete cycle, the 2 a.m. cycling typically stops.

**Example 2: The present-moment problem**
A manager finds he cannot be fully present in meetings because his mind wanders to other commitments. This is the classic cost of an untrustworthy system — the brain multi-tasks involuntarily. When his system is complete and reviewed, his mind can rest in the meeting because it knows that nothing is being lost.

## Anti-Patterns

**Partial capture.** Keeping "just the important things" in the system while tracking minor commitments mentally. The brain cannot distinguish important from minor when generating anxiety — both produce the same cognitive load.

**The "good enough" review.** Skimming the weekly review or skipping it when busy. Ironically, the busier you are, the more important the review. Skipping it when overwhelmed is exactly backwards.

**Complexity as a trust substitute.** Building elaborate system architectures to feel in control, without ever doing the complete initial mind sweep that gives the system real content. A sophisticated empty system produces no handshake.
