## When to Use

Use when someone is productive at the task level but feels disconnected from meaning, when priorities seem unclear, or when they need a framework for thinking at multiple altitudes simultaneously — from daily actions to life purpose.

## Fails When

- Horizons are treated as a one-time exercise rather than a regular reference
- Higher horizons are never translated into lower ones (vision without projects)
- Lower horizons are ignored in favour of the inspirational (purpose without next actions)

## Core Concept

The Horizons of Focus is Allen's model for thinking about commitments at six different altitudes, from immediate actions to lifetime purpose. The key insight is that effective work requires attention at all levels — not just the daily task list, and not just lofty goals. Most productivity failures occur because people operate exclusively at one altitude, ignoring the others.

**The six horizons:**

- **Runway (Ground / Current Actions):** What you are doing right now and today. Next actions, calls to make, emails to send. The immediate operational level.

- **10,000 feet (Current Projects):** All the multi-step outcomes you are committed to in the near term. Your projects list. Typically 30-90 day horizon.

- **20,000 feet (Areas of Focus and Responsibility):** The key roles and responsibilities that define your professional and personal life. Job description, key relationships, health, finances, professional development. These are ongoing standards to maintain, not outcomes to complete. They generate projects.

- **30,000 feet (One- to Two-Year Goals):** Where do you want to be in one to two years? What goals, outcomes, or changes do you want to have accomplished? These generate areas of focus and projects.

- **40,000 feet (Three- to Five-Year Vision):** Longer-range thinking about direction. Organisational strategy, career trajectories, life choices. These inform goals and priorities.

- **50,000 feet and above (Purpose and Principles):** Why do you exist? What are your values? What is your life's purpose? This is the highest altitude, rarely explicit but always influential. Everything below it should be in service of this.

**Moving between altitudes.** The model is not meant to be addressed from the top down every time. Most GTD practice happens at the runway and projects levels. But the higher horizons provide context that shapes which projects matter, which areas of focus to prioritise, and which daily actions actually move the needle. Periodically, a higher-altitude review is essential to ensure alignment between what you are doing and why.

**Alignment is the goal.** The system is working when a clear thread runs from ground to sky: your daily actions serve your projects, which serve your areas of focus, which reflect your goals, which embody your vision, which express your purpose. When something on the runway does not connect to any higher horizon, it may not belong there at all.

## How to Apply

1. **Map your current horizons.** For each altitude, spend time writing out what is there. What are all your current next actions? Projects? Areas of responsibility? Goals? Vision? Principles?

2. **Check for alignment.** Are your projects actually serving your goals and areas of focus? Are your goals connected to your longer-term vision? If not, something is either missing or misaligned.

3. **Look for gaps.** Areas of responsibility without projects may be neglected. Goals without associated projects are aspirations only. Identify and address these gaps.

4. **Review higher horizons periodically.** Ground and projects are reviewed weekly. Areas of focus, goals, and vision benefit from quarterly or annual review. Purpose and principles rarely need formal review but should be available as a reference.

5. **Use higher horizons to clarify priorities.** When you feel unclear about what to do, move up a horizon: what project does this serve? What area of focus? What goal? This often immediately clarifies priority.

## Examples

**Example 1: The busy but directionless professional**
An executive had a packed task list and over 40 projects but felt increasingly hollow about her work. A horizons review revealed that her 30,000-foot goal of transitioning to non-profit leadership was completely unrepresented at the project level — not one project touched it. Identifying this gap let her add two concrete projects that moved her toward her actual goal while maintaining current commitments.

**Example 2: The idealist without traction**
A social entrepreneur had clear purpose (50,000 feet) and an inspiring vision (40,000 feet) but almost no projects at the 10,000-foot level. His "system" was inspiration without action. Working from the top down, he translated his vision into goals, then goals into areas of focus, then areas of focus into a concrete list of projects — each with next actions.

## Anti-Patterns

**Living only on the runway.** Executing tasks all day without asking whether those tasks serve anything higher. High activity, low impact. Common in reactive work cultures.

**Living only at altitude.** Spending time on vision and purpose without connecting them to current projects and actions. Inspirational but ineffective. Common in planning-heavy organisations that never execute.

**Treating horizons as a one-time exercise.** Doing a horizons review once during a GTD setup and never returning to it. The higher horizons need periodic review to remain relevant as life and work change.
