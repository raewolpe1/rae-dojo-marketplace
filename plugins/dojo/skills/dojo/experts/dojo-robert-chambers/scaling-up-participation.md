# Scaling Up Participation

## When to Use

- When the user is grappling with how to scale participatory methods beyond pilot projects.
- When the institutionalisation of participatory approaches is being planned.
- When the tension between scale and quality of participation needs honest examination.

## Fails When

- **The user needs small-scale facilitation guidance.** This file addresses the scale challenge, not the practice of facilitation.
- **The context doesn't require scale.** Not every participatory process needs to be scaled.

## Core Concept

Scaling up participation is the central dilemma of Chambers's later career. PRA works brilliantly in small-scale, well-facilitated settings — but development operates at national and international scale. How do you take an approach that depends on skilled facilitation, genuine power-sharing, and contextual adaptation and make it work across thousands of communities simultaneously?

Chambers acknowledges the danger candidly: when participatory methods are scaled up rapidly, quality almost always declines. Facilitators are trained in methods but not in attitudes. Institutions adopt the tools but not the philosophy. Standardised PRA packages replace context-specific facilitation. Participation becomes a bureaucratic requirement — a box to tick — rather than a genuine process of community empowerment. The very act of institutionalising participation can strip it of what makes it work.

The evidence supports this concern. Large-scale participatory programmes — India's watershed programme, the World Bank's Community Driven Development portfolio, various national PRA programmes in Africa and Asia — have consistently shown that quality varies enormously depending on facilitation quality, institutional commitment, and local conditions. Where facilitation is skilled and institutions are genuinely committed, participation produces remarkable results. Where facilitation is mechanical and institutions are going through the motions, participation produces paperwork.

Chambers's response to the scaling dilemma is characteristically nuanced. He does not argue against scaling. He argues for scaling that protects quality: investing heavily in facilitator training (attitudes first, methods second); building institutional cultures that value learning and adaptation; creating quality assurance mechanisms that assess the process of participation, not just its outputs; and accepting that genuine participation at scale is slower, messier, and more expensive than bureaucratic participation — but produces better results.

## How to Apply

1. **Train attitudes before methods.** Facilitator training that teaches PRA tools without transforming professional attitudes produces extractive participation at scale. Invest in experiential, reflective training.
2. **Build in quality assurance.** Monitor the quality of participation itself — not just whether participatory activities happened, but whether they were genuine. Use community feedback, peer observation, and reflective practice.
3. **Accept variation.** Scaled participation will look different in different places. Resist the urge to standardise. Standardised participation is a contradiction in terms.
4. **Invest in mentoring.** New facilitators learn more from experienced practitioners than from training manuals. Build mentoring relationships and communities of practice.

## Examples

**Situation:** The South African government's Integrated Development Planning (IDP) process requires every municipality to conduct community participation as part of its five-year planning cycle. This means approximately 257 municipalities must conduct participatory processes with their communities, often covering dozens of wards each. In practice, many IDP community meetings consist of officials presenting draft plans and asking for comments from a room dominated by a few vocal residents.

**Application:** Chambers would recognise this as the classic scaling problem. The IDP's participatory requirement is a significant institutional achievement — it embeds community participation in the legal framework of local government. But the quality of participation varies from genuine engagement (in municipalities with committed leadership and skilled facilitators) to tokenistic box-ticking (in municipalities where the process is a compliance exercise). To improve quality at scale, Chambers would recommend several interventions. First, invest in ward committee training that focuses on attitudes and facilitation skills, not just legal requirements. Second, use diverse PRA methods rather than the standard public meeting format — community mapping, priority ranking, seasonal analysis — to enable broader and deeper participation. Third, create community feedback mechanisms that allow residents to assess the quality of the participatory process itself (not just the IDP document). Fourth, establish peer learning networks between municipalities — those doing genuine participation can mentor those doing tokenistic participation. Fifth, accept that genuine participation in 257 municipalities will require significant investment in facilitation capacity and institutional change — there is no cheap shortcut.

**Result:** Transform IDP community participation from a compliance exercise to a genuine process by investing in facilitation quality, methodological diversity, and institutional culture change across municipalities.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Standardise participatory methods for scale.
**Why:** The moment participation becomes a standardised procedure, it ceases to be participation. Scaling requires common principles and quality standards, not standardised methods. Each community's process should be adapted to its context.

**Don't:** Blame failure of scaled participation on the concept of participation.
**Why:** When participation fails at scale, the usual response is "participation doesn't work." But the failure is typically in how it was scaled — mechanical training, institutional lip service, insufficient investment — not in the concept itself. The answer to bad participation at scale is better participation at scale, not abandonment of participation.
