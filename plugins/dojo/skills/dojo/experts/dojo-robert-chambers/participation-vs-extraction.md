# Participation vs Extraction

## When to Use

- When the user needs to distinguish genuine participation from extractive practices dressed in participatory language.
- When participatory methods are being used instrumentally — to collect data rather than to empower communities.
- When the ethics of community engagement in research or programme design need examination.

## Fails When

- **Some extraction is legitimate.** Research requires data collection; the question is how, not whether.
- **The user needs methods for participatory monitoring.** This file covers the principle; see other files for methods.

## Core Concept

Chambers draws a sharp distinction between participation and extraction. Extraction is what happens when professionals use participatory tools to collect data for their own purposes — the community draws the map, but the professional takes it away. The community ranks its priorities, but the professional writes the report. The community's time and knowledge are used to produce outputs that serve professional and institutional needs, not community needs.

Genuine participation is the opposite: the community analyses its own situation, for its own purposes, using its own criteria. The outputs belong to the community. The analysis informs community action. The professional's role is to facilitate, not to extract — to help the community do what it wants to do, not to use the community to do what the professional wants to do.

The distinction matters because extraction disguised as participation is worse than honest extraction. A conventional survey is extractive, but everyone knows it — the researcher takes data, the respondent gives time, and there is no pretence of empowerment. But a "participatory" workshop that uses PRA tools to extract data creates a false impression of empowerment while still serving professional interests. Community members invest time and energy in an exercise they believe will influence decisions, only to find that the decisions were already made, or that their analysis was translated into a professional report that bears little resemblance to what they said. This breeds cynicism and makes future genuine participation harder.

Chambers identifies several warning signs of extractive participation: the facilitator has a predetermined agenda; the outputs leave with the professionals; the community is not told how its analysis will be used; the process generates data but no action; community members are not involved in interpretation or reporting; and the same communities are subjected to repeated participatory exercises without any visible result.

## How to Apply

1. **Apply the ownership test.** Ask: who owns the outputs of this process? If the answer is the professional team, the process is extractive regardless of what methods are used.
2. **Apply the action test.** Ask: will this process lead to action that the community wants? If the only outcome is a report for the donor or the government, the process is extractive.
3. **Apply the return test.** Ask: will the findings be returned to the community in a form they can use? If not, the community's knowledge has been taken without reciprocity.
4. **Be honest about your purpose.** If you need data for a professional report, say so. Don't dress extraction in participatory clothing. Honest data collection is preferable to fake participation.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African NGO receives funding to conduct a "participatory baseline assessment" for a new food security programme. The donor requires a baseline report with specific indicators. The NGO plans community workshops using PRA methods — mapping, seasonal calendars, wealth ranking — to generate the baseline data. The workshops are scheduled for two days in each community, after which the team will return to Johannesburg to write the baseline report.

**Application:** Chambers would identify this as extraction in participatory clothing. The PRA methods are being used to collect data against donor-defined indicators, not to enable community analysis. The community's maps, calendars, and rankings will be translated into the donor's logframe categories. The outputs will leave with the NGO team. The community will not see the baseline report. The community's involvement will not influence the programme design, which has already been determined by the proposal. The workshops will create an expectation of participation that the programme structure cannot fulfil. Chambers would recommend honesty: if the purpose is a baseline report, use a well-designed survey and be transparent about the purpose. Or, if genuine participation is intended, redesign the process: let the community's analysis shape the programme design (not just generate baseline data), leave the outputs with the community, return to share the report, and create mechanisms for community input throughout the programme — not just at baseline.

**Result:** Either be honest about extraction (and do it well and respectfully) or commit to genuine participation (and accept that this means the community's analysis may change the programme design).

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume that using PRA methods makes a process participatory.
**Why:** PRA methods are tools. They can be used extractively or participatorily depending on the purpose, the power dynamics, and who controls the process. Participation is defined by who benefits from the process, not by which tools are used.

**Don't:** Blame communities for "participation fatigue."
**Why:** When communities are tired of "participatory" processes, it is usually because they have been subjected to multiple rounds of extraction disguised as participation. The fatigue is rational — they have learned that their participation doesn't lead to action. The solution is genuine participation, not more sophisticated extraction.
