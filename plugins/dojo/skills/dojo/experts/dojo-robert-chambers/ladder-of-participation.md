# Ladder of Participation

## When to Use

- When the user needs to assess the quality or depth of participation in a development process.
- When the distinction between tokenistic and genuine participation requires a framework.
- When a typology of participation levels is needed for programme design, evaluation, or critique.

## Fails When

- **The user needs Arnstein's original ladder in detail.** Chambers builds on and modifies the concept; for the original 1969 formulation, go to Arnstein directly.
- **A binary assessment is sufficient.** Sometimes the question is simply "is this participatory or not?" and a typology adds unnecessary complexity.

## Core Concept

The idea of a "ladder" or spectrum of participation — from tokenistic to empowering — draws on Sherry Arnstein's 1969 typology but has been significantly developed by Chambers and others working in international development. The core insight is that "participation" is not a single thing but ranges across a spectrum from manipulation to self-mobilisation, and that most of what passes for participation in development practice clusters at the lower end of the spectrum.

Chambers identifies several levels, drawing on and adapting work by Jules Pretty and others. At the bottom: manipulation and passive participation — people are told what has been decided, or are present at meetings but have no influence. Next: participation by consultation — people are asked for their views, but the professionals decide which views to incorporate and how. Then: participation for material incentives — people contribute labour or resources in exchange for food, cash, or materials. This is participation in the programme but not in its design or governance. Further up: functional participation — people form groups or committees to meet project objectives, but the objectives were set externally. Then: interactive participation — people participate in joint analysis and planning, professional and community knowledge are combined, and groups take control over local decisions. At the top: self-mobilisation — people take initiatives independently of external institutions, develop contacts for resources and technical advice, but retain control over how resources are used.

Chambers makes several critical observations about this spectrum. First, most development practice operates at the lower levels while claiming to operate at the higher levels. Consultation is called participation. Material incentives are called community ownership. Functional committees are called community-driven development. The language of participation consistently oversells the reality. Second, moving up the ladder requires a genuine transfer of power, which institutions resist. Institutions benefit from participation at the lower levels — it provides data, legitimacy, and compliance — without the cost of sharing control. Third, the appropriate level depends on context. Self-mobilisation is not always the goal; sometimes interactive participation is the most realistic and productive level. The problem is not that all participation should be at the highest level but that it should be at the level it claims to be.

## How to Apply

1. **Assess honestly where your process falls on the spectrum.** Use the typology to diagnose the actual level of participation, not the intended or claimed level.
2. **Match language to reality.** If the process is consultation, call it consultation. If it is information provision, call it that. Don't use the language of empowerment for processes that are extractive.
3. **Design for the appropriate level.** Not every process needs to be at the highest level of the spectrum. But every process should be at the level it claims to be — and should be transparent about its actual level.
4. **Identify the barriers to moving up.** If a process is stuck at the consultation level when it should be at the interactive level, ask: what is preventing the shift? Usually, the answer is institutional reluctance to share power.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African district municipality claims that its water services development plan was developed "through extensive community participation." The user needs to assess the quality of this participation claim.

**Application:** Chambers would apply the participation spectrum to assess what actually happened. The municipality held public meetings in each ward (the legal minimum for IDP consultation). At these meetings, officials presented the draft water services plan and asked for comments. The meetings were held in English, in municipal halls during working hours. Attendance was dominated by ward committee members and local political figures. Comments were recorded in meeting minutes but there is no evidence of how they influenced the final plan. Applying the typology: this is participation by consultation at best — people were asked for their views on a plan designed by professionals. It is not interactive participation — the community did not participate in the analysis of water needs, the assessment of service options, or the design of the infrastructure plan. It is certainly not self-mobilisation — the community had no control over the process or its outcomes. The claim of "extensive community participation" oversells the reality by several levels. A genuinely participatory water services plan would start with community mapping of water sources, community analysis of water needs by season and by household type, community priority ranking of water service options, and community involvement in the technical design process — combining community knowledge of water realities with professional knowledge of engineering and hydrology. This would constitute interactive participation and would produce a plan that reflects community priorities rather than professional assumptions.

**Result:** Use the participation typology to provide an honest assessment of participation quality, and recommend specific changes needed to move from consultation to interactive participation.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the ladder as a linear progression.
**Why:** Participation does not necessarily progress from lower to higher levels over time. Without deliberate institutional change, processes can remain stuck at consultation level indefinitely — or can move backward when political conditions change or institutional commitment wanes.

**Don't:** Assume higher is always better.
**Why:** The appropriate level depends on context, purpose, and capacity. A well-conducted consultation process that honestly feeds into professional decision-making may be more productive than a poorly facilitated self-mobilisation process that produces confusion. The key is matching claims to reality and matching methods to the genuine level of power-sharing the institution is willing to commit to.
