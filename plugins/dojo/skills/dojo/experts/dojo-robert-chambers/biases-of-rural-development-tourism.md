# Biases of Rural Development Tourism

## When to Use

- When the user needs to understand how professional field practices create systematic biases.
- When a field visit or assessment methodology needs to be checked for bias.
- When data collection design needs to account for the ways professionals systematically miss reality.

## Fails When

- **The user is designing desk-based research.** These biases apply to field-based work.
- **The biases have already been addressed.** Don't belabour the point when the methodology is well designed.

## Core Concept

"Rural development tourism" is Chambers's devastating term for the typical field visit by a development professional: a brief trip from the capital, on paved roads, in the dry season, stopping at accessible villages, talking to male leaders, seeing the project site, and returning with confidence about what is happening. Every element of this normal practice introduces systematic bias.

Chambers catalogued six interlocking biases. **Spatial bias**: professionals travel on main roads, visit accessible communities, and see what is visible from the road. Remote communities, hidden settlements, and areas accessible only on foot or in the wet season are excluded. **Project bias**: visits focus on project sites, which are by definition areas of intervention. Areas without projects — often the most neglected — are not visited. **Person bias**: professionals talk to the accessible (those near the road), the articulate (those who speak the professionals' language), the powerful (leaders, elites), and the male. Women, children, the elderly, the disabled, the very poor, and the socially excluded are systematically missed. **Seasonal bias**: professionals visit in the dry season when travel is easy, fields look productive, and diseases are less prevalent. The hungry season, the flood season, and the disease season — when poverty is most acute — are avoided. **Diplomatic bias**: politeness prevents hard questions. Officials show success stories and hide failures. Professionals collude in this because challenging the narrative is uncomfortable. **Professional bias**: each professional sees through their disciplinary lens — the economist sees markets, the agronomist sees crops, the engineer sees infrastructure. No one sees the whole.

These biases compound each other. A dry-season visit on a main road to a project village, meeting the male village leader, produces a picture of rural reality that is systematically wrong — it is biased toward the accessible, the visible, the articulate, the powerful, and the seasonal best case.

## How to Apply

1. **Check every field methodology against the six biases.** For each bias, ask: how does our methodology address this? If it doesn't, redesign.
2. **Go off-road.** Visit the most remote, least accessible communities — not just the project sites and the roadside villages.
3. **Visit in the worst season.** If you only visit in the dry season, you are seeing the best-case scenario. Visit during the hungry season, the flood season, the disease season.
4. **Seek the excluded.** Specifically seek out women, children, the elderly, the disabled, the very poor, ethnic minorities, and others who are excluded from normal consultations.
5. **Stay longer.** Brief visits reinforce every bias. The longer you stay, the more you see past the official story.

## Examples

**Situation:** A monitoring visit to a South African rural development programme in Limpopo takes place in March (after the harvest, when conditions are relatively good), follows the N1 highway with detours to project sites, meets with ward councillors and project committee chairpersons, and concludes that the programme is progressing satisfactorily.

**Application:** Chambers would identify every bias at work. Spatial: the N1 corridor and project sites were visited; remote villages off the tar road were not. Seasonal: March is post-harvest; conditions in October (pre-harvest, peak hunger) would reveal a different reality. Person: ward councillors and committee chairs are the most powerful local actors; women subsistence farmers, elderly pension-dependent households, and child-headed households were not consulted. Project: project sites show intervention areas; the communities not reached by the programme were invisible. The monitoring team's conclusion of "satisfactory progress" reflects the reality of the best-off, most accessible, most powerful people at the best time of year. To see the actual reality of the programme's impact on the poorest, the team would need to visit remote villages, in October, talking specifically to the most vulnerable households, about their experience — which is likely quite different from the picture the current visit produced.

**Result:** Redesign the monitoring methodology to counteract each of the six biases — different locations, different seasons, different informants — and compare the resulting picture with the one produced by the biased methodology.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume that awareness of bias eliminates it.
**Why:** Knowing about bias is necessary but not sufficient. The biases are structural — they are built into the logistics of professional travel and the social dynamics of field interaction. They require structural counter-measures, not just awareness.

**Don't:** Skip field visits because they are biased.
**Why:** The answer to biased field visits is better field visits, not no field visits. Desk-based analysis has its own biases and cannot substitute for direct engagement with communities.
