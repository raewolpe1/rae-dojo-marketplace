# Paradigms of Development

## When to Use

- When the user needs Chambers's critique of dominant development paradigms and his alternative.
- When blueprint, top-down, or technocratic approaches to development are the problem.
- When the contrast between "things" paradigms and "people" paradigms is relevant.

## Fails When

- **The user needs a comprehensive history of development thought.** Chambers offers one perspective, not a survey.
- **The paradigm critique is too abstract for the problem at hand.** Sometimes the issue is practical, not paradigmatic.

## Core Concept

Chambers distinguishes two fundamental paradigms in development. The first — which he variously calls the "blueprint" approach, the "things" paradigm, or the "normal professional" approach — treats development as a technical problem. It centres on things: infrastructure, technology, production, GDP. It works through standardised designs, predetermined targets, top-down implementation, and professional control. It values quantification, replicability, and efficiency. It assumes that problems can be diagnosed by experts, solutions can be designed centrally, and implementation can be controlled through management systems.

The second — which he calls the "process" approach, the "people" paradigm, or the "new professional" approach — treats development as a human process. It centres on people: their capabilities, their choices, their wellbeing, their agency. It works through adaptive management, local learning, participatory processes, and iterative adjustment. It values diversity, responsiveness, and empowerment. It assumes that local people understand their own situations best, that solutions must be adapted to context, and that implementation must be flexible and learning-oriented.

Chambers argues that the blueprint paradigm dominates because it serves professional interests — it is compatible with centralised planning, quantitative accountability, short project cycles, and professional control. The process paradigm is threatening because it requires professionals to give up control, to accept uncertainty, to learn rather than teach, and to be accountable to communities rather than to headquarters.

The paradigm distinction is not absolute. Chambers acknowledges that some aspects of development require blueprint approaches — you cannot build a bridge through participatory process alone. But he argues that the things paradigm has colonised domains where the people paradigm should prevail: livelihoods, governance, community development, social protection, and any area where the complexity of human behaviour makes standardised solutions ineffective.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose the paradigm.** For any programme or policy, ask: is this fundamentally a "things" problem or a "people" problem? Does the design reflect the appropriate paradigm?
2. **Check for paradigm mismatch.** When a programme designed on blueprint principles is failing in a complex human context, the paradigm may be the problem — not the implementation.
3. **Advocate for process approaches.** In livelihoods, governance, community development, and social protection, argue for adaptive, participatory, learning-oriented approaches rather than predetermined, target-driven, professionally controlled ones.
4. **Accept the discomfort.** Process approaches are uncomfortable for professionals and institutions because they require giving up the illusion of control. This discomfort is a feature, not a bug.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African government department designs a national rural livelihoods programme using a blueprint approach: standardised interventions (poultry farming, vegetable gardens, sewing cooperatives), predetermined targets (10,000 households per province), centralised procurement of inputs, and quarterly reporting against output indicators. After three years, the programme has met most of its targets but evaluations show minimal sustained livelihood improvement.

**Application:** Chambers would diagnose a paradigm mismatch. Livelihoods are a "people" problem — they depend on local markets, local skills, local opportunities, seasonal dynamics, household composition, risk profiles, and individual capabilities. But the programme was designed as a "things" problem — standardised inputs, predetermined activities, centralised control. The poultry project fails in areas without reliable feed supply. The vegetable gardens fail where water is scarce. The sewing cooperatives fail where there is no market for the products. The programme met its targets (things delivered) but missed its purpose (lives improved) because it imposed standardised solutions on diverse contexts. A process approach would start differently: what are households already doing for livelihoods? What local opportunities exist? What constraints do households face? What would they do differently if they had small amounts of flexible support? The answers would vary radically between locations, producing a diverse portfolio of locally appropriate interventions rather than a uniform national programme — harder to manage, harder to report on, but far more likely to improve livelihoods.

**Result:** Redesign the programme from blueprint to process — replace standardised interventions with flexible, locally determined livelihood support, accepting the management complexity this entails.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat "blueprint bad, process good" as an absolute rule.
**Why:** Some development challenges genuinely require technical, standardised approaches. The problem is not that blueprint approaches exist but that they dominate in contexts where process approaches would be more effective.

**Don't:** Use paradigm critique to avoid accountability.
**Why:** Process approaches are not an excuse for vagueness. They require different forms of accountability — learning-oriented, participatory, adaptive — but they still require accountability. A programme that is "participatory" but produces no discernible improvement for anyone is not a success.
