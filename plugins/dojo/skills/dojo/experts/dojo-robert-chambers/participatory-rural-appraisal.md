# Participatory Rural Appraisal

## When to Use

- When the user needs to design or conduct a participatory assessment using PRA/PLA methods.
- When the principles underlying participatory methods need explanation.
- When the distinction between extractive data collection and participatory analysis matters.

## Fails When

- **The user needs a step-by-step manual for specific PRA tools.** This file covers concepts and principles; detailed tool guides are practice resources.
- **The context is urban and the "rural" framing is misleading.** PRA methods work in urban settings too — the principles transcend the rural origin.

## Core Concept

Participatory Rural Appraisal (PRA) — later broadened to Participatory Learning and Action (PLA) — is a family of methods and approaches that Chambers helped develop and popularise from the late 1980s onwards. PRA emerged from three methodological streams: activist participatory research (Paulo Freire's tradition), agroecosystem analysis, and rapid rural appraisal. Chambers synthesised these into a coherent approach centred on the principle that local people are capable of sophisticated analysis when given appropriate methods and facilitation.

The core PRA methods include: social mapping (communities draw maps of their area, marking features that matter to them); seasonal calendars (communities chart seasonal variations in income, food, disease, workload, and other dimensions); transect walks (systematic walks through an area, observing and discussing what is found); wealth ranking (communities classify households by wellbeing using their own criteria); institutional analysis (Venn diagrams or matrices showing relationships between community organisations and external institutions); and historical timelines (communities reconstruct their history, identifying key events and changes).

But Chambers insists that PRA is not primarily about methods. It is about three things: behaviour (the facilitator's attitudes and actions matter more than the tools — humility, respect, patience, willingness to learn); ownership (the analysis belongs to the community, not the facilitator — they draw the maps, they do the ranking, they keep the outputs); and power (PRA shifts analytical power from professionals to local people — "they can do it" is the fundamental discovery of PRA practice).

The evolution from PRA to PLA reflected a broadening of the approach: from rural to all settings, from appraisal to ongoing learning and action, and from a discrete event to a continuous process of community-led analysis and planning.

## How to Apply

1. **Start with behaviour, not methods.** Train facilitators in attitudes (listening, humility, respect) before training them in methods (mapping, ranking, diagramming).
2. **Hand over the stick.** Literally and metaphorically, let local people do the analysis. They draw the map. They rank the households. They identify the priorities. The facilitator asks questions and learns.
3. **Triangulate.** Use multiple methods, multiple informant groups, and multiple facilitators. PRA's validity comes from triangulation across methods and perspectives.
4. **Leave the outputs with the community.** The maps, diagrams, and calendars belong to the community. If you need copies, photograph them — but the originals stay.
5. **Use PRA for action, not extraction.** The purpose of PRA is to enable communities to analyse their own situations and plan their own actions. If the only outcome is data for a professional report, PRA has been misused.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African municipal planning team needs to understand community priorities for a new ward-based planning process. The normal approach would be a household survey or a community meeting where officials present options and ask for feedback.

**Application:** Chambers would recommend a PRA-based approach instead. Social mapping: community members draw their ward, identifying infrastructure, services, problem areas, and assets. This produces a richer picture than any survey and gives the community analytical ownership. Seasonal calendar: community members chart how problems vary across the year — water shortages in October, flooding in January, highest unemployment in winter. This reveals temporal dynamics that a single-point survey misses. Wealth ranking: community members classify households by wellbeing (using their own criteria, not the income poverty line), revealing who is most vulnerable and why. Institutional analysis: community members map which organisations serve them well and which don't, and where the gaps are. The planning team learns from the community's analysis rather than imposing its own framework. The outputs stay with the community for their own planning, and copies inform the municipal IDP process. The community has done the analysis; the municipality has learned from it; and the process has shifted power from officials to residents.

**Result:** Replace extractive surveying with participatory analysis that produces richer information, builds community analytical capacity, and shifts power toward residents.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Reduce PRA to a toolkit.
**Why:** PRA is a philosophy of engagement, not a bag of tricks. Using PRA tools with extractive attitudes produces extractive results with participatory decoration.

**Don't:** Do PRA once and never return.
**Why:** One-off PRA exercises are extractive if they don't lead to action. Genuine PRA is part of an ongoing process of community analysis, planning, action, and reflection.
