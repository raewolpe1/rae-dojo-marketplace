# Chambers's Critique of RCTs

## When to Use

- When the user needs Chambers's specific critique of randomised controlled trials in development.
- When the tension between experimental rigour and participatory relevance is being debated.
- When participatory alternatives to RCTs need articulation.

## Fails When

- **The user needs a technical critique of RCT methodology.** Chambers's critique is philosophical and political, not statistical. For technical critiques, see Cartwright or Heckman.
- **An RCT is genuinely the appropriate method.** Chambers does not reject all experimentation; he rejects the hierarchy that places RCTs above all other evidence.

## Core Concept

Chambers's critique of RCTs is distinctive because it comes not from econometrics (like Heckman) or philosophy of science (like Cartwright) but from development practice and ethics. His objection is not primarily that RCTs are statistically flawed but that they embody and reinforce the power relations that participation seeks to transform.

First, the power critique. RCTs are designed by professionals, controlled by professionals, and analysed by professionals. Communities are subjects of the experiment, not agents of their own analysis. The randomisation itself is an exercise of power — professionals decide who receives an intervention and who does not, often without meaningful community consent. The ethical framework of RCTs treats informed consent as sufficient, but Chambers argues that consent given in the context of extreme poverty and power asymmetry is not genuinely free. A community that needs a borehole will agree to be randomised because the alternative is nothing — but this is not the same as freely choosing to participate in an experiment.

Second, the knowledge critique. RCTs answer one question — "what is the average treatment effect?" — and ignore the questions that communities and practitioners most need answered: why does the intervention work where it works? Why does it fail where it fails? What adaptations would make it work better? Who benefits most and least, and why? These questions require the kind of contextual, qualitative, participatory inquiry that RCTs by design exclude.

Third, the practical critique. RCTs take years, cost millions, and produce findings that are specific to the context where they were conducted. By the time results are published, the context has changed. Meanwhile, participatory methods can produce actionable local knowledge in days at minimal cost — knowledge that is specific to the community, owned by the community, and immediately useful for community action.

Chambers does not reject experimentation entirely. He argues for reversing the hierarchy: participatory methods should be the primary approach to understanding development, with experimental methods used selectively where they add specific value — not the other way around.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask the power question first.** Before designing an RCT, ask: who controls this process? Who decided the research question? Who decides what happens to the control group? If the answers all point to professionals, consider whether a participatory approach would be more ethical and more useful.
2. **Ask the relevance question.** Will the RCT answer the questions that communities and practitioners most need answered? If the answer is "it will tell us the average effect but not why, where, or for whom," consider whether other methods would be more valuable.
3. **Combine approaches.** If an RCT is warranted, embed participatory methods within it — community-defined outcome indicators, participatory process evaluation, community-led interpretation of results. Don't let the experimental design exclude community voice.
4. **Challenge the hierarchy.** When funders or reviewers insist that RCTs are the "gold standard," question the metaphor. Gold standard for whom? For answering which questions? At what cost to whom?

## Examples

**Situation:** An international research consortium proposes an RCT to evaluate a community health worker programme in rural KwaZulu-Natal. Thirty communities will be randomised to receive the programme; thirty will serve as controls, receiving the programme after the two-year study period. The primary outcome is child mortality reduction, measured through demographic surveillance.

**Application:** Chambers would raise several concerns. Ethically, withholding a health intervention from thirty communities for two years, when there is already substantial evidence that community health workers reduce child mortality, is difficult to justify — the communities in the control group will experience preventable child deaths during the study period. Politically, the randomisation treats communities as experimental units rather than as agents with their own priorities and decisions. Epistemologically, the RCT will tell us the average effect of the programme on child mortality, but it will not tell us why some communities see larger effects than others, what adaptations community health workers make that improve effectiveness, what community characteristics support or undermine the programme, or what the community health workers themselves think about what works and what doesn't. These are the questions that would inform programme improvement and scale-up. Chambers would advocate for a participatory evaluation approach: communities define their own health priorities and assessment criteria, community health workers contribute to ongoing monitoring and reflection, and the evaluation produces actionable knowledge for programme improvement — not just a publication-ready estimate of average treatment effect.

**Result:** Replace or complement the RCT with participatory evaluation methods that produce knowledge communities and practitioners can use, while respecting community agency and avoiding the ethical problems of withholding interventions.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Dismiss RCTs categorically.
**Why:** Chambers's position is nuanced — he objects to the hierarchy that places RCTs above all other evidence, not to experimentation per se. Some questions are genuinely best answered by experimental methods. The critique is of the monopoly claim, not of the method itself.

**Don't:** Treat participatory methods as inherently more rigorous.
**Why:** Participatory methods have their own quality challenges — facilitation quality, elite capture, social desirability bias. The argument is not that participatory methods are more rigorous but that they are more relevant, more ethical, and more empowering — and that these qualities matter as much as statistical rigour.
