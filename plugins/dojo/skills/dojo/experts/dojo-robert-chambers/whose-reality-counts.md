# Whose Reality Counts?

## When to Use

- When the user needs to challenge knowledge hierarchies in development practice.
- When professional knowledge is being privileged over local knowledge without justification.
- When the epistemological argument for participation needs articulation.

## Fails When

- **The knowledge in question is genuinely technical and specialist.** Not all knowledge hierarchies are unjustified.
- **The user needs practical methods for accessing local knowledge.** This file covers the argument; see participatory-rural-appraisal.md for methods.

## Core Concept

"Whose reality counts?" is the central question of Chambers's 1997 book of the same name, and it is the question he believes should precede every development decision. The argument is both epistemological (about knowledge) and political (about power).

The epistemological argument: development professionals have a particular kind of knowledge — theoretical, quantitative, abstracted, generalised. Local people have a different kind — contextual, qualitative, embodied, specific. Both are valuable, but in development practice, professional knowledge consistently trumps local knowledge. Programme designs reflect professional analysis; evaluation uses professional methods and criteria; success is measured against professional indicators. The reality that counts is the professional's reality. Chambers argues that this hierarchy is not justified by the quality of the knowledge — local knowledge is often more accurate, more detailed, and more relevant to the specific context than professional knowledge. The hierarchy is maintained by power, not by epistemology.

The political argument: the power to define reality is the deepest form of power. When professionals define what poverty means (using income thresholds), what development means (using GDP growth), and what success means (using log-frame indicators), they are exercising power over the definition of reality itself. The poor may have a completely different understanding of poverty (multidimensional, relational, seasonal, contextual), development (security, dignity, voice, capability), and success (resilience, community cohesion, children's wellbeing). But their understanding doesn't count because they don't have the power to make it count.

Chambers's prescription: reverse the knowledge hierarchy. Start by asking local people how they understand their situation, what their priorities are, what criteria they would use to assess progress. Use professional knowledge to complement, not replace, local analysis. And always ask: whose reality is being privileged in this decision, and whose is being excluded?

## How to Apply

1. **Start with local analysis.** Before bringing professional analysis to a situation, ask local people how they understand it. Their analysis is the starting point, not the validation of yours.
2. **Use multiple ways of knowing.** Combine professional knowledge (quantitative, theoretical, comparative) with local knowledge (contextual, experiential, specific). Neither is sufficient alone.
3. **Challenge the indicators.** When programme indicators are defined by professionals, ask whether they capture what matters to local people. If not, whose reality do they represent?
4. **Make the knowledge hierarchy visible.** When professional analysis contradicts local analysis, don't automatically assume the professional analysis is correct. Examine both, and ask why they differ.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African poverty assessment uses the national poverty line (upper-bound poverty line of approximately R1,335 per person per month in 2023) to classify households as poor or non-poor. A household earning R1,400 per month per person is classified as non-poor.

**Application:** Chambers would ask: whose reality is this classification capturing? The household earning R1,400 may be above the statistical poverty line but may consider itself desperately poor — because the income is irregular, because the household has medical expenses, because the breadwinner's job is insecure, because the income supports dependents not counted in the per-capita calculation, or because the cost of living in their specific location exceeds the national average. The poverty line is a professional construct — useful for aggregate measurement but potentially misleading for any specific household. If the household's own assessment of its poverty doesn't match the professional classification, whose reality counts? Chambers would argue that both are informative, but development practice that relies only on the professional measure will miss the lived reality of poverty — which is multidimensional, relational, and contextual in ways that a single monetary threshold cannot capture.

**Result:** Supplement professional poverty measures with local people's own assessments of their wellbeing, vulnerability, and priorities — and take the differences seriously.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume local knowledge is always correct.
**Why:** Chambers does not claim that local people are always right. He claims that their knowledge is systematically undervalued. The answer is not to reverse the hierarchy completely but to take both forms of knowledge seriously and examine where they agree and disagree.

**Don't:** Use "whose reality counts?" as a rhetorical weapon.
**Why:** The question is meant to prompt genuine inquiry into knowledge and power, not to shut down professional analysis. Used well, it opens a richer conversation. Used badly, it becomes a way to dismiss any form of external assessment.
