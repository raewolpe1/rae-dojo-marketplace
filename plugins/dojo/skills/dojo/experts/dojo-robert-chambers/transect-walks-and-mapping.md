# Transect Walks and Mapping

## When to Use

- When the user needs practical guidance on spatial participatory methods.
- When community mapping or transect walks are part of an assessment or planning process.
- When spatial understanding of a community's resources, constraints, and dynamics is needed.

## Fails When

- **The spatial dimension is not relevant.** Not every assessment needs spatial methods.
- **GIS or professional mapping is what's needed.** This file covers participatory spatial methods, not technical cartography.

## Core Concept

Transect walks and community mapping are among the most powerful PRA methods because they are spatial, visual, and grounded in physical reality. They counteract the biases of professional observation by structuring how a community is seen and who describes it.

A transect walk is a systematic walk through a community or landscape, usually along a cross-section (transect) that cuts across different zones — for example, from the river through the floodplain, across the village, up the hillside to the grazing land. The walk is guided by local people who explain what they see, what has changed, what works, and what doesn't. The result is a cross-section diagram showing different zones, their characteristics, and the issues affecting each. The transect reveals what a roadside visit misses: the hidden spring that has dried up, the eroded hillside that used to be productive, the informal settlement behind the ridge, the path that floods and cuts off the school.

Community mapping — where local people draw maps of their area on the ground (using sticks, stones, seeds, and other local materials) or on paper — is equally powerful. Unlike professional maps, community maps show what matters to the community: not just infrastructure and roads, but water points, gathering places, dangerous areas, sacred sites, seasonal routes, social boundaries, and problem spots. Community maps are drawn by the community, for the community, using the community's categories and priorities.

Both methods work because they are visual (people can see and discuss what is represented), spatial (they capture the physical reality that abstract indicators miss), inclusive (even people who cannot read can draw maps and point out features), and owned (the community creates the output and keeps it).

## How to Apply

1. **Let the community choose the route.** For transect walks, ask community members where the walk should go to see the full reality of their area — including the problems, not just the showcase areas.
2. **Walk with diverse guides.** Different people see different things. A woman and a man walking the same transect will point out different features. A young person and an elder will emphasise different changes.
3. **Record on the spot.** Draw the transect diagram as you walk, with community members contributing observations and explanations at each zone.
4. **For mapping, use the ground first.** Ground maps (drawn with sticks and seeds) are more accessible than paper maps and allow more people to participate simultaneously. Transfer to paper for preservation.
5. **Discuss what the map reveals.** The map itself is a starting point for analysis. Ask: what are the patterns? Where are the problems concentrated? What has changed? What resources are underused?

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African water services authority needs to plan water infrastructure for a rural community in KwaZulu-Natal. The professional approach would be: obtain a topographic map, assess population distribution from census data, identify technically optimal locations for boreholes and reservoirs.

**Application:** Chambers would recommend starting with a community transect walk and mapping exercise. Community members walk with the planning team across the settlement, from the river to the hilltop, identifying: where people currently collect water (and how far they walk), which water sources dry up in which season, where the paths become impassable in rain, which households are furthest from any water source, where livestock water and where people water, and what the community considers the priority locations for infrastructure. A community map would show all of this spatially — information that is not available on any topographic map or in any census dataset. The technical planning can then use this community-generated spatial analysis as its foundation, combining local knowledge of water needs and seasonal patterns with professional knowledge of hydrology and engineering. The result is infrastructure that serves community priorities rather than technical optimality alone.

**Result:** Combine community spatial knowledge with professional technical expertise, producing infrastructure plans grounded in local reality.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat community maps as inferior to professional maps.
**Why:** Community maps capture different information — social features, seasonal dynamics, local priorities — that professional maps miss. They are different tools for different purposes, not better or worse versions of the same thing.

**Don't:** Use participatory mapping just to validate professional plans.
**Why:** If the infrastructure locations have already been decided, doing a mapping exercise creates the appearance of participation without the reality. Map first, plan second.
