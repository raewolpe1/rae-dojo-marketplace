# Power and Development

## When to Use

- When power dynamics are distorting a development programme, process, or evaluation.
- When the user needs to analyse how power shapes development outcomes.
- When the connection between power, knowledge, and development practice needs articulation.

## Fails When

- **The power analysis needed is institutional or political economy.** Chambers addresses interpersonal and professional power; for formal political economy analysis, other frameworks are needed.
- **The user needs a technical power analysis framework.** Chambers is more philosophical than methodological on power.

## Core Concept

Power is the thread running through all of Chambers's work, though he addresses it differently from political scientists or political economists. Chambers is concerned with the power that operates in development relationships — between professionals and communities, between donors and recipients, between experts and local people, between upper and lower in every hierarchy. This is not the power of formal authority or political control but the power of definition, of framing, of deciding what counts.

Chambers identifies several dimensions of power in development. The power to define reality: who decides what poverty means, what development means, what success looks like. The power to set the agenda: who decides what questions are asked, what problems are prioritised, what solutions are considered. The power to control resources: who decides how money is spent, what conditions are attached, what accountability is required. The power to validate knowledge: who decides what counts as evidence, what methods are rigorous, what analysis is credible. And the power of convenience: the tendency for the powerful to adopt whatever framing is easiest, most comfortable, and least threatening to their position.

Chambers argues that these forms of power are largely invisible to those who hold them. Professionals do not experience their power as power — they experience it as competence, responsibility, and good practice. The donor who imposes a logframe does not see this as an exercise of power but as good management. The economist who defines poverty as income below a threshold does not see this as a power claim but as analytical rigour. Making power visible — to those who hold it — is the first step toward changing how it operates.

## How to Apply

1. **Map the power in the room.** Before any development interaction, ask: who has power here, what kind, and how does it shape what will happen? Include yourself in the analysis.
2. **Name the power of definition.** When indicators, criteria, or frameworks are proposed, ask: who defined these? Whose categories are being used? Whose are being excluded?
3. **Follow the accountability.** In any programme, ask: who is accountable to whom? If accountability flows upward (to donors, to government) but not downward (to communities), the power structure is inverted.
4. **Create conditions for the less powerful to speak.** Separate groups by gender, age, wealth, or status for discussions. The less powerful will not speak freely in the presence of the more powerful.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African donor-funded rural development programme requires quarterly reports against a logframe with indicators defined by the donor. The implementing organisation spends significant staff time collecting data against these indicators. Community members were not consulted about which indicators matter to them. The programme is rated as "on track" by the donor's assessment.

**Application:** Chambers would dissect the power relations. The donor defined the indicators — exercising the power of definition. The indicators reflect what the donor considers important (outputs delivered, targets met, money spent), not necessarily what the community considers important (whether livelihoods actually improved, whether the most vulnerable were reached, whether the community feels more capable). The implementing organisation's accountability flows upward to the donor, not downward to the community — the community has no mechanism to say "this programme is not working for us" in a way that changes anything. The quarterly reporting absorbs staff time that could be spent on community engagement — so the accountability system itself reduces programme quality. The programme is rated "on track" because it is meeting the donor's criteria, but this tells us nothing about whether it is meeting the community's criteria. The power to define success determines what success looks like. Chambers would argue for downward accountability mechanisms: community scorecards, participatory monitoring where communities define their own indicators, and processes where community assessment has consequences for programme management.

**Result:** Redesign accountability to flow downward as well as upward, giving communities the power to define and assess programme success alongside donor requirements.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Analyse power only in others.
**Why:** The most important power analysis is of your own power. Development professionals consistently underestimate the power they carry — the power of their expertise, their institutional position, their resources, and their mobility.

**Don't:** Treat power as something to be eliminated.
**Why:** Power is inherent in all social relationships. The goal is not to eliminate power but to make it visible, to redistribute it, and to ensure it serves the interests of the least powerful rather than the most powerful.
