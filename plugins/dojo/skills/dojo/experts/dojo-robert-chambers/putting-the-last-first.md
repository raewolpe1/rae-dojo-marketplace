# Putting the Last First

## When to Use

- When the user needs Chambers's foundational argument for reversing development priorities.
- When the poorest and most marginalised are being overlooked in programme design or evaluation.
- When the user needs to argue for pro-poor targeting against technocratic or efficiency arguments.

## Fails When

- **The programme is genuinely universal and the "last first" framing is not applicable.** Some interventions are appropriately universal.
- **The user needs targeting methodology.** This file covers the philosophy; targeting methods are technical tools.

## Core Concept

"Putting the last first" is Chambers's foundational principle, articulated in his 1983 book *Rural Development: Putting the Last First*. The "last" are the poorest, most marginalised, most powerless people in any development context — those who are last in line for services, last to be heard, last to benefit from growth, and first to suffer from shocks. They are "last" not by accident but by structure: the systems of development practice systematically exclude them.

The argument has three dimensions. First, an ethical dimension: development that does not reach the poorest has failed its most basic moral purpose, regardless of aggregate metrics. An average improvement that leaves the poorest behind is not development — it is growth that widens inequality. Second, an epistemological dimension: the "last" have knowledge that professionals cannot access through normal methods. Their reality is different from that of the "first" (the better-off, the accessible, the articulate), and understanding their reality requires deliberate effort. Third, a practical dimension: programmes designed around the priorities and constraints of the "last" often work better for everyone, because the poorest face the most binding constraints. Solving their problems often solves problems that affect the less poor as well.

Chambers was not naive about the difficulty of putting the last first. He recognised that the "last" are hard to reach (remote, hidden, socially excluded), hard to hear (they may lack confidence, language, or social permission to speak), and politically inconvenient (serving them may not generate political returns). But he argued that these difficulties are reasons to try harder, not excuses to give up.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask who is last.** In any development context, identify who is most marginalised, most excluded, most invisible. This requires going beyond the obvious categories — the poorest household may not be the most marginalised person within that household.
2. **Seek them out deliberately.** The "last" will not come to you. They will not attend community meetings, respond to surveys, or appear on administrative lists. You must go to them, in their places, at their times, using their languages.
3. **Design from their constraints.** Start programme design from the constraints facing the "last" — not from average conditions. If the programme works for the poorest, it will likely work for the less poor. The reverse is not true.
4. **Monitor disaggregated effects.** Average programme effects can mask the experience of the "last." Always disaggregate by relevant categories — wealth, gender, disability, location, age — and track whether the programme is reaching the most marginalised.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African municipal integrated development plan (IDP) prioritises infrastructure investment based on population density and economic return — concentrating investment in areas with the most people and the highest economic activity.

**Application:** Chambers would argue that this prioritisation systematically excludes the "last": people in remote rural areas, informal settlements on the urban periphery, and communities with low population density. These are often the poorest and most underserved communities, but they rank low on both density and economic return criteria. Putting the last first would mean asking: which communities have the least access to basic services? Where are the greatest unmet needs? Designing infrastructure investment to reach the most underserved first — even if the cost per beneficiary is higher — is both ethically right and practically sound, because it addresses the most binding constraints on human development. The IDP process itself illustrates the bias: public participation processes for IDPs are held in accessible locations, announced through formal channels, and conducted in dominant languages. The "last" — those without transport, without access to formal communication, without fluency in the official language — are structurally excluded from the process that determines investment priorities.

**Result:** Reverse the prioritisation: start with the most underserved communities and work inward, and redesign the participation process to reach those currently excluded.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume that growth will trickle down to the "last."
**Why:** Fifty years of evidence shows that growth without deliberate pro-poor policies leaves the poorest behind. The "last" are last precisely because the normal dynamics of growth and development do not reach them.

**Don't:** Romanticise the poor.
**Why:** Putting the last first does not mean treating the poor as virtuous or their choices as beyond question. It means recognising their knowledge, respecting their agency, and designing development to serve their needs. The poor are people, not symbols.
