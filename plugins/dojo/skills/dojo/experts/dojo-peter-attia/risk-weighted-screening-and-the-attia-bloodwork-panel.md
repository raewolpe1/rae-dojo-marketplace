# Risk-Weighted Screening and the Attia Bloodwork Panel

## When to Use
- When someone asks what tests to get, or what a longevity panel includes.
- When a standard checkup "came back normal" and the user wants more depth.
- When setting up the data needed before any target-setting.
- When deciding how early and how often to screen given individual risk.

## Fails When
- **The user wants one marker explained.** Route to the specific file (apoB, Lp(a), insulin).
- **You list tests without rationale.** Every test must earn its place by changing a decision; screening for its own sake is not the point.
- **You promise diagnosis.** This is risk stratification and early detection, not a substitute for clinical workup.

## Core Concept
Attia's screening philosophy follows directly from Medicine 3.0: measure early, measure comprehensively, and weight the aggressiveness to the individual's risk — because you cannot manage what you have not measured, and the chronic diseases are silent until late. A standard annual physical, with its thin lipid panel and fasting glucose, is calibrated to catch disease that has already arrived. Attia wants data that reveals trajectory years to decades earlier.

**The lipid and cardiovascular core.** The standard panel reports total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides. Attia adds the measures that actually drive decisions: **apoB** (the direct count of atherogenic particles — the single most important lipid number), and **Lp(a)** measured at least once in a lifetime (the commonest inherited cardiovascular risk, invisible on the standard panel). For higher-risk individuals he favours direct visualisation of disease — a **coronary artery calcium (CAC) score** to detect existing calcified plaque, and in some cases **CT angiography** to see soft plaque the calcium score misses.

**The metabolic core.** Fasting glucose and HbA1c are lagging indicators — they move only after years of dysfunction. Attia adds **fasting insulin** and, ideally, an **oral glucose tolerance test with insulin measured at intervals**, because the insulin response reveals problems long before glucose drifts. He watches **triglycerides**, the **triglyceride-to-HDL ratio** as a rough insulin-resistance proxy, **uric acid**, and markers of liver fat (ALT, and imaging where warranted). Continuous glucose monitoring can add individualised insight into glycaemic response.

**Body composition and bone.** A **DEXA scan** gives far more than a bathroom scale: it quantifies lean mass, fat mass, **visceral adipose tissue** (the metabolically dangerous fat around the organs), and **bone mineral density**. Visceral fat and sarcopenia are both major, often-missed risks; bone density matters enormously for the fracture cascade that ends so many Marginal Decades.

**Cardiorespiratory fitness and strength.** Because VO2max and strength are among the strongest mortality predictors, Attia treats them as vital signs: a formal **VO2max test** (gold standard, on a metabolic cart), plus practical strength benchmarks — **grip strength**, leg strength, and functional tests. These are screening data, not just gym numbers.

**Genetics and other.** **ApoE genotype** once, to calibrate dementia-risk intensity. A comprehensive metabolic and hormonal panel (thyroid, sex hormones where relevant), inflammatory markers (hs-CRP), and homocysteine. Cancer screening weighted to risk — and here Attia is notably more aggressive than guidelines: **colonoscopy earlier than the standard starting age** for many people (the disease is common, slow, and curable when caught early), and consideration of advanced imaging or multi-cancer early-detection blood tests for those whose risk justifies it.

The organising principle throughout is *risk-weighting*. Population guidelines are built for average risk and for cost-effectiveness at scale. The individual with a strong family history of early heart attack, an elevated Lp(a), or an ApoE e4 allele is not average, and their screening should reflect that — earlier, more comprehensive, more frequent. Conversely, a test that will not change any decision should not be ordered just because it exists. Every measurement has to earn its place by potentially moving an action: confirming a target, triggering a treatment, or changing surveillance frequency.

The other principle is *cadence*. Some things are measured once (Lp(a), ApoE — they don't change). Some are tracked regularly (apoB, metabolic markers, body composition, fitness — typically annually or more often when actively managing). The point of repeated measurement is to see the trajectory, because in chronic disease the slope is more informative than any single point. A "normal" value drifting steadily in the wrong direction is far more useful intelligence than a single reassuring snapshot.

## How to Apply
1. **Get the high-yield additions first.** apoB, Lp(a) (once), and fasting insulin are the tests most likely to change the picture for someone who's only ever had a standard panel.
2. **Add body composition.** A DEXA for visceral fat, lean mass, and bone density — far more useful than weight or BMI.
3. **Measure fitness as a vital sign.** Formal VO2max and grip/leg strength benchmarks, not just a subjective sense of fitness.
4. **Weight to individual risk.** Family history, Lp(a), ApoE → screen earlier, deeper, more often. Don't default to the guideline floor.
5. **Track the slope.** Repeat the changeable markers on a cadence; once-only for the genetic ones. Watch trajectory, not just the single value.

## Examples
**Situation:** "My annual physical was all normal — am I good?"
**Application:** A standard physical omits the most informative tests. Add apoB, Lp(a), fasting insulin, a DEXA, and a fitness measure. "Normal" on a thin panel is not reassurance about trajectory.
**Result:** Either genuine reassurance backed by real data, or early detection of a drift that was invisible before.

**Situation:** Father had a heart attack at 52; user is 45 and asymptomatic.
**Application:** This is elevated individual risk — weight up. apoB and Lp(a) now, a CAC score to look for existing plaque, and an aggressive apoB target. Don't wait for the guideline-age screening.
**Result:** Risk is quantified and managed a decade before guidelines would have engaged.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Equate "no diagnosis on a standard panel" with "low risk."
**Why:** The standard panel misses the markers that reveal trajectory; absence of a named disease isn't reassurance.

**Don't:** Order tests that won't change a decision.
**Why:** Every measurement should earn its place by potentially moving an action. Screening theatre adds cost and anxiety, not information.

**Don't:** Screen everyone identically.
**Why:** Risk-weighting is the whole point — individual history should set how early, deep, and often you test.
