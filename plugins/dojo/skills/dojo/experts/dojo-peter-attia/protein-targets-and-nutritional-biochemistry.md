# Protein Targets and Nutritional Biochemistry

## When to Use
- When someone asks how much protein to eat, especially over 50.
- When addressing muscle loss (sarcopenia) and how to defend against it.
- When the user wants Attia's overall nutrition stance and its honest limits.
- When designing diet to support strength training.

## Fails When
- **The question is really about glucose/insulin.** Route to the metabolic-flexibility file.
- **You overstate nutrition science.** Attia is candid that most of it is weak; don't launder weak evidence as strong.

## Core Concept
Attia's nutrition stance has two distinctive features: a strong, specific emphasis on **protein** as the one macronutrient most people under-consume relative to their longevity needs, and an unusual intellectual honesty about how **weak most nutrition science actually is**.

Start with the honesty, because it shapes everything else. Attia is openly skeptical of the confidence with which nutrition claims are made. The field relies heavily on epidemiology — food-frequency questionnaires and observational cohorts riddled with confounding and recall error — and on short, small randomised trials. The result is that for most specific dietary questions (is this fat good, is this food bad), the evidence does not support the certainty people assert. He therefore holds most nutrition positions *provisionally* and resists dogma about specific diets (keto, vegan, carnivore, Mediterranean as ideologies). His framework reduces nutrition to a few things that actually matter: **don't be over- or under-nourished in total energy; protect lean mass; control the metabolic consequences (glucose/insulin); and get adequate protein.** Most of the rest, he argues, is noise relative to those levers.

Now the protein emphasis, where he is much more confident, because the case rests on muscle physiology rather than food epidemiology. With age, two things happen: muscle mass declines (sarcopenia), and the muscle becomes **anabolically resistant** — it takes more of a protein/leucine stimulus to trigger the same muscle-protein-synthesis response that a younger body gets easily. Combined with the fact that muscle mass and strength are among the strongest predictors of longevity and independence, this means older adults need *more* protein than younger ones, not less — the opposite of the conventional under-eating that drives age-related muscle loss.

The target: Attia recommends roughly **1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day** for an active person trying to build or preserve muscle — well above the RDA of 0.8 g/kg, which he considers a floor for preventing deficiency, not an optimum for healthy aging. For a man around 80 kg, that's roughly **130–175 g/day**. Two refinements matter: **distribution** — spreading protein across meals (roughly 30–50 g per meal, 3–4 times a day) is more effective for stimulating muscle protein synthesis than loading it all at once, especially given anabolic resistance; and **quality/leucine** — complete proteins rich in the amino acid leucine (animal proteins, whey) most efficiently trigger synthesis, while plant proteins are usable but generally require larger quantities to hit the same leucine threshold. And protein only does its full job alongside the **resistance-training stimulus** — protein is the substrate, training is the signal; you need both to build and hold muscle.

Beyond protein, Attia's other nutritional priorities: keep total energy balance appropriate (excess energy, from any source, drives the visceral fat and insulin resistance that feed the Four Horsemen); manage the glycaemic and insulin consequences of what you eat (more in the metabolic file); ensure adequate fibre and micronutrient density; and prioritise food quality without becoming dogmatic about a named diet. He's pragmatic about tools — time-restricted eating, caloric awareness, even GLP-1 agonists where obesity is the problem — judged by whether they help the individual reach appropriate body composition and metabolic health, not by ideology.

The throughline is consistency with his broader method: be aggressive and specific where the evidence is strong (protein for muscle preservation, energy balance for metabolic health), and be humble and provisional where it is weak (most specific food claims). The failure mode he's correcting runs in both directions — people who chase elaborate dietary rules with false confidence while under-eating protein and over-eating total energy, missing the two levers that actually matter. Get enough protein, distributed and high-quality, paired with resistance training; keep energy balance and metabolic markers in check; and don't mistake the certainty of nutrition influencers for the actual state of the evidence.

## How to Apply
1. **Set the protein target.** ~1.6–2.2 g/kg/day for active adults preserving/building muscle — well above the 0.8 g/kg RDA. For ~80 kg, ~130–175 g/day.
2. **Distribute it.** ~30–50 g per meal across 3–4 meals beats one big dose, especially with age-related anabolic resistance.
3. **Favour quality/leucine.** Complete, leucine-rich proteins trigger synthesis most efficiently; plant-based eaters need larger quantities to match.
4. **Pair with resistance training.** Protein is the substrate, training is the signal — neither works fully without the other.
5. **Hold other nutrition claims provisionally.** Anchor on protein, energy balance, and metabolic markers; resist dietary dogma where evidence is weak.

## Examples
**Situation:** A 55-year-old eating "healthy" but getting ~70 g protein/day wonders why strength is stalling.
**Application:** 70 g is likely well under target for his body weight, and age-related anabolic resistance compounds it. Raise to ~1.6–2.2 g/kg, distribute across meals, ensure leucine-rich sources, and confirm the resistance-training stimulus is present.
**Result:** Adequate substrate plus training restores the muscle-building response.

**Situation:** Someone asks Attia to settle "keto vs vegan vs Mediterranean — which is best for longevity?"
**Application:** He'd resist the framing — the evidence doesn't support that level of certainty about named diets. Reframe to what's actually established: adequate protein, appropriate energy balance, good metabolic markers, food quality. Any of those diets can be made to satisfy or violate those criteria.
**Result:** Energy shifts from diet tribalism to the few levers that matter.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Apply the 0.8 g/kg RDA to active aging adults.
**Why:** That's a deficiency floor, not an optimum; aging muscle is anabolically resistant and needs more, not less.

**Don't:** Present nutrition claims with false confidence.
**Why:** Most nutrition science is weak; Attia holds specific food claims provisionally and so should you.

**Don't:** Expect protein to build muscle without training.
**Why:** Protein is the substrate; resistance training is the required signal. Both are necessary.
