# Appiah and Development

## When to Use
- When the development practitioner needs a framework for navigating the ethical challenges of working across cultural boundaries.
- When designing programmes that must maintain universal commitments while adapting to diverse cultural contexts.
- When development practice is trapped between cultural imperialism ("our way is universal") and cultural relativism ("all ways are equally valid").

## Core Concept
Appiah has not written extensively about development practice, but his cosmopolitan ethics provides the most sophisticated available framework for the cross-cultural dimension of development work. Development practitioners are cosmopolitans by necessity — they work across cultural boundaries, engage with communities whose values differ from their own, and must maintain universal commitments (to health, education, justice, dignity) while respecting the particular forms through which different communities express their moral lives.

Cosmopolitan development practice holds three commitments simultaneously. Universal concern: the programme exists because every human being matters, and the suffering of distant strangers creates obligations. Respect for difference: the community's own understanding of what constitutes a good life is morally significant and must be engaged, not overridden. Conversation: the relationship between the programme and the community should be conversational — a genuine engagement across difference in which both parties learn and potentially change.

This goes beyond "cultural sensitivity." Cultural sensitivity is a procedural requirement: be polite, be respectful, know the local customs. Cosmopolitan development is a substantive ethical commitment: hold your universal principles with conviction and your particular practices with humility, and enter every cross-cultural encounter prepared to learn something that changes your own understanding.

## How to Apply
1. **Frame the practitioner as cosmopolitan.** "You are not a neutral technician delivering a universal product. You are a particular person, with particular values and particular blind spots, engaging with particular communities whose values and knowledge may differ from yours. This is the cosmopolitan position — committed, humble, and conversational."
2. **Maintain the thin, open the thick.** "The programme's thin commitments — dignity, equity, accountability — are non-negotiable. The thick expression — the specific institutional forms, cultural practices, and operational modalities — should emerge from conversation with the community."
3. **Design for mutual learning.** "The programme should be designed to learn from the community, not only to serve it. What does the community know that the programme does not? What values does the community hold that might improve the programme's design? Mutual learning is the hallmark of cosmopolitan engagement."
4. **Accept partial agreement.** "Full consensus between the programme and the community is neither necessary nor likely. Partial agreements — on specific objectives, specific practices, specific protections — are the realistic and respectful outcome of cosmopolitan engagement."

## Examples
**Situation:** A water and sanitation programme has designed a standard community management model: a water committee with elected members, transparent fee collection, and a maintenance fund. The model works well in some communities but fails in others where traditional authority structures conflict with the elected committee model.
**Application:** "The programme has universalised a thick practice — the elected water committee — that reflects a particular model of community governance. The thin principle — that the community should have a functional, accountable mechanism for managing its water supply — is genuinely universal. But the thick expression — elected committees with transparent fee collection — is one culture's model. In communities where traditional authority is the legitimate governance mechanism, the elected committee may undermine rather than support functional management. The cosmopolitan redesign: maintain the thin (functional, accountable water management) and open the thick (the specific governance mechanism) to conversation. Ask: what governance mechanism does this community trust? How does the community currently make collective decisions about shared resources? What accountability mechanisms already exist? The answer may be a traditional council, an age-set system, or a religious authority structure. If this mechanism can deliver accountable water management, it is a valid thick expression of the thin principle — even if it does not resemble the elected committee model. The standard is accountability and functionality, not the specific institutional form."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce cosmopolitan development to cultural sensitivity.
**Why:** Cultural sensitivity is about manners; cosmopolitan development is about ethics. The cosmopolitan practitioner does not merely avoid offending — she genuinely engages with the community's moral framework and is prepared to change her own understanding.

**Don't:** Ignore power asymmetries.
**Why:** The "conversation" between a donor-funded programme and a resource-poor community is structured by power asymmetries that Appiah's framework does not fully address. The cosmopolitan conversation is genuine only when the power differential is acknowledged and mitigated.
