# Appiah and Universalism

## When to Use
- When the charge of cultural imperialism is raised against universal moral principles — human rights, gender equality, democratic governance — and the practitioner needs to navigate between genuine universals and false universalisations.
- When defending universal moral commitments without treating them as the property of any particular culture.
- When distinguishing between universals that genuinely apply across cultures and particular Western values that have been falsely universalised.

## Core Concept
Appiah is a universalist — he holds that some moral principles apply to all human beings, regardless of culture. But his universalism is careful, modest, and conversational rather than assertive, imperial, and declaratory.

The key distinction is between thin universals and thick particulars. Thin universals are abstract moral principles shared across cultures: that cruelty is wrong, that persons deserve respect, that fairness matters, that certain forms of suffering should be prevented. These principles are thin because they underdetermine specific practices — they tell you that cruelty is wrong without telling you exactly what counts as cruelty in every situation. Thick particulars are the specific moral practices through which communities express these thin universals: specific family structures, specific religious observances, specific social norms, specific legal arrangements.

The error of aggressive universalism is to take one culture's thick expression of a thin universal and treat it as the universal itself. When "gender equality" is operationalised as "the specific institutional arrangements that characterise gender relations in Northern European social democracies," a thick particular has been falsely universalised. The thin universal — that women and men have equal moral worth — is genuinely universal. The thick expression — specific employment protections, specific family law, specific reproductive rights frameworks — is the particular form that one set of societies has developed. Other societies may express the same thin universal through different thick practices.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the genuine universal.** "What is the thin universal principle at stake? Dignity? Fairness? Non-cruelty? Freedom from arbitrary domination? State the principle at the thin level — abstract enough to be genuinely universal."
2. **Distinguish thick from thin.** "Is the specific practice being promoted genuinely universal, or is it one culture's thick expression of a universal principle? If the latter, ask: are there other thick expressions — from the target community's own tradition — that serve the same universal?"
3. **Test universality through conversation.** "A genuine universal can survive conversation across cultures — it resonates, even if it is expressed differently. A false universalisation cannot — it meets resistance because it is experienced as imposition rather than recognition."
4. **Defend the thin with humility.** "When defending universal principles, do so with humility: 'we believe that every person deserves dignity — and we recognise that different traditions express this differently.' Humility about thick expression is compatible with confidence about thin principle."

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor requires that all funded programmes include a "gender mainstreaming" component based on a specific gender analysis framework developed by a Northern European development agency. National programme staff in a West African country express discomfort, arguing that the framework does not fit their cultural context.
**Application:** "The thin universal — that women and men have equal moral worth and deserve equal opportunity — is genuine and defensible across cultures. The thick particular — this specific gender analysis framework, with its specific categories, its specific indicators, and its specific institutional assumptions — is one culture's expression of the universal. The national staff's discomfort may indicate that the framework's thick expression does not resonate with their community's moral vocabulary — not because the community rejects gender equality but because the community's own resources for gender justice take different forms. The cosmopolitan response: maintain the thin universal (gender equality is non-negotiable) while opening the thick expression to conversation. What concepts of fairness, dignity, and proper treatment of women exist within the community's own tradition? How have women within the tradition articulated their own claims? The programme can pursue gender equality through these indigenous moral resources rather than importing a framework that may be technically sophisticated but culturally foreign. This is not relativism — the commitment to gender equality remains. It is cosmopolitan universalism — pursuing the universal through the particular."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assert universals without conversation.
**Why:** Universal principles that are asserted rather than conversationally established are experienced as imposition. The conversation — even when it does not produce agreement — confers legitimacy on the principle.

**Don't:** Abandon universals to relativism.
**Why:** Some moral principles genuinely apply across cultures. The recognition that their thick expressions vary does not mean the thin principles are invalid. Dignity, fairness, and non-cruelty are real moral requirements, not Western preferences.
