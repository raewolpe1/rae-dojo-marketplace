# Honour and Moral Revolution

## When to Use
- When attempting to change entrenched moral practices — and rational argument alone is not working.
- When understanding the social mechanisms through which moral change actually occurs: shifts in what communities find honourable and shameful.
- When applying Appiah's analysis (from *The Honor Code*, 2010) of how practices like foot-binding, duelling, and slavery were abandoned through changes in honour, not through philosophical argument.

## Core Concept
In *The Honor Code: How Moral Revolutions Happen* (2010), Appiah argues that the great moral revolutions — the end of Atlantic slavery, the abolition of foot-binding in China, the end of duelling in Britain — were not produced by new moral arguments. The arguments against these practices had been available for centuries. What changed was not what people knew but what they found honourable and shameful. Moral revolutions happen when practices that were once honourable become shameful — or when practices that were once shameful become honourable.

The mechanism is honour: the social currency of respect, esteem, and standing within a community. People change their behaviour not when they are persuaded by argument but when the behaviour becomes a source of shame rather than pride. British gentlemen stopped duelling not because they were persuaded that duelling was illogical but because duelling became associated with the lower classes — it became ungentlemanly. Chinese families stopped binding their daughters' feet not because they read anti-foot-binding treatises but because foot-binding became associated with backwardness in the eyes of a modernising national culture.

For development practice, this analysis is both liberating and humbling. Liberating because it reveals a mechanism for moral change that does not depend on the practitioner's persuasive powers: if the honour dynamics of a community can be engaged, change can occur from within rather than being imposed from outside. Humbling because it means that the development practitioner's rational arguments, evidence, and advocacy may be insufficient — moral change requires engaging the social mechanisms that actually govern behaviour.

## How to Apply
1. **Map the honour system.** "What is honourable in this community? What is shameful? How do individuals gain or lose standing? Understanding the honour system reveals the social mechanisms that govern behaviour — mechanisms that are often more powerful than formal rules or rational argument."
2. **Identify the honour connection.** "How is the practice you want to change connected to honour? If the practice is honourable (it confers status, respect, or standing), changing it requires disconnecting it from honour — making the practice shameful rather than honourable."
3. **Find the honour entrepreneurs.** "Who within the community has the standing to redefine what is honourable? Leaders, elders, successful members of the community who can model a new honour script — who can demonstrate that the changed behaviour is compatible with (or even required by) honour."
4. **Avoid honour destruction.** "Change strategies that shame individuals or communities — that strip them of honour without offering an alternative source of standing — produce resistance rather than change. The goal is honour redefinition, not honour destruction."

## Examples
**Situation:** A community health programme is trying to reduce open defecation. The programme has provided latrines, conducted hygiene education, and delivered behaviour change communication. But many households continue to defecate in the open, and latrine use remains low.
**Application:** "The programme has deployed rational arguments (hygiene education) and material incentives (latrines). It has not engaged the honour system. In many communities, open defecation is not experienced as shameful — it is simply the way things have always been done. The latrine, conversely, may carry its own associations: it may be seen as an imposition from outsiders, as unhygienic (waste close to the home), or as associated with poverty (only those who cannot walk to the bush use a latrine). To change this, engage the honour mechanism. Community-Led Total Sanitation (CLTS) has understood this instinctively: it does not educate about hygiene — it triggers disgust and shame by making the community see its own practice through new eyes. The walk of shame — following the trail of faecal contamination through the community — does not provide new information. It provides a new experience of the practice: one that transforms it from invisible habit to visible shame. The moral revolution occurs not when people learn that open defecation is unhygienic (they may already know this) but when open defecation becomes shameful and latrine use becomes honourable — when the family that uses a latrine gains standing rather than losing it. Design for honour, not for education."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat honour as always positive.
**Why:** Honour systems can enforce oppressive practices — honour killings, caste-based exclusion, gender-based violence. The analysis of honour is a tool for understanding moral change, not an endorsement of honour systems as they currently operate.

**Don't:** Present moral revolutions as programmable.
**Why:** Moral revolutions are complex, contingent, and often slow. The practitioner can engage the honour mechanism — but cannot guarantee a revolution. Understanding the mechanism improves the odds; it does not ensure the outcome.
