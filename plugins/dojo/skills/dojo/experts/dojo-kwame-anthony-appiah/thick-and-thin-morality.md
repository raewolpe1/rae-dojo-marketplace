# Thick and Thin Morality

## When to Use
- When a programme must articulate what is non-negotiable (thin universals) and what can be adapted to context (thick particulars).
- When navigating the relationship between abstract moral principles and the specific practices through which communities live morally.
- When the language of "standards" and "adaptation" needs a philosophical framework.

## Core Concept
Appiah draws on and adapts Michael Walzer's distinction between "thick" and "thin" morality. Thin morality consists of abstract, widely shared moral principles: do not be cruel, treat persons with dignity, be fair, keep promises, protect the vulnerable. These principles are thin because they are abstract — they provide moral orientation without specifying exact practices. Thick morality consists of the specific, culturally embedded practices through which communities live their moral lives: particular family structures, particular religious observances, particular norms of hospitality, particular systems of justice.

The relationship between thick and thin is not hierarchical but complementary. Thin principles without thick practices are empty — they provide no guidance for actual life. Thick practices without thin principles are parochial — they provide rich moral life within the community but no basis for moral engagement across communities. Ethical life requires both: thin principles that provide universal moral orientation and thick practices that give those principles concrete, liveable form.

For development practice, the thick/thin distinction provides a principled framework for contextualisation. The programme's thin commitments — dignity, equity, non-discrimination, accountability — are non-negotiable. They apply in every context. But the thick expression of these commitments — the specific institutional forms, the specific cultural practices, the specific language and categories through which they are pursued — should emerge from the community's own moral resources. This is not cultural relativism (which would abandon the thin). It is cosmopolitan contextualisation (which maintains the thin while respecting the thick).

## How to Apply
1. **Articulate the thin.** "What are the non-negotiable moral principles of this programme? State them at the thin level: dignity, equity, non-cruelty, accountability. These apply everywhere."
2. **Open the thick to context.** "How should these thin principles be expressed in this community? What thick practices — from the community's own moral tradition — give the thin principles concrete, liveable form? The thick should emerge from conversation, not from importation."
3. **Test thick against thin.** "When a thick practice is proposed — a particular targeting mechanism, a particular participation model, a particular accountability structure — test it against the thin principles. Does it respect dignity? Is it equitable? Does it avoid cruelty? If it passes, the thick practice is acceptable regardless of whether it matches the metropolitan model."
4. **Protect the thin.** "When a community practice violates thin universals — when it is cruel, when it denies dignity, when it discriminates — the thin takes priority. But even then, pursue the conversation: find resources within the community's own tradition that support the thin principle."

## Examples
**Situation:** A social protection programme is being implemented in multiple countries. The programme's design includes detailed operational guidelines specifying targeting methods, payment mechanisms, grievance procedures, and monitoring requirements. Country teams request flexibility to adapt the guidelines to local contexts, but the headquarters is concerned about maintaining programme standards.
**Application:** "Distinguish thin from thick. The thin principles — that the programme reaches those who need it most (equity), that recipients are treated with respect (dignity), that there is a mechanism for addressing problems (accountability), and that the programme does not cause harm (non-cruelty) — are non-negotiable. They apply in every country. The thick operational details — how targeting is conducted, how payments are delivered, how grievances are raised, how monitoring is structured — should be adapted to context. In one country, community-based targeting may be more equitable than proxy means testing. In another, mobile money may serve dignity better than physical cash distribution because it avoids public queuing. In a third, traditional dispute resolution may be a more accessible grievance mechanism than the formal complaints system specified in the guidelines. The headquarters should define the thin: equity, dignity, accountability, non-cruelty. The country teams should define the thick: the specific operational forms through which these principles are expressed in each context. The standard is the thin principle, not the thick procedure."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce all morality to thin universals.
**Why:** The thin is necessary but insufficient. People do not live by abstract principles — they live by the thick practices that give those principles substance. A programme that specifies only thin principles without allowing for thick contextualisation provides direction without content.

**Don't:** Use the distinction to avoid difficult judgments.
**Why:** When a thick practice violates thin principles, the thin takes priority. The thick/thin distinction does not provide an escape from moral judgment — it provides a framework for knowing when judgment is required and when flexibility is appropriate.
