# Cultural Patrimony

## When to Use
- When questions about who owns cultural objects, cultural knowledge, or cultural practices arise in development contexts — particularly when colonial history has produced the current distribution.
- When navigating debates about indigenous intellectual property, traditional knowledge, or the restitution of cultural objects.
- When the concept of cultural "ownership" itself needs to be examined.

## Core Concept
In *Cosmopolitanism* and in his essay "Whose Culture Is It?", Appiah challenges the conventional framework of cultural patrimony — the idea that cultures own objects and knowledge in the way that individuals own property. This challenge is uncomfortable because it cuts against both nationalist claims (these objects belong to our nation) and progressive restitution claims (these objects were stolen and must be returned).

Appiah does not deny that colonial looting was wrong. He argues instead that the framework of cultural ownership is more complicated than either side acknowledges. Cultures are not bounded, discrete entities that produce and own objects. They are porous, mixed, and continuously exchanging. The "Greek" heritage claimed by modern Greece includes contributions from Egypt, Persia, and the broader Mediterranean. The "African" masks in European museums were often produced for trade, not for ritual. The assumption that every cultural object has a single, rightful cultural "owner" oversimplifies the complex history of cultural production, exchange, and appropriation.

For development practice, cultural patrimony questions arise in contexts of indigenous knowledge, traditional practices, and cultural resources. Who owns traditional knowledge — the community that developed it, the nation-state in which the community is located, or humanity as a whole? When a pharmaceutical company develops a drug from traditional plant knowledge, what obligations flow to the community? These questions cannot be answered by treating cultures as property-holding entities — they require the cosmopolitan recognition that cultural production is always collaborative, always mixed, and never purely "owned."

## How to Apply
1. **Examine the ownership claim.** "When a claim of cultural ownership is made — 'this knowledge belongs to our community' — examine the claim without dismissing it. Who produced this knowledge? Through what history of exchange, adaptation, and collaboration? What political function does the ownership claim serve?"
2. **Acknowledge colonial context.** "When cultural objects or knowledge have been extracted through colonial power, the extraction was wrong — not because it violated cultural ownership but because it violated the dignity and autonomy of the people from whom the extraction occurred."
3. **Design for benefit-sharing, not ownership.** "Rather than resolving who 'owns' traditional knowledge, design for equitable benefit-sharing. When traditional knowledge contributes to a commercial product, the community should benefit — not because it 'owns' the knowledge but because justice requires that those whose contributions are used should share in the benefits."
4. **Resist cultural essentialism.** "The framework of cultural patrimony can reinforce cultural essentialism — the idea that cultures are discrete, bounded entities with fixed properties. The cosmopolitan alternative: recognise that all cultural production is collaborative and that benefit-sharing obligations flow from justice, not from ownership."

## Examples
**Situation:** A biodiversity conservation programme is documenting traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) held by indigenous communities. The documentation will be used in the programme's conservation planning. Community elders express concern that their knowledge will be "taken" — documented in the programme's database and used without their control.
**Application:** "The elders' concern is legitimate, even if the ownership framework is complicated. Their knowledge was produced through generations of observation, experimentation, and transmission — and the programme is extracting it for its own purposes. The colonial parallel is unmistakable: metropolitan institutions extracting value from indigenous communities. But the cosmopolitan analysis adds nuance. The knowledge is not a bounded object that can be simply 'owned' or 'stolen.' It is a living practice — embedded in the community's relationship to its environment, expressed through its language, transmitted through its social structures. What the programme is documenting is a translation of this knowledge into the programme's categories — and the translation may lose what matters most. Design for reciprocity rather than extraction. The community should control the documentation process — deciding what is shared, how it is represented, and who has access. The programme should provide something in return — not payment for knowledge (which commodifies it) but resources that support the community's own knowledge practices: language preservation, intergenerational transmission, and the institutional recognition that indigenous knowledge is not supplementary data but primary expertise."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss cultural patrimony claims as nationalism.
**Why:** Even when the ownership framework is problematic, the underlying concerns — about colonial extraction, about dignity, about control over one's own heritage — are legitimate and must be addressed.

**Don't:** Treat culture as simple property.
**Why:** The property framework oversimplifies cultural production, which is always collaborative, historical, and mixed. Benefit-sharing and reciprocity are better frameworks than ownership.
