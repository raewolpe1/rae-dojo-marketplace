## When to Use

When someone already has reliable daily routines and wants to add new habits. When someone asks "what's the best time to do [habit]?" When someone has failed multiple times to build a habit with a fixed time.

## Fails When

The anchor habit is itself unreliable. If the "after coffee" moment doesn't happen consistently, the stack collapses. Also fails when the stack grows too long — more than 3-4 behaviours chained becomes a performance, not a habit.

## Core Concept

Habit stacking is the technique of linking a new habit to an existing one using the formula:

**"After [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT]."**

The existing habit becomes the cue for the new behaviour. This is a specific application of implementation intentions (when-where planning) and exploits the brain's natural tendency to chain behaviours together.

Why it works: existing habits are among the most reliable cues in your environment. They happen at a predictable time, in a predictable place, in a predictable state of mind. They are already encoded as automatic responses. When you attach a new behaviour to one of these existing sequences, you borrow the cue reliability of the anchor habit.

The stack also creates what researchers call "contextual interference" — the existing behaviour primes the neural patterns associated with it, making the transition to the next behaviour lower-friction than starting cold.

Examples of anchor habits: morning coffee, sitting down at the desk, arriving home, finishing lunch, brushing teeth, putting down the phone, starting the car.

**Building a stack:**
- Identify an existing habit that is rock-solid: something you do every day without exception.
- Choose a new habit that is logically adjacent (similar location, mood, or tools).
- Write the formula: "After [ANCHOR], I will [NEW HABIT]."
- Start with the two-minute version of the new habit.
- Only after the stack runs automatically for 2+ weeks, consider extending the new habit.

**Multi-habit stacks:** You can chain several behaviours into a sequence. "After my alarm goes off, I will make my bed. After making my bed, I will put on my running shoes. After putting on my running shoes, I will step outside." Each behaviour becomes the cue for the next.

Warning: stacks can become brittle if the anchor changes. If you travel, your morning coffee ritual shifts. The stack may not follow. Design contingency anchors for environments that vary.

## How to Apply

1. **List your most reliable daily behaviours.** At least 5 anchors. Morning, midday, evening. Include mundane ones (putting the kettle on, sitting at your desk, washing up after dinner).
2. **Match each new habit to the most logically adjacent anchor.** A learning habit goes after a work anchor. A physical habit goes after a movement anchor.
3. **Write the formula explicitly.** Don't keep it in your head — write it on paper or stick a note on the anchor location.
4. **Keep the new habit to two minutes.** The stack is for automaticity, not performance. Volume comes later.
5. **After 2 weeks, review what fired and what didn't.** If the anchor was unreliable, find a better one. Don't extend the habit until the two-minute version is automatic.

## Examples

**Example 1 — Learning habit for a consultant**

She wants to read more industry research. Anchor: making morning coffee (100% reliable). New habit: while the kettle boils, open one saved article. Two minutes. Just open it. Don't finish it — just open it. The reading habit fires every morning because the cue (kettle) is inviolable. After three weeks she's reading consistently; the two-minute habit has naturally extended to 10-15 minutes because she's already in it.

**Example 2 — Reflection practice for a team leader**

He wants to do a daily review of decisions made. Anchor: closing the laptop at end of work (highly reliable). New habit: before closing the laptop, write one sentence about the most important thing that happened. The anchor creates the moment. The two-minute version (one sentence) ensures it always happens. Over time it naturally expands to a paragraph.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Using a weak anchor.** "After I feel like it" or "after I get home" (which varies by 2 hours) are not reliable anchors. Choose a behaviour that happens at the same time, in the same place, every day.
- **Stacking onto an anchor that's too early or too late in the day.** Anchor habits at your peak energy time for cognitively demanding new habits.
- **Building the stack too long too fast.** Three new habits stacked in the first week is a recipe for system collapse. Add one at a time, and only after the previous link is automatic.
