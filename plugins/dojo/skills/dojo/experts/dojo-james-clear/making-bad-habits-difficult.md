## When to Use

When someone wants to break a bad habit. When discussing addiction, compulsive behaviour, or problematic routines. When willpower-based approaches have failed. When environment redesign hasn't fully solved a bad habit — friction increase is the next tool.

## Fails When

When the bad habit is deeply reinforced by severe addiction, trauma, or medical conditions that require clinical intervention rather than behavioural design. Friction strategies help with habitual behaviours; they are insufficient for addiction requiring medical treatment.

## Core Concept

The Third Law of Behaviour Change, inverted, is: Make It Difficult. Adding friction between a person and a bad habit reduces the probability that the habit will occur — not through willpower, but through the mechanics of response effort.

Every additional step required to perform a behaviour reduces its probability. This is a function of how the brain's habit system works: it is optimised for the path of least resistance. The easier a behaviour is, the more frequently it occurs. Correspondingly, the harder a behaviour is, the less frequently it occurs.

The key insight: you do not need to make a bad habit impossible. You only need to make it slightly harder than the competing behaviour. If smoking requires going outside in the rain versus staying warm inside, some smoking occasions disappear. If junk food requires a 15-minute drive rather than being in the cupboard, some junk food occasions disappear. You are not defeating the habit through virtue — you are rerouting around it through architecture.

**Commitment devices:** A commitment device is an action taken in the present that locks in good behaviour in the future. Ulysses tied himself to the mast to resist the sirens — he knew willpower would fail at the moment of temptation, so he pre-committed before the temptation arrived. Commitment devices work best when made in advance, when willpower and rationality are at full capacity, rather than in the moment when temptation is active.

Examples of commitment devices: cutting up credit cards, giving money to a friend to donate to a cause you dislike if you fail, deleting social media apps, putting your phone in another room before bed, buying only single-serving packages to prevent overeating.

**Friction taxonomy:**
- *Physical friction:* Distance, extra steps, inconvenience. Put your phone charger across the room. Keep junk food on a high shelf. Use a website blocker.
- *Financial friction:* Pre-committing money to good behaviour or penalty funds for bad behaviour.
- *Social friction:* Making the bad habit visible to others, adding accountability or social cost.
- *Cognitive friction:* Adding a waiting period between impulse and action (a 24-hour rule before purchases, a 10-minute rule before checking social media).

The goal is not punishment but prediction. Friction strategies work because they create a gap between the automatic impulse and the automatic execution. In that gap, the behaviour becomes less automatic — and something else can happen.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the specific trigger and response chain for the bad habit.** Where does the habit start? What makes the response effortless?
2. **Add at least two frictions to the response chain.** One friction creates a weak barrier; two or more create a meaningful deterrent.
3. **Consider commitment devices for high-willpower moments.** What can you do now, while motivation is high, to pre-commit your future behaviour?
4. **Make the competing good behaviour easier simultaneously.** Friction strategy works best combined with reducing friction on the desired behaviour. Bad habit harder + good habit easier = doubled effect.
5. **Monitor for workarounds.** People become creative about removing friction they've added themselves. Notice if you are working around your own friction systems, and redesign.

## Examples

**Example 1 — Late-night phone scrolling**

Bad habit: scrolling phone in bed for 60-90 minutes before sleep.

Loop: Cue = lying in bed. Craving = stimulation/escape. Response = pick up phone (zero friction; it's on the nightstand). Reward = novelty hits, temporary escape.

Friction interventions: Move phone charger to kitchen. Get a physical alarm clock so the phone is not the alarm. Add a 9:30 PM reminder to plug the phone in the kitchen. Result: accessing the phone in bed now requires getting up, going to the kitchen, and returning. Most instances of the impulse simply don't survive this friction.

**Example 2 — Impulsive spending**

Bad habit: adding items to online shopping cart and purchasing without reflection.

Commitment device options: Remove saved payment information from all shopping sites (adding friction of re-entering card details). Install a browser extension that adds a 24-hour waiting period before purchase. Delete shopping apps from phone (forcing web browser access with more steps). Set up a separate account for discretionary spending with a monthly transfer limit — physical cap on available funds.

The wallet rule (from Clear): if you have to retrieve your wallet, you will purchase less than if you pay by default saved method. Each friction moment is a decision point where the purchase can fail to occur.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Adding friction but leaving the cue and craving intact.** Friction slows the response; it doesn't eliminate the craving. If the craving is strong enough, even substantial friction won't prevent the behaviour indefinitely. For strong cravings, also work on cue elimination and craving reinterpretation.
- **Designing frictions you'll remove.** Frictions that can be easily disabled by the same person who installed them are weak. The strongest commitment devices involve another person or an external system that is harder to reverse.
- **Using friction as the only strategy.** Friction is most powerful combined with environment design (removing cues), identity change ("I'm not a person who scrolls in bed"), and an alternative habit that satisfies the same craving. Used alone, friction is a delaying mechanism, not a replacement.
