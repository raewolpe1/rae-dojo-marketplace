## When to Use

When someone is trying to change a behaviour but keeps relapsing. When motivation has run dry. When someone asks "how do I become someone who exercises / reads / ships consistently?" When the conversation is about long-term change rather than a single action.

## Fails When

The person needs a quick tactic, not a reframe. If someone has a working system and just needs to add one habit, skip the identity conversation.

## Core Concept

Most people approach behaviour change from the outside in: outcomes → processes → identity. They set a goal (lose 10kg), build a process (exercise 3× per week), and hope their identity follows. This is backwards.

The durable path runs inside out: identity → processes → outcomes.

Every habit is a vote for the type of person you are. When you write one sentence in your journal, you cast a vote for "I am a writer." When you do one rep, you vote for "I am an athlete." No single vote decides an election — but accumulated votes build a clear winner.

The mechanism: our actions are motivated by our self-image. If you identify as "someone who runs," lacing up shoes is consistent with who you are. Skipping feels like a betrayal of self. If you identify as "someone trying to run," skipping is just a failed attempt. The identity frame changes the emotional cost of inconsistency.

Three-step identity shift:
1. **Decide who you want to be.** Not what you want to achieve — who you want to become. "I want to be someone who creates every day." "I want to be someone who thinks clearly under pressure."
2. **Prove it with small wins.** Your identity is not declared; it is earned. Every action that aligns with your desired identity is evidence. Every misaligned action is counter-evidence.
3. **Trust the accumulation.** Identity change is not a moment; it is a trajectory. The question is not "am I there yet?" but "am I casting the right votes today?"

The practical implication: when designing a new habit, ask "what kind of person would do this?" before asking "what do I need to do?" A person who reads 50 books per year doesn't read for 30 minutes every day — they are a reader, and reading is what they do.

Counter-intuition: you don't need to change your behaviour before you change your identity. You change your identity first — even tentatively, even imperfectly — and the behaviour follows. "I'm not a smoker" (identity) is more durable than "I'm trying to quit smoking" (effort).

## How to Apply

1. **Name the identity.** Ask: "What type of person consistently does [the behaviour you want]?" Write it as a statement: "I am someone who..." This is your destination identity.
2. **Find the two-minute proof.** What is the smallest possible action that is consistent with that identity? That's your starting habit.
3. **Count the votes, not the outcomes.** Track reps, not results. Did you cast the vote today? That's the only metric that matters during identity formation.
4. **Reframe failures as data.** Missing one day doesn't break the identity — it's one counter-vote in a long election. The question after a miss is not "why did I fail?" but "how do I cast the next vote immediately?"
5. **Let the identity expand the habit.** Once the identity feels genuine, the behaviour naturally expands. You don't need to force it. A real runner eventually runs longer. A real writer eventually writes more.

## Examples

**Example 1 — Solo consultant, business development habit**

A consultant says she needs to network more but hates it. She has tried "send 5 emails a week" repeatedly.

Identity reframe: "You're not trying to network. You're deciding you're someone who is genuinely curious about other people's work and reaches out when that curiosity fires." The goal isn't 5 emails — it's becoming a person who stays naturally connected to the field. When she reads an article and thinks "Marcus would find this interesting," she sends it. Not from duty — from identity. The frequency will come. The identity comes first.

**Example 2 — Someone who keeps missing gym sessions**

"I keep falling off after two or three weeks." The problem isn't motivation — it's identity. He identifies as "trying to get fit," not as "an athlete." Reframe: "After this workout, you're not someone who worked out once more. You're someone who has cast a vote for being an athlete. Tomorrow you cast another. You can't feel the result yet, but you're accumulating identity evidence." The emotional relationship with the gym changes. Misses feel different when identity is at stake.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Confusing identity declaration with identity formation.** Saying "I'm a writer" without casting any votes is fantasy, not identity. The votes must precede the confident claim.
- **Making the identity too outcome-based.** "I am someone who runs 5km every day" is a performance standard, not an identity. "I am a runner" is an identity. Identities survive bad days; performance standards don't.
- **Waiting for the identity to feel true before starting.** Identity is built by action, not the reverse. Start before it feels genuine. The feeling follows the evidence.
