## When to Use

When someone has a habit they want to start but keeps forgetting or postponing. When vague intentions ("I should exercise more") aren't converting to action. When discussing habit scheduling, commitment, or follow-through. When someone knows what they want to do but not when or where.

## Fails When

When the scheduled time or location is not actually reliably available — if "Monday morning" is consistently disrupted by other commitments, the implementation intention fails not because the format is wrong but because the anchor is unreliable. Identify a stable anchor before specifying the intention.

## Core Concept

An implementation intention is a specific plan of the form: "When situation X arises, I will perform behaviour Y."

The most common format: "I will [BEHAVIOUR] at [TIME] in [LOCATION]."

Research by psychologist Peter Gollwitzer shows that people who form implementation intentions are 2-3x more likely to follow through on a stated intention than people who state the intention without a plan. The mechanism is straightforward: a vague intention requires a decision at execution time ("when should I do this? where? after what?"). An implementation intention eliminates that decision by pre-specifying the context. When the context occurs, the behaviour fires automatically.

James Clear incorporates implementation intentions as the mechanism behind the first law of behaviour change: Make It Obvious. An implementation intention makes the cue for a habit obvious by pre-determining it. Without an implementation intention, the cue for the habit is vague or environmental — you might notice an opportunity, or you might not. With an implementation intention, the cue is specific and guaranteed to occur.

The temporal component is critical. "I will meditate every day" is not an implementation intention. "I will meditate for 10 minutes at 7:00 AM in the kitchen after making my coffee" is. The precision matters because precision eliminates ambiguity at execution time. Ambiguity at execution time costs willpower and increases the probability of deferral.

Implementation intentions pair naturally with habit stacking. "After [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT]" is a form of implementation intention where the cue is an existing behaviour rather than a time. Both formats eliminate execution-time decision-making.

The deeper principle: most habit failures are not motivation failures. They are specificity failures. The person who fails to meditate daily often genuinely wants to meditate; they simply haven't specified when and where clearly enough for the intention to survive contact with a busy day. Making the intention specific makes it executable.

The research-grounded advice: the best time/location anchor for a new habit is one that reliably occurs in your existing schedule. A morning routine, a commute, a lunch break, a post-dinner walk. The new habit slots into an existing reliable event, rather than floating in unscheduled time where it competes with whatever else happens to arise.

## How to Apply

1. **State the behaviour with precision.** Not "exercise" but "20-minute walk." Not "write" but "write 300 words."
2. **Identify the time anchor.** A specific time of day, or an existing event that reliably occurs. "At 6:30 AM" or "after morning coffee" or "immediately after I close my laptop at the end of the work day."
3. **Specify the location.** "In the kitchen," "at my desk," "on the path by the river." Location activates context-specific memory and primes associated behaviours.
4. **Write the implementation intention in full.** "I will [behaviour] at [time] in [location]." Writing it is more effective than holding it mentally.
5. **Review the anchor for reliability.** Does the anchor actually occur consistently? If your "after lunch" anchor is frequently missed because lunch is irregular, find a more stable anchor.

## Examples

**Example 1 — Exercise**

A consultant wants to exercise more but keeps skipping.

Vague intention: "I'll exercise most mornings."

Implementation intention: "I will do 20 minutes of kettlebell work at 6:15 AM in the living room, before I shower."

The specificity eliminates the morning deliberation ("should I exercise today? how long? what exercise?"). The kettlebell is already in the living room (environment design). The time is before competing priorities arise. The shower immediately after is a natural reward that anchors the routine.

**Example 2 — Reading**

A professional wants to read more non-fiction.

Vague intention: "I'll read more, maybe in the evenings."

Implementation intention: "I will read for 20 minutes in the armchair in the bedroom at 9:30 PM before I turn off the light."

The armchair (location) becomes associated with reading. 9:30 PM is before the typical phone-scrolling window, not competing with it. The book lives on the armchair table (environment design). The sequence — sit in chair, open book — is specific enough to be automatic.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Vague implementation intentions.** "I will exercise sometime on Monday morning" is not an implementation intention — it still requires execution-time decisions. The format demands specificity.
- **Setting multiple new implementation intentions simultaneously.** One new implementation intention at a time. Multiple new habits require multiple new cues; the cognitive load compounds and failures cascade.
- **Anchoring to an unreliable event.** If the anchor event doesn't reliably occur, the implementation intention has no trigger. "After my lunchtime meeting" fails if the meeting gets cancelled or moved.
