## When to Use

When someone wants to build consistent habits around using AI tools — Claude, ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, or others. When AI tools are adopted but usage is inconsistent. When AI integration into workflow is aspirational but hasn't become automatic. When someone uses AI for some tasks but hasn't systematised it.

## Fails When

When the question is which AI tools to use or how to evaluate them — that is a tool selection question, not a habit formation question. Clear's framework determines how usage becomes automatic; it cannot determine what to use or whether AI is appropriate for a given task.

## Core Concept

AI tools face a specific habit formation challenge: they are high-leverage but low-cue. A gym bag on the floor is a visible cue for exercise. An app icon on a phone does not naturally cue a person to think "what could I use AI for right now?" The result is that many people adopt AI tools with enthusiasm, use them for a few obvious tasks, then plateau. The tools have not become habitual — they have not been embedded in the cue-response architecture of daily work.

Clear's framework, applied to AI tool habits, generates specific interventions:

**Identity: The AI-native practitioner.** The deepest lever is identity. A person who identifies as "someone who experiments with AI tools daily" will generate more consistent usage than one who thinks "I should use AI more." The identity frames AI usage as expression of character rather than a chore or experiment.

**Implementation intentions for AI usage.** The most powerful application of Clear's framework to AI tools is the implementation intention: specifying in advance which tasks will trigger AI involvement.

Examples:
- "Whenever I start a new writing task, I will open Claude first and draft the structure with it."
- "Whenever I need to research a topic, I will use Perplexity before searching Google."
- "Every Monday at 9am, I will ask Claude to help me plan my week."
- "Whenever I'm stuck on a problem for more than 20 minutes, I will describe it to Claude."

These if-then structures transform AI tools from options into automatic responses to specific cues.

**Habit stacking with existing workflows.** Rather than adding AI usage as a separate behaviour, stack it onto existing habits:
- After opening my email client each morning → draft responses with AI assistance.
- After a meeting ends → use AI to write the summary within 5 minutes.
- Before submitting any report → run it through AI for structure and gaps review.
- During weekly planning → use AI to think through priorities and identify blind spots.

**Environment design for AI tools.** Make the tool the path of least resistance:
- Pin the AI tool as a browser tab that opens automatically.
- Set AI assistant as the default for certain file types (AI draft first, then edit).
- Add AI to the beginning of existing workflows rather than as a separate step.
- Create prompt templates for recurring tasks (stored in a document, always accessible) to reduce the friction of starting a prompt.

**Process measures for AI habit building.** What can be measured to track AI habit formation?
- Number of times AI was consulted per day/week (deliberately counted, not tracked automatically).
- Number of tasks for which AI was the first tool used rather than the last resort.
- Number of new use cases tried per week.
- Time saved on specified tasks (as a motivating reward measure).

**The two-minute entry point.** The two-minute rule for AI habits: start with the smallest possible AI interaction for each task. Not "I will write an entire report with AI" — that creates a high bar that delays starting. Instead: "I will open Claude and describe what I need to write, just to see what it suggests." The two-minute version of consulting AI is describing the problem.

**Rewarding consistency.** Clear emphasises that the reward must follow the habit quickly. For AI tool habits, the reward is often the quality of output — which is immediate. Noticing and acknowledging "that response was genuinely useful" reinforces the loop. Keeping a short log of AI-assisted wins (tasks completed faster, better outputs, new ideas) creates a concrete reward mechanism.

## How to Apply

1. **Choose one specific trigger for AI integration.** Pick one recurring task or moment in your workflow and commit: "Every time I [X], I will use AI first." Start with one trigger, not a general aspiration to "use AI more."
2. **Write the implementation intention explicitly.** "I will [use AI tool] at [time/trigger] to [specific function]." Write it down. Put it somewhere visible.
3. **Reduce the friction of starting.** Is the tool one click away? Is a starting prompt pre-written for your most common use case? Pre-write three prompts for your three most common tasks.
4. **Measure interactions, not just outcomes.** Count how many times you consulted AI this week. This makes the process visible and creates a habit streak to maintain.
5. **Stack onto one existing habit this week.** Don't redesign your whole workflow. Pick one existing habit and add "consult AI first" immediately before or after it.

## Examples

**Example 1 — The consultant who uses AI sporadically**

Pattern: Uses Claude when stuck, not proactively. Usage is reactive (rescue) rather than integrated.

Clear-informed intervention:
- Identity: "I am someone who consults AI before drafting any client-facing document."
- Implementation intention: "Whenever I start a new proposal or report, I open Claude and describe the client situation before writing anything myself."
- Habit stack: After reading the client brief → open Claude → describe the situation and ask for a structural outline.
- Two-minute version: Just describe the project to Claude. See what it notices.
- Result after 4 weeks: AI-first drafting becomes automatic for proposals. Quality of first drafts improves. Time spent on initial structure drops significantly.

**Example 2 — The knowledge worker building a weekly review habit**

Pattern: Has heard of weekly reviews, tries and forgets, restarts every few months.

Clear-informed intervention:
- Implementation intention: "Every Friday at 4pm I will open Claude and paste my tasks/notes from the week, then ask it to help me identify patterns and plan next week."
- Cue design: Friday 4pm recurring calendar event titled "Weekly Review with Claude." Event reminder is the cue.
- Template: Pre-written prompt saved in a document: "Here is what I worked on this week: [paste]. Help me identify: what went well, what I should change, what I'm avoiding, and what my priorities for next week should be."
- Two-minute version: Just paste this week's tasks and ask one question.
- Result: Review habit stabilises because AI makes it genuinely useful (rather than a navel-gazing exercise) and the prompt template removes the friction of starting.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Using AI as a last resort.** Consulting AI only when stuck (after significant time invested) captures a fraction of the potential value. The highest leverage is using AI at the beginning — for structure, framing, and direction — before investing time in a direction that might be suboptimal.
- **Setting general intentions rather than specific ones.** "I will use AI more" is not an implementation intention. "Every time I draft an email longer than three paragraphs, I will ask Claude to review the structure and clarity" is. Specificity is what makes implementation intentions work.
- **Not maintaining a prompt library.** The friction of starting each AI interaction from scratch is a significant barrier. A small, curated set of prompts for your most frequent tasks reduces this friction dramatically. Ten minutes building a prompt library creates a lasting friction reduction.
