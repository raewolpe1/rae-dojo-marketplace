## When to Use

When someone's habit is not sticking and they don't know why. When designing a new habit system from scratch. When diagnosing why a previously working habit has broken down. When someone asks "how do I build [any habit]?"

## Fails When

The conversation is purely about identity or motivation at a philosophical level. The four laws are an engineering framework — they require a concrete behaviour to be effective.

## Core Concept

Every habit has four components: a cue, a craving, a response, and a reward. The four laws of behaviour change are a direct translation of this loop into actionable design:

**Law 1: Make it obvious (cue)**  
Habits that are not visible don't get done. The brain is not scanning for new behaviours — it's responding to familiar cues. If the cue isn't in your environment, the habit doesn't fire. Strategies: implementation intentions (when/where), habit stacking (after X, I will Y), environment redesign (put the thing where you'll see it).

**Law 2: Make it attractive (craving)**  
Habits persist when the anticipation of reward is pleasurable. We do things because we expect them to feel good. Strategies: temptation bundling (pair the habit with something you enjoy), reframe the identity (you get to do this, not have to), join a culture where the behaviour is the norm.

**Law 3: Make it easy (response)**  
Habits form at the path of least resistance. Difficulty is the primary reason habits die. Friction is invisible but powerful. Strategies: two-minute rule (reduce the habit to its smallest form), reduce the number of steps between you and the behaviour, increase the steps between you and the bad habit, prepare the environment (lay out shoes, open the document, charge the device).

**Law 4: Make it satisfying (reward)**  
The brain encodes behaviours that produce good feelings immediately after. If the reward is delayed (weight loss, financial security, career progress), the brain gets weak reinforcement. Strategies: habit tracking (visual progress is immediately satisfying), immediate small rewards, never miss twice (the streak itself becomes a reward signal).

**Inversion for breaking bad habits:**  
The same four laws apply in reverse: make it invisible (remove the cue), make it unattractive (reframe), make it difficult (add friction), make it unsatisfying (add immediate consequence).

**Diagnostic order:** When a habit fails, work through the four laws in sequence. Which one is broken? Fix the lowest-scoring law first — patching motivation (Law 2) won't help if the habit is invisible (Law 1) or exhausting (Law 3).

## How to Apply

1. **Name the specific behaviour.** Vague habits ("exercise more") can't be engineered. Be precise: "Do 10 push-ups after waking up."
2. **Score each law 1-3.** Is the cue visible? Is the behaviour attractive? Is it easy? Is it satisfying immediately? Any score of 1 is a failure point.
3. **Address the lowest score first.** Don't try to fix all four at once. One design change at a time.
4. **Implement the fix.** For Law 1: write an implementation intention. For Law 2: add temptation bundling. For Law 3: find the two-minute version. For Law 4: start a paper chain tracker.
5. **Re-score after 2 weeks.** Habits that survive two weeks have cleared at least some automaticity threshold.

## Examples

**Example 1 — Morning journal that keeps failing**

Scoring: Law 1 (obvious) = 1: the journal lives in a drawer. Law 2 (attractive) = 2: writing is okay but not exciting. Law 3 (easy) = 2: it's accessible. Law 4 (satisfying) = 1: no immediate reward.

Two lowest laws: 1 and 4. Fix Law 1: put the journal on the pillow the night before. Fix Law 4: use a streak tracker — never miss twice. Don't touch motivation or identity yet. Fix the engineering first.

**Example 2 — Reducing phone scrolling at night**

Inverse application. Law 1: make the phone invisible (charge it outside the bedroom — eliminates the cue entirely). Law 3: make it difficult (turn off social media apps after 8pm with Screen Time; re-enabling takes friction). Laws 2 and 4 matter less when Laws 1 and 3 are fixed. The habit breaks because the cue and the ease are gone simultaneously.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Skipping the diagnosis and going straight to motivation.** Motivation is Law 2. If Law 1 or Law 3 is broken, boosting motivation produces temporary results at best.
- **Treating the four laws as sequential.** They work simultaneously. A habit can fail on Law 4 while passing Laws 1-3. All four must hold.
- **Applying the laws to goals rather than behaviours.** "Lose weight" is not a behaviour; "walk for 10 minutes after lunch" is. The four laws need a concrete behaviour to act on.
