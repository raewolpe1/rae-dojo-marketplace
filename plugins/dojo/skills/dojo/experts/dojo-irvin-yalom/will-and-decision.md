# Will and Decision

## When to Use
- When someone is paralysed by a decision and the paralysis is about commitment, not information.
- When the distinction between wishing and willing needs to be drawn.
- When someone intellectually knows what they want but cannot act on it.

## Core Concept
Yalom distinguishes between wishing and willing. Wishing is passive — it fantasises about a different future while leaving the present unchanged. Willing is active — it commits to a course of action and accepts responsibility for the consequences. Many people who present with "I can't decide" are actually caught between wishing and willing: they wish things were different but are unwilling to do what would make them different, because willing requires accepting the loss of alternatives and the risk of being wrong.

The existential dimension of decision is the act of *decidere* — cutting off. Every genuine decision kills a possible future. Choosing this job means not choosing that one. Committing to this relationship means closing the door on other possibilities. This cutting-off is a small death, and it activates the same anxiety as the larger death — the fear of irreversibility, of finitude, of foreclosed possibility. Decision paralysis, in Yalom's framework, is not an information problem (as decision theorists might frame it) but an existential one: the person is defending against the awareness that their freedom is real and its exercise is irreversible.

Yalom also identifies the phenomenon of the "unkept resolution" — the person who decides and then fails to act. They declare their intention, feel a burst of resolve, and then nothing changes. In Yalom's analysis, the resolution was a wish dressed as a will. Genuine willing involves not just the declaration of intent but the mobilisation of the whole person toward the chosen path. It includes practical planning, acceptance of consequences, tolerance for uncertainty, and the willingness to be wrong.

## How to Apply
1. **Distinguish wishing from willing.** "I want to change careers" is a wish. "I have updated my CV, spoken to three people in the new field, and set a date to resign" is a will.
2. **Name the small death.** "What would you have to give up to make this choice? What possibility dies?" Naming the loss makes it real and manageable.
3. **Explore what the paralysis protects.** As long as you haven't chosen, you haven't risked being wrong. The paralysis preserves the fantasy of perfect options.
4. **Focus on action, not resolution.** "What would the first concrete step be?" is more useful than "what do you want to do?"

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant has been "thinking about" starting their own practice for three years but has taken no concrete steps.
**Application:** Yalom would say: "Three years is a long time to think about something without doing anything about it. I want to suggest that what you've been doing is not deciding — it's wishing. Wishing is pleasant because it keeps all the possibilities open: you can imagine the independent practice without risking the security of employment, without testing whether clients would come, without confronting whether you're willing to tolerate the financial uncertainty. Willing would look different. It would involve a specific timeline, concrete steps, and the acceptance that starting your own practice means leaving the safety of a salary. The question I'd ask is: what are you protecting yourself from by not acting? Is it the fear of failure? The fear of losing financial security? The fear of discovering that independent practice isn't what you imagined? Whatever it is, naming it is the first step toward willing rather than wishing."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce decision-making to cost-benefit analysis.
**Why:** When the problem is existential, rational analysis doesn't resolve it. The person already knows the options. What they lack is the willingness to commit.

**Don't:** Push for premature action.
**Why:** Understanding what the paralysis protects against is essential before encouraging action. Action without understanding often leads to another unkept resolution.
