# Group Therapy

## When to Use
- When Yalom's contributions to group therapy theory and practice need to be explained.
- When someone is designing or facilitating a therapeutic or support group.
- When the unique therapeutic power of groups — beyond what individual therapy offers — needs to be articulated.

## Core Concept
Yalom's *The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy* (first published 1970, now in its sixth edition) is the foundational text of modern group therapy. His framework identifies eleven therapeutic factors — mechanisms through which group therapy produces change — that operate in ways impossible in individual treatment.

The most important factors include universality (discovering that others share your struggles, which directly addresses existential isolation), altruism (the experience of being helpful to others, which generates meaning), interpersonal learning (using the group as a social microcosm to understand and change relational patterns), group cohesiveness (the therapeutic analog of the individual therapeutic relationship), catharsis (emotional expression in a safe environment), and instillation of hope (seeing others who have progressed).

The group is a social microcosm — a miniature version of each member's relational world. How a person behaves in the group — who they seek out, who they avoid, how they handle conflict, whether they take risks — mirrors how they behave outside the group. This makes the group a laboratory for interpersonal learning. The here-and-now principle applies with particular force: what is happening between group members right now is the most therapeutically potent material available.

Yalom's group facilitation centres on activating the here-and-now, promoting interpersonal feedback, and ensuring the group provides a corrective emotional experience. The facilitator does not lecture, advise, or dominate. The facilitator creates conditions in which the group's own therapeutic potential can operate. This requires restraint — the facilitator must resist the urge to be the most important person in the room — and courage — the facilitator must name dynamics that the group may prefer to avoid.

## How to Apply
1. **Design for interaction, not instruction.** The power of the group lies in what happens between members, not in what the facilitator says.
2. **Activate the here-and-now.** "What is happening between you two right now?" is as powerful in groups as in individual therapy.
3. **Promote interpersonal feedback.** Create norms where members give each other honest, caring feedback about their impact.
4. **Trust the group.** The therapeutic factors operate through the group's own dynamics. The facilitator's job is to create conditions, not to deliver treatment.

## Examples
**Situation:** A team facilitator says "I run regular team retrospectives but they feel superficial — people report what happened but never really engage with each other."
**Application:** Yalom would say: "Your retrospectives are structured around content — what happened, what worked, what didn't. That's useful for process improvement, but it misses the relational layer. What happened between the people? Who felt supported and who felt alone? Where did conflict simmer without being addressed? Where did someone take a risk and how did the team respond? If you want depth, you need to create safety for interpersonal honesty and then activate it. Try this: at the end of the content retrospective, ask each person to share one thing they appreciated about another team member's contribution and one thing they found difficult in the working relationship this sprint. That simple structure moves the conversation from content to process — from what happened to what happened between us. It will feel uncomfortable at first. That discomfort is the sign that something real is happening."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat group therapy as a cost-saving measure — "cheaper than individual."
**Why:** Group therapy offers something individual therapy cannot: the experience of belonging, the opportunity to help others, and the social microcosm for interpersonal learning. It is a modality, not a discount.

**Don't:** Over-facilitate.
**Why:** The group's therapeutic power comes from interactions between members, not from the facilitator's interventions. A facilitator who talks too much steals the group's agency.
