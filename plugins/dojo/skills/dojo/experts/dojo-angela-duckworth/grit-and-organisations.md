# Grit and Organisations

## When to Use
- When diagnosing why an organisation abandons strategies, programmes, or reforms before they can produce results.
- When designing organisational structures that support sustained effort over years.
- When leadership wants to build institutional perseverance without simply demanding it.

## Core Concept
Organisational grit is not the sum of individual grit scores. An organisation full of gritty individuals can still behave in ungritty ways if the structures, incentives, and leadership patterns undermine sustained effort. Conversely, a well-designed organisation can support sustained effort even among people who would not describe themselves as exceptionally gritty. The unit of analysis matters: organisational grit is a property of the system, not the people.

Three structural factors most commonly undermine organisational grit. First, short time horizons. If leadership turnover cycles every two or three years, every new leader brings a new strategy, and no strategy gets enough time to produce results. The organisation is not lacking grit — it is structurally prevented from sustaining direction. Second, misaligned incentives. If performance reviews, promotions, and funding decisions reward novel initiatives over sustained improvement of existing ones, the rational response is to start new things and abandon old ones. The organisation's reward structure selects against persistence. Third, punitive failure cultures. Deliberate practice requires experimentation, and experimentation produces failure. If the organisation punishes failure, people stop experimenting, and improvement stagnates — which looks like a lack of grit but is actually a rational adaptation to a hostile environment.

Duckworth's insight is that organisations can design for grit the same way families can. The Hard Thing Rule scales: every team commits to one sustained improvement effort, nobody abandons it before the defined review point, and teams choose their own focus area. Leadership participation is non-negotiable — if senior leaders exempt themselves from sustained effort, the message is clear regardless of what the values statement says.

The connection to culture is direct. A gritty organisation is one where difficulty is normalised, sustained effort is rewarded, and the combination of high standards and high support operates at the institutional level, not just the interpersonal level.

## How to Apply
1. **Audit time horizons.** "How long does a strategic priority typically survive before being replaced? If the answer is less than three years, the problem is structural, not motivational."
2. **Audit incentives.** "What actually gets rewarded — launching new initiatives or improving existing ones? The answer reveals whether the organisation's reward structure supports grit or undermines it."
3. **Check for leadership continuity.** "Does leadership turnover reset strategic direction? If so, build continuity mechanisms — institutional strategy documents, transition protocols, board-level commitment to direction."
4. **Normalise struggle at the institutional level.** "Create spaces where teams can report on difficulties without those reports being treated as evidence of failure."

## Examples
**Situation:** A development agency has launched and abandoned four monitoring systems in six years. Each new country director brings a different approach. Staff are blamed for "resistance to change."
**Application:** Duckworth would say: "This is not a staff grit problem — this is an organisational grit problem caused by leadership turnover. Every new country director resets the strategic direction, which means no approach gets enough time to embed. The staff who are being blamed for 'resistance to change' may actually be the grittiest people in the system — they have persisted through four cycles of disruption. Three fixes. First, separate strategic direction from leadership tenure. The monitoring approach should be a board-level decision that survives leadership transitions, not a country director's personal preference. Second, define a minimum commitment period for major systems — three years before any fundamental redesign is permitted. Incremental improvements are fine; wholesale replacement is not. Third, audit the incentives. If new country directors are rewarded for 'making their mark' — which means changing things — the organisation is structurally selecting against persistence. Reward directors who sustain and improve what exists, not just those who launch something new. The staff are not the problem. The system is the problem."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Equate organisational grit with hiring gritty people.
**Why:** Structural factors — incentives, time horizons, leadership continuity — override individual grit. Hiring persistent people into a system that punishes persistence produces frustration, not organisational grit.

**Don't:** Demand institutional persistence without providing institutional support.
**Why:** Telling an organisation to "stick with the strategy" while cutting resources, rotating leadership, and rewarding novelty is incoherent. Organisational grit requires organisational conditions.
