# Grit and Culture

## When to Use
- When the environment — family, team, organisation — is shaping whether people sustain effort or give up.
- When culture needs to be designed or modified to support long-term commitment.
- When someone's lack of grit is better explained by their context than by their character.

## Core Concept
Duckworth's research indicates that gritty individuals tend to be found in gritty cultures. Culture shapes grit by setting expectations about effort, providing models of sustained commitment, and establishing norms about whether difficulty is a signal to quit or to push through. The causal arrow runs both ways: gritty people create gritty cultures, and gritty cultures develop gritty people.

A gritty culture has two features that must coexist: high standards and high support. Demanding excellence without providing support produces burnout and attrition. Providing support without demanding excellence produces comfort and stagnation. The combination — "I believe you can do this, and I am going to help you get there" — is what Duckworth calls "wise practice" in parenting and what David Yeager extends into institutional design.

Cultures that undermine grit typically do so through their reward structures. An organisation that rewards talent displays over sustained improvement, that promotes based on charisma rather than consistent performance, that celebrates quick wins over long-term development, is structurally discouraging grit. Similarly, a culture that punishes failure harshly prevents the risk-taking and experimentation that deliberate practice requires.

The family is the first gritty culture. Duckworth's Hard Thing Rule — every family member does a hard thing, nobody quits in the middle, you choose your hard thing yourself — is a micro-culture intervention. It establishes three norms simultaneously: effort is expected, commitment is maintained, and autonomy in choosing the domain is respected.

## How to Apply
1. **Audit the culture's reward structure.** "What does this organisation actually reward — talent or effort? Quick wins or sustained improvement? Individual brilliance or consistent teamwork?"
2. **Check for the high standards + high support combination.** "Are standards demanding? Is support genuine? You need both."
3. **Look for role models of sustained effort.** "Are there visible examples of people who succeeded through persistence? Or are all the heroes 'naturals'?"
4. **Design cultural norms around effort.** "Normalise struggle. Celebrate improvement over performance. Share stories of persistence, not just results."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme director notices that her team has high turnover — good people join but leave within two years, citing lack of growth.
**Application:** Duckworth would say: "Before you look at the individuals who left, look at the culture they left. High turnover in a team of good people is usually a culture signal, not an individual character problem. Three questions to diagnose: First, does your team culture combine high standards with high support? If you demand excellence but don't invest in development — mentoring, training, stretch assignments — people who are motivated to grow will leave for environments that develop them. Second, does your reward structure value sustained contribution? If promotions go to whoever has the flashiest recent result rather than the most consistent long-term performance, you are systematically selecting against grit. Third, is struggle normalised? If the culture treats difficulty as evidence of a problem rather than as a normal part of growth, people who hit inevitable challenges will interpret their struggle as a signal to leave rather than as a signal to persist. The fix is cultural, not individual. You cannot recruit your way out of a culture problem — you will just recycle good people through a system that fails to retain them."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Blame individuals for lacking grit in an environment that punishes persistence.
**Why:** If the culture rewards short-term results, penalises failure, and provides no support for development, low grit is a rational adaptation, not a character flaw.

**Don't:** Reduce culture to slogans or values statements.
**Why:** Culture is what the organisation actually does, not what it says. A values statement that proclaims grit while rewarding quick wins is worse than no statement at all.
