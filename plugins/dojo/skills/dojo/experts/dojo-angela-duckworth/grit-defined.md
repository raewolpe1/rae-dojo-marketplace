# Grit Defined

## When to Use
- When someone asks what grit is and needs a clear, precise definition.
- When grit needs to be distinguished from perseverance alone, from conscientiousness, or from resilience.
- When introducing the construct to someone unfamiliar with Duckworth's work.

## Core Concept
Grit is the combination of passion and perseverance for especially long-term goals. It is not just hard work — hard work is the perseverance component. Grit also requires passion, which in Duckworth's framework means consistency of interest over time: the sustained commitment to a particular direction, domain, or purpose across years, not weeks or months.

The two components are equally important and conceptually distinct. Perseverance is the tendency to sustain effort and interest despite setbacks, plateaus, and lack of progress. Passion is the tendency to maintain consistent goals and interests over time rather than shifting from one pursuit to another. A person who works extremely hard but changes direction every six months has high perseverance but low passion — and is not gritty. A person who is deeply committed to a direction but gives up at the first difficulty has passion but low perseverance — also not gritty.

Grit is specifically about long-term goals — what Duckworth calls "ultimate concerns." These are the top-level objectives that organise years of effort. Below them sit mid-level goals (strategies and projects) and low-level goals (daily tasks). Grit means maintaining the top-level goal while being flexible about mid-level and low-level goals. Quitting a project is not a failure of grit if the ultimate concern remains intact. Changing a strategy is not quitting if it serves the same overarching purpose.

The effort equation formalises the relationship: talent × effort = skill, and skill × effort = achievement. Effort appears twice. A person of moderate talent who applies sustained, deliberate effort will develop substantial skill, and that skill multiplied by continued effort produces achievement that a more talented but less effortful person cannot match. This is not a denial of talent — talent matters — but a correction to the bias that overweights talent and underweights effort.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess both components.** When evaluating grit, look for both sustained effort (perseverance) and sustained direction (passion). One without the other is not grit.
2. **Distinguish top-level from lower-level goals.** "Are you changing your ultimate objective, or just your approach?" Changing approaches is strategic; changing objectives may indicate low passion.
3. **Apply the effort equation.** "Where is the effort in this equation? If talent is present but effort is inconsistent, the achievement will underperform what talent predicts."
4. **Frame grit as developable.** "Grit tends to increase with age and experience. It is a quality you build, not one you're born with."

## Examples
**Situation:** A young programme officer says "I think I might not be a gritty person — I've changed jobs three times in five years."
**Application:** Duckworth would say: "Let's look more carefully. Changing jobs is not necessarily evidence of low grit — it depends on what you were changing. If your top-level goal has remained consistent — say, improving educational outcomes for underserved communities — and each job change moved you closer to that goal or taught you something essential for it, then your direction has been consistent even though your positions have changed. That's strategic flexibility, not fickleness. But if each job change represented a new fascination that faded — education, then health, then governance, then private sector — that pattern does suggest the passion component of grit is underdeveloped. Not that you lack the capacity for passion, but that you may still be in the interest-development phase, exploring domains before finding the one that sustains your engagement over years. The question is not 'am I gritty?' — which sounds like a verdict. It's 'what am I building sustained commitment toward?' — which is a direction to develop."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Define grit as simply "working hard" or "never giving up."
**Why:** This misses the passion component entirely and reduces grit to stubbornness. Grit without passion is grinding. Grit without strategic flexibility is rigidity.

**Don't:** Present grit as a fixed personality trait you either have or lack.
**Why:** Grit develops over time through experience. Framing it as fixed discourages the effort to cultivate it — which is ironic for a concept about sustained effort.
