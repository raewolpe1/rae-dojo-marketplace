# Wise Practice (Strategic Quitting)

## When to Use
- When someone is deciding whether to quit or persist with a commitment, project, or career path.
- When grit is being confused with stubborn persistence in a failing approach.
- When the goal hierarchy — top-level versus lower-level goals — needs to be clarified.

## Core Concept
Grit does not mean never quitting. Duckworth is explicit about this: grit means maintaining commitment to your top-level goals while being flexible about how you pursue them. Quitting a tactic, a project, or even a job is entirely consistent with grit if it serves a higher-level goal. What grit opposes is not all quitting but aimless quitting — abandoning direction, not just method.

The goal hierarchy is the framework for making quit-or-persist decisions. At the top are your ultimate concerns — the broad purposes that give your life direction. Below them are mid-level goals — strategies and major projects. Below those are low-level goals — daily tasks and specific tactics. Grit operates primarily at the top level: you maintain your ultimate concerns even when mid-level and low-level goals need to change.

A researcher who leaves one university for another because the new institution better supports her research agenda has not quit — she has changed tactics in service of the same goal. A consultant who pivots from government to NGO work because the NGO sector better serves her development mission has not abandoned her direction — she has adapted her route. But a person who abandons their professional direction every two years because the current path got difficult may be failing at the passion component of grit.

The key question is: "Am I quitting on a bad day, or have I given this enough time to know it is genuinely the wrong path?" Quitting on a bad day — when everything feels hopeless because of a single setback — is usually premature. But persisting for years in a direction that is genuinely wrong — because you cannot distinguish grit from stubbornness — is equally problematic.

## How to Apply
1. **Clarify the goal hierarchy.** "What is your top-level goal? Is quitting this project abandoning that goal, or is it changing the route to that goal?"
2. **Check the timing.** "Are you deciding on a bad day? Give it 24 hours or a week before making a permanent decision."
3. **Test for genuine misfit.** "Have you given this enough time and effort to know it is genuinely wrong for you? Or are you in the difficult middle that every sustained effort goes through?"
4. **Distinguish tactical from directional quitting.** "Changing your method is strategy. Changing your goal may or may not be appropriate — but it should be a deliberate decision, not a reflexive response to difficulty."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme officer is considering leaving the development sector entirely after a particularly frustrating year of donor-driven work that felt meaningless.
**Application:** Duckworth would say: "Let's use the goal hierarchy. What is your top-level goal — your ultimate concern? If it is 'improving lives in underserved communities,' then the question is whether leaving the development sector abandons that goal or pursues it differently. You could improve lives through social enterprise, through local government, through education, through health systems. Leaving the development sector is quitting a mid-level strategy, not necessarily quitting your purpose. But — and this is important — are you making this decision from a clear head or from frustration? A particularly bad year does not tell you that the sector is wrong for you; it tells you that this year was bad. Before deciding, ask: is the frustration about the sector or about this specific organisation, this specific role, this specific donor relationship? If it's the specific context, changing contexts within the sector might be the right move. If it's the sector itself — if you have genuinely concluded after sustained engagement that the development sector cannot serve your purpose — then leaving is strategic, not weak. But take time to distinguish 'I'm exhausted' from 'this is genuinely wrong for me.' They feel similar but they are different."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Tell someone to never quit.
**Why:** Blind persistence in a genuinely wrong direction wastes years. Grit includes the wisdom to know when a lower-level goal should change in service of a higher-level one.

**Don't:** Make the quit-or-persist decision for someone.
**Why:** Only the person living the situation can assess whether their top-level goal has genuinely changed or whether they are having a bad day. The framework helps; the decision is theirs.
