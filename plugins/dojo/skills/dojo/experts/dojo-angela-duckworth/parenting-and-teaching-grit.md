# Parenting and Teaching Grit

## When to Use
- When the question is about developing grit in children, students, or early-career professionals.
- When designing educational programmes, youth development initiatives, or mentorship structures.
- When the "wise parenting" model — demanding and supportive — needs application.

## Core Concept
Duckworth's research on how grit develops draws on Diana Baumrind's parenting styles research. The authoritative parenting style — combining high demands with high responsiveness — is associated with the best outcomes, including the development of grit. This is the "wise parenting" model: setting high standards, providing warm support, granting age-appropriate autonomy, and modelling sustained effort.

The developmental trajectory matters. In childhood and adolescence, the emphasis should be on interest development — broad exploration, multiple activities, discovering what captures attention. Pressuring a young person to commit deeply to one domain before they have explored broadly is counterproductive. Interest must precede passion. The Hard Thing Rule provides a structured approach: everyone does a hard thing, you can't quit until the natural stopping point (end of term, end of season), and you choose the hard thing yourself. This builds the experience of sustained effort without imposing premature commitment.

As young people mature, the emphasis shifts from exploration to deepening. Late adolescence and early adulthood is when consistent interest typically begins to emerge — when someone moves from "I like lots of things" to "I really care about this particular thing." Teachers and mentors play a crucial role at this stage by modelling deliberate practice, connecting effort to purpose, and providing the scaffolding that helps young people push through difficulty rather than abandon it.

Duckworth explicitly cautions against trying to "teach grit" as a classroom subject. Grit develops through experience — through doing hard things, persisting, and experiencing the rewards of sustained effort. You can create the conditions, but you cannot instruct someone into being gritty.

## How to Apply
1. **Match expectations to developmental stage.** Early: broad exploration. Middle: deepening engagement. Late: sustained commitment.
2. **Apply the Hard Thing Rule.** Everyone does a hard thing. You can't quit in the middle. You choose your hard thing.
3. **Model grit, don't lecture about it.** "Children learn more from what you do than from what you say. Show them your own sustained effort."
4. **Provide scaffolding during difficulty.** "The moment a young person wants to quit is the teaching moment. Help them through it — don't do it for them, but don't leave them alone with it either."

## Examples
**Situation:** A youth development programme manager asks "How do we build grit into our curriculum for township youth aged 14–18?"
**Application:** Duckworth would say: "Three design principles. First, give them a choice of hard things. The programme should offer multiple challenging activities — sports, music, community projects, technical skills — and each participant should choose one. Autonomy in choosing is essential; you cannot develop grit by forcing someone into a domain they didn't select. Second, build in commitment structures. Whatever they choose, they commit for the full programme cycle — a term, a semester. They cannot quit when it gets hard. But the commitment has a defined endpoint, so the ask is bounded. Third, make the challenge progressive. Start achievable, then increase difficulty so participants experience both early success and later struggle. The struggle is the point — that is where grit develops. And throughout, provide mentors who model sustained effort and who support participants through difficulty without rescuing them. The mentor's role is not to make the hard thing easy but to make the hard thing survivable. And be careful about one thing: do not use grit language to blame participants for dropping out. If your programme has high attrition, examine the programme before examining the participants."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Teach grit as a lesson plan.
**Why:** Grit develops through experience. A lecture about perseverance does not produce perseverance. Create the conditions — challenge, commitment, support — and grit develops through the lived experience.

**Don't:** Pressure premature commitment.
**Why:** Forcing a 14-year-old to "find their passion" and commit deeply is developmentally inappropriate. They need exploration first. Commitment deepens naturally through engagement.
