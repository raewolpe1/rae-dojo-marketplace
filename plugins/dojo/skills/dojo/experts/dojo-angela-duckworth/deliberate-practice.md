# Deliberate Practice

## When to Use
- When someone is putting in effort but not improving.
- When the distinction between "doing the work" and "practising deliberately" needs making.
- When designing training or development programmes that build genuine skill.

## Core Concept
Deliberate practice, drawn from Anders Ericsson's research and central to Duckworth's framework, is what makes effort productive. It is not simply doing the work — it is a specific kind of practice with four defining features: a clearly defined stretch goal that targets a specific weakness; full concentration and effort; immediate and informative feedback; and repetition with reflection and refinement.

Most people do not practise deliberately. Once they reach a level of competence that is "good enough" for their role, they shift from deliberate practice to autopilot — performing the skill without the effortful concentration on improvement. A consultant who has given fifty presentations does not automatically give better presentations than one who has given ten. If presentations eleven through fifty were performed on autopilot — same structure, same approach, no targeted improvement — the additional experience did not produce additional skill. Only deliberate practice does.

Duckworth emphasises that deliberate practice is not enjoyable in the way flow is enjoyable. Flow is the pleasurable state of being fully absorbed in an activity at the right challenge level. Deliberate practice is the effortful state of working on things you cannot yet do well. It feels like struggle, not flow. The gritty person does deliberate practice not because they enjoy it in the moment but because they are committed to improvement over time. They tolerate the discomfort of practising at the edge of their ability because they have passion for the ultimate goal.

The practical implication is that effort without deliberate practice produces diminishing returns. More hours of the same quality practice yield progressively less improvement. The person who practices deliberately for two hours outperforms the person who practices mindlessly for five hours.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the specific weakness.** "What specifically are you trying to improve? If you can't name it precisely, your practice is not deliberate."
2. **Check for stretch goals.** "Are you practising at the edge of your current ability, or are you repeating what you already do well?"
3. **Ensure feedback loops.** "How do you know if you're improving? Who or what gives you immediate feedback on your performance?"
4. **Design for concentration.** "Are you fully focused during practice, or are you going through the motions?"

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluator says "I've written dozens of evaluation reports but I don't think my writing is getting any better."
**Application:** Duckworth would say: "That is completely consistent with the research. Experience alone does not produce improvement — deliberate practice does. You have been writing reports on autopilot: same structure, same analytical approach, same writing style. Each report is competent but not substantially better than the last. To shift into deliberate practice, you need to do four things. First, identify a specific weakness — perhaps your executive summaries are too long, or your recommendations are too vague, or your data visualisation is weak. Pick one. Second, set a stretch goal for that specific weakness — 'my next executive summary will be under 500 words and will lead with the most counterintuitive finding.' Third, get feedback — ask a colleague whose writing you admire to read that executive summary and tell you specifically what works and what doesn't. Fourth, revise based on the feedback and then do it again on the next report. This is effortful. It is slower than writing on autopilot. It does not feel productive in the moment. But it is the only path from competent to excellent."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Equate time spent with deliberate practice.
**Why:** Ten thousand hours of mindless repetition does not produce expertise. Ericsson's research — which Duckworth draws on — specifies that the hours must be deliberate practice, not just accumulated experience.

**Don't:** Suggest deliberate practice should feel good.
**Why:** It feels effortful and uncomfortable because you are working on what you cannot yet do well. If practice feels easy and enjoyable, it is probably not deliberate.
