# Hard Thing Rule

## When to Use
- When someone wants a practical, implementable structure for developing grit.
- When families, teams, or organisations want a micro-culture of sustained effort.
- When the concept needs explaining or adapting to a professional context.

## Core Concept
The Hard Thing Rule is Duckworth's most concrete practical tool. It has three parts. First, everyone does a hard thing — an activity that requires daily deliberate practice. Second, you can quit, but not until a natural stopping point — end of the season, end of the term, after the performance. You cannot quit on a bad day. Third, you choose your hard thing yourself. No one picks it for you.

The genius of the rule is in the combination. The requirement to do something hard builds the experience of sustained effort. The prohibition on quitting in the middle builds the habit of persisting through difficulty. And the autonomy of choice ensures that the effort is directed by interest, not coercion — which is essential because grit requires passion, and passion cannot be imposed.

Duckworth applied this rule in her own family, with each family member — including the parents — committing to a hard thing. The parents' participation is not incidental; it models sustained effort and prevents the rule from feeling like a punishment imposed by adults on children.

In professional contexts, the Hard Thing Rule can be adapted. A team might adopt a version where each member commits to developing one professional skill over a quarter — not their comfort zone, but a stretch — and reports on progress. The combination of public commitment, a defined time horizon, and autonomous choice creates the same grit-building dynamic.

## How to Apply
1. **Choose something genuinely hard.** "It must require deliberate practice — not something you already do well, but something that stretches you."
2. **Commit to a defined period.** "You will do this for one term, one season, one quarter. You cannot quit before the period ends."
3. **Choose it yourself.** "No one assigns your hard thing. You pick what interests you. Autonomy protects passion."
4. **Apply it collectively.** "The rule works best when everyone participates, including leaders. It creates shared culture, not imposed obligation."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consulting team leader wants to build a culture of continuous improvement. The team is competent but has stopped growing.
**Application:** Duckworth would say: "Adapt the Hard Thing Rule for your team. At the start of each quarter, every team member — including you — selects one professional skill they will deliberately practise for the next three months. It must be something they find challenging but relevant: data visualisation, facilitation, policy writing, quantitative analysis, presenting to hostile audiences. They commit publicly to the team, and at the end of the quarter they share what they learned. Two non-negotiable conditions: first, you participate. If the leader exempts themselves, the rule becomes a mandate rather than a culture. Second, everyone chooses their own skill. If you assign skills, you undermine the passion component — people persist at things they chose, not things imposed on them. You will find that the team's competence increases measurably over two or three cycles, and more importantly, the norm shifts from 'we're good enough' to 'we're always developing.' That norm is gritty culture in miniature."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Impose the hard thing.
**Why:** Autonomy is essential. Forced effort builds resentment, not grit. The person must choose what they find challenging and interesting.

**Don't:** Allow quitting on bad days.
**Why:** The prohibition on mid-commitment quitting is where the grit-building happens. The moment of difficulty is the teaching moment — surviving it is the experience that builds the habit of persistence.
