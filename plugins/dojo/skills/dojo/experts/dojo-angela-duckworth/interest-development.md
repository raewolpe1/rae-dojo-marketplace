# Interest Development

## When to Use
- When someone has not yet found a sustained interest and is searching.
- When the developmental process from curiosity to interest to passion needs explaining.
- When someone is frustrated that they cannot "find their passion."

## Core Concept
Interest is the first of Duckworth's four psychological assets of grit, and it is foundational — without genuine interest, sustained effort becomes grinding. But interest is not discovered in a flash of revelation. It develops through a process: triggering (initial attraction), engagement (deeper exploration), and deepening (sustained, evolving fascination that becomes part of your identity).

The triggering phase involves exposure to many different activities, domains, and ideas. Most triggers do not develop into lasting interests. This is normal and necessary — exploration is not a failure of commitment; it is the prerequisite for finding something worth committing to. Young people and career-changers who feel pressure to "find their passion" often misunderstand this phase. They expect a dramatic moment of clarity. Instead, interest develops gradually through repeated engagement with something that captures their attention and rewards their curiosity.

The engagement phase involves going deeper — taking a class, joining a community, reading extensively, practising the skill. Interest deepens when the domain reveals its complexity. Activities that seem simple from outside become fascinating when you discover the layers of mastery within them. This is where many people drop off: the initial novelty fades, the difficulty increases, and without the deeper engagement that reveals complexity, they move to the next novelty.

Mature passion — the consistency of interest that grit requires — develops when the domain has become part of your identity. You are not just someone who does this work; you are someone who is this kind of practitioner. This typically takes years and cannot be rushed. Telling someone to "follow their passion" before they have had the exploration and engagement phases is setting them up for frustration.

## How to Apply
1. **Normalise exploration.** "Not knowing what you're passionate about is not a problem — it's a developmental stage. You need more exploration, not more determination."
2. **Encourage deeper engagement before quitting.** "You've tried this for two months. That's enough to know if there's initial interest, but not enough to know if deeper engagement would be rewarding. Go a little further before deciding."
3. **Look for what captures attention naturally.** "What do you find yourself reading about, thinking about, or doing when no one is making you?"
4. **Don't force it.** "Passion cannot be manufactured through willpower. It can only be discovered through engagement and deepened through practice."

## Examples
**Situation:** A recent graduate entering the development sector says "Everyone seems to have a specific cause they're passionate about — education, health, climate. I find them all interesting but I'm not passionate about any one thing."
**Application:** Duckworth would say: "You are exactly where you should be. You are in the exploration phase, and that is the right place for someone early in their career. The people who seem to have clear passions have typically been engaging with their domain for years — you are comparing your month three to their year ten. My advice: don't force a choice. Take the opportunity that lets you engage with the widest range of work. As you engage more deeply, you will notice that some topics pull you in more than others — you read more about them, you think about them outside of work, you want to understand the nuances. Those attractions are the early signals of interest. Follow them, go deeper, and see what happens. Passion will develop through engagement, not through a moment of clarity sitting at your desk trying to figure out what you care about. The only mistake you can make is to commit prematurely to something you haven't engaged with deeply enough, or to keep skimming the surface of everything without ever going deep into anything."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Tell someone to "follow their passion" if they don't have one yet.
**Why:** This advice presupposes that passion exists fully formed, waiting to be discovered. For most people, passion develops through a process. The advice should be "develop your interests" not "follow your passion."

**Don't:** Interpret exploration as a lack of grit.
**Why:** Exploration is the necessary precursor to passion. A young person who tries many things before committing is not failing at grit — they are building the foundation for it.
