# Back Pain Diagnosis — Finding the Pain Mechanism

## When to Use
- When someone wants to understand the source of their back pain rather than chase symptoms.
- When you must establish the mechanism before recommending any exercise or correction.
- When teaching the assessment-first principle that defines the McGill Method.

## Fails When
- **Red flags are present.** Saddle numbness, bladder/bowel changes, progressive leg weakness, fever, unexplained weight loss — these bypass biomechanics and go straight to a physician.
- **It replaces a needed clinical exam.** Self-assessment orients; complex or persistent cases need a skilled clinician.
- **The user wants a one-word diagnosis.** The goal is a mechanism, not a label.

## Core Concept
The defining error in back care is to treat before knowing what provokes the pain. "Back pain" is not a diagnosis; it is a symptom with many distinct mechanisms, and two of them — flexion intolerance and extension intolerance — require opposite advice. Give a flexion-intolerant back the flexion exercises meant for an extension-intolerant one and you will make it worse. So assessment must come first, always. The purpose of assessment is not to name a pathology; it is to identify the specific posture, motion, and load that switch the pain on, so that the very first intervention can be to stop doing exactly that.

McGill's assessment is *provocative*: rather than avoiding the pain, you deliberately and carefully reproduce it, because reproducing it tells you its mechanism. A handful of simple observations locate most backs. Does bending forward — reaching for your shoes, slumping in a chair, rounding to pick something up — bring the pain on? That points to flexion intolerance, by far the most common pattern, especially in seated workers. Does leaning backward, standing for a long time, or lying face-down hurt instead, while sitting feels like relief? That points to extension intolerance. Does a particular twist or side-bend trigger it? That points to a rotational or lateral mechanism. Is it worst first thing in the morning (when discs are swollen and flexion-sensitive) or worst after being upright all day (more suggestive of joint or stenotic mechanisms)? Each answer narrows the mechanism.

Clinicians using the full McGill Method add standardised provocative tests and movement screens, and they assess the patient's tolerance — how much load and what postures the tissue accepts before complaining. But the underlying logic is available to anyone: pay close, honest attention to what makes it worse and what makes it better, in terms of direction (flexion vs extension), load, and duration. Keep a brief log if needed. The pattern usually reveals itself within days.

Two cautions complete the picture. First, the imaging trap. A disc bulge or degeneration on an MRI is extremely common in pain-free people, so a scan finding is not proof of the pain source. The mechanism is established by how the back behaves mechanically, not by the picture. Second, the red-flag screen. Before any of this, rule out the signs of serious pathology — they are rare but they are emergencies, and biomechanics has nothing to offer them.

Once the mechanism is known, everything downstream becomes simple. If flexion is the trigger, the first move is to eliminate unnecessary flexion from the day and to learn to maintain a neutral spine; only then do you build stability. If extension is the trigger, you do the reverse. The assessment is not an academic preliminary. It is the single most consequential step in the entire process, because it determines whether every later choice helps or harms.

## How to Apply
1. **Screen for red flags first.** Saddle anaesthesia, bladder/bowel changes, progressive weakness, fever, unexplained weight loss → physician, promptly. Stop here if present.
2. **Test the direction.** Does forward bending/slumping provoke it (flexion intolerance) or does leaning back/standing/lying prone (extension intolerance)?
3. **Test load and duration.** Does it worsen with load, with sustained postures, after sitting, or after standing?
4. **Note the timing.** Worse on waking (flexion-sensitive disc) or worse after being upright (joint/stenotic patterns)?
5. **Identify the trigger, then plan to remove it first.** The mechanism dictates the entire downstream plan. Don't anchor on an MRI finding.

## Examples
**Situation:** A desk worker's pain is worst when slumping and reaching forward, eases when standing and walking.
**Application:** Classic flexion intolerance. First intervention: eliminate slump and unnecessary flexion, preserve the lumbar curve, build stability — not flexion stretches.
**Result:** Removing the trigger settles the pain before any exercise is added.

**Situation:** Someone's pain is worst when standing long or lying face-down, and sitting relieves it.
**Application:** Points to extension intolerance — the opposite mechanism. The flexion advice that helps the first person would aggravate this one. The plan inverts.
**Result:** Correctly matched advice; avoidance of the common mistake of universal "back exercises."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Prescribe before identifying the mechanism.
**Why:** Flexion- and extension-intolerant backs need opposite advice. A wrong guess re-provokes the injury.

**Don't:** Diagnose from the MRI.
**Why:** Disc findings are common in pain-free people and correlate poorly with the pain source.

**Don't:** Avoid the pain entirely during assessment.
**Why:** Carefully reproducing the pain is what reveals the mechanism. (Within reason — never push into severe or neurological symptoms.)
