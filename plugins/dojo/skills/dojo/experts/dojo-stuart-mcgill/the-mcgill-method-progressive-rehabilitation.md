# The McGill Method — Progressive Rehabilitation

## When to Use
- When someone with low-back pain wants a structured path back to capacity.
- When explaining why recovery must follow an order.
- When a person keeps flaring because they jump straight to strengthening or sport.

## Fails When
- **Red flags are present.** Serious pathology needs medical care first.
- **The mechanism is unknown.** Without the trigger identified, the first step cannot be taken.
- **Stages are skipped.** Building strength on an unstable, still-provoked base re-injures.

## Core Concept
The McGill Method is a structured, individualised approach to low-back rehabilitation built on a single governing idea: remove the cause of pain first, then rebuild capacity in a deliberate order. It is the opposite of the scattershot approach in which a painful back is handed a generic sheet of exercises. Every step is sequenced, and the sequence is not optional — skipping ahead is the most common reason people fail to recover or keep relapsing.

The method begins with assessment, because you cannot remove a trigger you have not identified. The clinician (or the attentive individual) finds the specific posture, motion, and load that provoke the pain — the pain mechanism — through careful provocative testing. Is this a flexion-intolerant back, an extension-intolerant one, a rotational pattern? The answer dictates everything downstream, because the corrective for one is the aggravator for another.

The first intervention is subtractive: eliminate the provoking movements from daily life. If flexion is the trigger, that means removing unnecessary flexion — fixing the slumped sitting, the rounded lifting, the toe-touch stretches — and learning to maintain a neutral spine through the day. This step alone often settles much of the pain, because the tissue stops being re-injured every day. Nothing is "strengthened" yet; the leaking bucket is simply patched. Many people are astonished that the biggest gains come from what they *stop* doing.

Next, pain-free movement is grooved. The person learns to move — to hinge, to get out of a chair, to roll over in bed, to walk — without provoking the mechanism, replacing the faulty patterns that contributed to the injury with spine-sparing ones. Walking is introduced and progressed as a foundational tonic, because it loads the spine gently and rhythmically and pumps nutrition into the discs.

Then comes building stability, primarily through the Big Three (modified curl-up, side plank, bird-dog), trained for endurance. This develops the muscular stiffness that holds the spine neutral under the demands of daily life and protects it from the buckling micro-movements that provoke pain. Stability and endurance precede strength deliberately: a spine must be controllable before it is loaded heavily.

Only after stability and endurance are established does the plan progress to strength, and after strength, if the person's goals require it, to speed, power, and finally task- or sport-specific demands. This is the stability-mobility pyramid in action: each stage rests on the one below, and a stage built before its foundation is built on sand.

Two features run through the whole method. First, it is *individualised* — there is no universal programme, because hip anatomy, stiffness type, pain mechanism, and goals differ between people. Second, it is *mechanism-driven* throughout — every choice is justified by what loads or protects the specific tissue at fault. The method's discipline is patience: the temptation is always to skip to strengthening or to return to sport before the foundation is laid, and that temptation is precisely what produces the recurrent back that never fully recovers. Recovery is the unglamorous sequence, respected in order.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess and find the mechanism.** Identify the provoking posture/motion/load before anything else. (Screen red flags first.)
2. **Remove the trigger.** Eliminate the provoking movements from daily life and learn neutral-spine habits. Expect much of the pain to settle here.
3. **Groove pain-free movement.** Replace faulty patterns (sitting, lifting, rolling, walking) with spine-sparing ones; introduce walking.
4. **Build stability and endurance.** The Big Three, trained for endurance, to develop protective stiffness.
5. **Progress in order:** strength, then power, then task-specific demands — only once each prior stage is solid. Keep it individualised throughout.

## Examples
**Situation:** A person with recurrent flexion-intolerant pain keeps doing core-strengthening classes and keeps flaring.
**Application:** They have skipped to strengthening while still provoking the back daily. Step back: remove the flexion triggers, groove neutral movement, then build stability with the Big Three before any class.
**Result:** The flares stop because the daily re-injury stops; capacity then builds on a settled base.

**Situation:** An athlete wants to return to sport two weeks after a back injury.
**Application:** Respect the pyramid. The base — pain-free movement and stability — must precede strength and the explosive, sport-specific demands. Rushing the top of the pyramid re-injures.
**Result:** A slower but durable return, without the relapse cycle.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Start with strengthening.
**Why:** Strengthening a still-provoked, unstable back builds on sand and re-injures. Remove the trigger and build stability first.

**Don't:** Use a generic exercise sheet.
**Why:** The plan must match the individual's mechanism, anatomy, and goals. Generic prescriptions ignore the trigger.

**Don't:** Rush back to sport or heavy load.
**Why:** Skipping pyramid stages is the classic cause of the recurrent, never-fully-healed back.
