# The Stiffness Spectrum and Athlete Archetypes

## When to Use
- When someone asks what kind of training suits their body.
- When explaining why a programme that helped a friend injured them.
- When matching mobility/stability emphasis to individual type.

## Fails When
- **It becomes an excuse.** "I'm just stiff" is not a reason to skip mobility a person genuinely needs.
- **The pain mechanism is ignored.** Type guides emphasis; the trigger still governs the plan.
- **Red flags are present.** Refer.

## Core Concept
People are not built the same, and one of the most important individual differences for training is where a person sits on the spectrum from supple to stiff. At one end are the naturally loose-jointed — the people who can fold forward and place their palms flat on the floor, who are hypermobile, whose tissues are elastic. At the other end are the naturally stiff — the people who have always been "tight," whose joints have less passive range, whose tissues are dense and resistant. Most people fall somewhere between, but the two poles need almost opposite training, and prescribing one pole's programme to the other is a reliable way to cause injury.

The supple, hypermobile person already has abundant range — often too much. Their problem is rarely a lack of flexibility; it is a lack of *control* over the range they have. A hypermobile lumbar spine is an unstable one, prone to drifting into end-range positions where passive structures, not muscles, take the load. For this person, more stretching is actively counterproductive: it adds range they cannot control and pushes them deeper into the vulnerable end-range. Their training emphasis must be *stability* — building the muscular stiffness to control their excessive mobility, learning to hold neutral, avoiding the end-range positions their loose joints fall into so easily. The dancer and the contortionist are the extreme cases; they need to be taught to stiffen, not to stretch.

The naturally stiff person is the opposite. Their tissues resist motion; their joints have limited passive range. For them, some targeted mobility — at the hips and thoracic spine especially — is genuinely useful, because they may lack the range to hinge or rotate well, and the deficit forces compensation into the spine. But the stiff person also tends to be well-suited to heavy, stable loading: their dense, resistant tissues tolerate compression and produce force well. The powerlifter archetype is often a naturally stiff person, and trying to turn them into a supple gymnast through aggressive stretching can de-stabilise a system that derived its strength from its stiffness.

The practical and frequently-missed point is that this is why copying someone else's programme is dangerous. The flexibility routine that keeps a hypermobile yoga teacher functional would, applied to a stiff powerlifter, strip away the stiffness that protects them; the heavy stable loading that builds a powerlifter would, applied to a hypermobile person without first building control, drive them deeper into uncontrolled end-range. The programme must fit the person's type, not the person the programme was designed for. "It worked for my training partner" is not evidence that it will work for you; it may be evidence of the opposite.

Layered onto the stiffness spectrum is anatomical variation, especially in the hips. Hip sockets differ in depth and orientation between individuals, which determines how deep a person can squat or hinge before the joint pinches and the spine is forced to compensate. The right squat depth for one person's hips is the wrong depth for another's. So matching training to the individual means reading both the stiffness type and the joint anatomy, and then setting mobility and loading emphases accordingly. The general direction of the assessment: identify whether the person needs more control of existing range (the supple) or more usable range plus their natural capacity for stable loading (the stiff), and program from there. The aim is never to make everyone the same; it is to help each body express its own design well.

## How to Apply
1. **Locate the person on the spectrum.** Naturally supple/hypermobile, naturally stiff, or in between?
2. **For the supple:** emphasise stability and control; avoid more stretching; teach holding neutral and staying out of end-range.
3. **For the stiff:** add targeted hip and thoracic mobility; leverage their suitability for heavy, stable loading.
4. **Never copy others' programmes wholesale.** Match the plan to this person's type and hip anatomy, not to whoever the programme was built for.
5. **Read the hip anatomy** to set squat/hinge depth — the joint, not the textbook, sets the range.

## Examples
**Situation:** A hypermobile person does daily deep stretching and still has back pain.
**Application:** They have too much uncontrolled range. Stop adding stretch; emphasise stability and end-range control with the Big Three and neutral-spine training.
**Result:** The instability driving the pain is addressed; flares reduce.

**Situation:** A stiff, strong lifter is told to do a gymnast's mobility routine and starts feeling unstable under the bar.
**Application:** Aggressive global stretching is stripping protective stiffness. Keep targeted hip/thoracic mobility only; preserve the stable loading that suits their type.
**Result:** Stability and strength preserved; mobility added only where it helps.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Prescribe stretching to a hypermobile person.
**Why:** They lack control, not range. More range pushes them deeper into vulnerable end-range.

**Don't:** Force a stiff, strong body into a supple-athlete routine.
**Why:** It strips the protective stiffness their strength depends on.

**Don't:** Copy a programme because it worked for someone else.
**Why:** The right plan depends on your stiffness type and hip anatomy, which may be the opposite of theirs.
