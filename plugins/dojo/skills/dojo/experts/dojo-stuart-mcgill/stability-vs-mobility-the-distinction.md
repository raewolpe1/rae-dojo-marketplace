# Stability vs Mobility — The Distinction

## When to Use
- When someone asks whether to stretch or strengthen their back.
- When diagnosing why a lower back is overworked (often stiff hips).
- When correcting the assumption that more flexibility is always better.

## Fails When
- **The pain mechanism is unknown.** This framework guides training, but a provocative back still needs its trigger identified first.
- **It is applied as a rigid rule.** Individuals vary; some thoracic or hip prescriptions differ by anatomy.
- **Red flags are present.** Refer.

## Core Concept
One of the most useful ideas in spine care is that stability and mobility are different tasks, and the healthy body assigns them to different joints. The lumbar spine's job is primarily *stability* — it should be stiff, holding a neutral position, resisting unwanted motion. The hips and the thoracic (upper) spine are built for *mobility* — they are the joints that should move. Injury and pain very often arise when this assignment is reversed: when the hips and upper back are stiff, and the lower back becomes loose and mobile to compensate.

The principle behind this is sometimes called the joint-by-joint view, and the spine illustrates it perfectly. The body is a stacked alternation of joints that want to be mobile and joints that want to be stable. The ankle wants mobility, the knee stability, the hip mobility, the lumbar spine stability, the thoracic spine mobility, and so on. When a "mobile" joint loses its motion, the adjacent "stable" joint is forced to give up its stability and move instead — and the joint that moves when it should be still is the joint that gets hurt.

The classic and most common example is the desk worker with stiff hips. Years of sitting shorten and stiffen the hip musculature and reduce hip range. When this person bends to pick something up, the hips cannot supply the motion, so the lumbar spine flexes to make up the difference — and now the spine is doing the hips' job, under load, repeatedly. The lower back was never meant to be the primary bending joint. It pays for the borrowed motion with cumulative disc loading. The pain presents in the back, but the deficit is in the hips.

The corrective follows directly: restore motion where motion belongs, and demand stiffness where stiffness belongs. Mobilise the hips and the thoracic spine — these can and should move freely. Stabilise the lumbar spine — train it to hold neutral and resist motion, which is exactly what the Big Three develop. The instinct to "stretch the tight lower back" is usually exactly backwards. The lower back is not tight in a way that needs stretching; it is often *overworked* and *under-stabilised*, and it is being asked to move because the hips won't. Stretching it into more flexion adds motion to the joint that already has too much and re-provokes the very loading that hurts it.

This is why "more mobility is always better" is false, and sometimes harmful. A lumbar spine with excessive mobility is an unstable, vulnerable spine. The goal is the *right* mobility at the *right* joints, paired with the right stiffness at the others. A person who can touch their palms to the floor but cannot hold a neutral spine under load has trained the wrong quality at the wrong joint.

There is, finally, individual variation layered on top of this map — people differ in how stiff or supple they naturally are, and in hip anatomy that governs how much hip motion is even available. But the basic assignment holds widely: mobile hips and thoracic spine, stable lumbar spine. Get that right and a great deal of back trouble simply does not occur.

## How to Apply
1. **Assign correctly:** hips and thoracic spine = mobility; lumbar spine = stability. Train accordingly.
2. **Look upstream and downstream for the deficit.** Back pain often signals stiff hips, not a weak back. Test hip range.
3. **Mobilise the hips and upper back;** stiffen the lumbar spine with the Big Three. Resist the urge to stretch the lower back into flexion.
4. **Reject "more flexibility is always better."** Excess lumbar mobility is instability. Aim for the right mobility at the right joint.
5. **Layer in individual variation** — adjust hip-mobility goals to the person's anatomy and stiffness type.

## Examples
**Situation:** A seated worker bends and rounds the lower back to lift, with limited hip motion.
**Application:** The stiff hips force lumbar flexion. Mobilise the hips, teach the hip hinge, stabilise the trunk — rather than stretching the "tight" back.
**Result:** The back stops borrowing the hips' motion; loaded flexion drops.

**Situation:** A flexible person can fold forward easily but has recurrent back pain.
**Application:** The flexibility is not protective; the lumbar spine may be too mobile and under-stabilised. Prioritise stability over more stretching.
**Result:** A stiffer, better-controlled spine and fewer flare-ups.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Stretch a "tight" lower back into flexion.
**Why:** It is usually overworked and under-stabilised, not short. Stretching adds motion to a joint that needs stiffness.

**Don't:** Pursue maximal flexibility everywhere.
**Why:** Excess lumbar mobility is instability. The goal is mobility at the hips and thoracic spine, stability at the lumbar spine.

**Don't:** Treat the painful site as the problem site.
**Why:** Back pain frequently originates in stiff hips. Fix the deficit, not just the symptom.
