# McGill on the Physiotherapy and Surgical Establishment

## When to Use
- When someone is weighing back surgery for a disc bulge.
- When explaining why stretching and passive treatments often disappoint.
- When framing the gap between common practice and the mechanism-based evidence.

## Fails When
- **It becomes blanket dismissal.** McGill criticises specific practices, not physiotherapy or surgery wholesale. Many clinicians do excellent mechanism-based work.
- **Genuine surgical indications are downplayed.** Real neurological compromise and red-flag pathology need surgery; he does not dispute that.
- **Red flags are present.** Refer urgently.

## Core Concept
McGill's stance toward parts of the back-care establishment is one of evidence-anchored, calm dissent — not hostility, but the steady disagreement of a scientist who has measured what actually loads and protects the spine and watched common practice diverge from it for forty years. His critique falls on specific practices, and it is important to state it precisely rather than as a wholesale rejection.

The first target is *stretching-as-cure*, especially the reflexive prescription to stretch a painful back into flexion. For the most common back — the flexion-intolerant one — pulling the knees to the chest or touching the toes feels wonderful for about twenty minutes, because the stretch stimulates receptors that dampen pain perception. The relief is real, and that is exactly the trap: the movement that produced the relief is also another cycle of the loaded flexion that caused the injury. People do it several times a day, every day, sanding the wound while admiring the bandage. The relief is the bait; the flexion is the hook. McGill does not oppose all stretching — mobility at the hips and thoracic spine is valuable, and specific directional exercises matched to an assessed mechanism are fine — but the generic "stretch your sore back" advice is, for many, precisely backwards.

The second target is *passive modalities offered as cures* — heat, ultrasound, generic manipulation, and the like, applied without identifying or addressing the pain mechanism. These can provide temporary symptom relief, which has a place, but offered as treatments they leave the cause in place. McGill's objection is the substitution of passive symptom-dampening for the active, mechanism-based work of finding the trigger, removing it, and rebuilding capacity. A treatment that does not change what loads the tissue is not a cure.

The third and sharpest target is *surgery prescribed from imaging rather than from a mechanical assessment*. Disc bulges, degeneration, and "abnormalities" are extremely common in people with no pain at all — image a population of pain-free fifty-year-olds and you will find disc bulges in a large fraction of them. A finding on a scan therefore correlates poorly with the source of pain, and operating on the picture rather than on a carefully established mechanism is too often operating on the wrong target. Many people offered surgery for a disc bulge could resolve their pain by identifying and removing the mechanical trigger and rebuilding stability — the conservative path tried properly first. McGill is emphatic, however, that this is not anti-surgery: where there is genuine, progressive neurological compromise or red-flag pathology, surgery is necessary and appropriate. His objection is to surgery as a default response to an MRI finding before a proper mechanical assessment and a real conservative trial have been done.

The fourth target is the *static-posture cult* — the idea that there is one correct posture to hold all day and that holding it is the answer. The spine is built to move; the damage comes from sustained loading in any single position. "Sit up straight and hold it" replaces one sustained posture with another. The answer is variety and interruption, not a perfect frozen shape.

The unifying thread is mechanism. McGill's complaint about each of these practices is the same: they skip the step of identifying what actually provokes the specific tissue, and they substitute something — a stretch, a modality, an operation, a posture rule — that feels like treatment but does not address the cause. The corrective is always to return to mechanism: assess, find the trigger, remove it, rebuild. Presented with a user weighing any of these options, the right move is not to tell them what to choose but to give them the questions that reveal whether the proposed path addresses their mechanism or merely their symptom — and, for surgery, to encourage a proper mechanical assessment and conservative trial first, while respecting that genuine indications exist.

## How to Apply
1. **For stretching:** ask whether the relief is temporary and whether the movement re-provokes the mechanism. Redirect flexion-intolerant backs away from forward stretching; keep hip/thoracic mobility.
2. **For passive modalities:** distinguish temporary symptom relief from a cure. Ask what actually changes the loading.
3. **For surgery:** encourage a proper mechanical assessment and a real conservative trial first; note that disc findings are common in pain-free people — but respect genuine neurological/red-flag indications.
4. **For posture rules:** replace "hold one perfect posture" with variety and interruption.
5. **Always return to mechanism.** Give the user the questions that reveal whether a proposed path addresses cause or merely symptom. Don't make the medical decision for them.

## Examples
**Situation:** A user has been told by a clinician to stretch their sore back daily and it isn't helping.
**Application:** If the back is flexion-intolerant, the stretch is re-provoking it. Redirect to trigger-removal and stability; keep mobility at hips and thoracic spine.
**Result:** The re-injury cycle stops; pain settles.

**Situation:** A user is offered surgery after an MRI shows a disc bulge.
**Application:** Explain that disc findings are common in pain-free people and that a mechanical assessment and proper conservative trial should usually come first — while stressing that genuine progressive neurological signs do warrant surgery. Encourage them to ask their surgeon how the finding was linked to their specific pain.
**Result:** A more informed decision; conservative care tried properly where appropriate.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss physiotherapy or surgery wholesale.
**Why:** The critique is of specific practices. Many clinicians do excellent mechanism-based work, and real surgical indications exist.

**Don't:** Tell the user what medical decision to make.
**Why:** Give them the mechanism-revealing questions; the decision is theirs and their clinician's.

**Don't:** Treat an MRI finding as proof of the pain source.
**Why:** Disc bulges are common in pain-free people and correlate poorly with pain. Establish the mechanism clinically.
