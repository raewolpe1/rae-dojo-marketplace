# Building the Stability-Mobility Pyramid

## When to Use
- When structuring a full training or rehabilitation progression.
- When explaining why foundations must precede strength and power.
- When auditing a programme that jumps straight to heavy or explosive work.

## Fails When
- **Stages are collapsed or reordered.** The order is the point; skipping it builds on sand.
- **The pain mechanism is unaddressed.** The pyramid sits on top of trigger-removal, not instead of it.
- **Red flags are present.** Refer.

## Core Concept
Capacity should be built in a fixed order, like a pyramid: each level rests on the one below it, and a level constructed before its foundation is built on sand. This is the organising principle behind both McGill's rehabilitation and his performance training, and the most common cause of recurrent injury is violating it — jumping to strength, speed, or sport-specific work before the base is laid. The pyramid converts a vague "get fit" or "get better" goal into a disciplined sequence with the right thing done at the right time.

The base of the pyramid is *pain-free, well-grooved movement patterns*. Before anything is loaded or accelerated, the fundamental patterns — the hip hinge, the squat, pushing, pulling, carrying, and simply moving the spine through daily tasks — must be performed without provoking pain and with sound mechanics, above all a neutral spine maintained under control. If the patterns are faulty, every load and every repetition added on top merely reinforces and amplifies the fault. So the patterns come first, and in a person with pain, this stage is preceded by removing the provoking trigger (the subtractive first step of the McGill Method).

The next level is *whole-body stability and endurance*. Here the muscular corset is trained to stiffen and hold the spine neutral, and to do so for the durations daily life and sport demand — which is an endurance quality, not a maximal one. The Big Three are the core of this level. The aim is a spine that can be held stable and controllable under sustained, varied demand. Only a spine that is controllable should proceed to be heavily loaded.

The next level is *strength*. With sound patterns and a stable, enduring base in place, the body can now be loaded progressively — carries, loaded hinges, pushing and pulling under resistance — to build force-producing capacity. Strength built on top of stability is safe and useful; strength built before stability loads an uncontrolled spine and invites injury.

Above strength sit *speed, power, and agility* — the explosive, fast qualities. These impose high, rapid loads and demand that all the lower levels be solid, because there is no time to consciously correct mechanics at speed; the patterns and stability must be automatic. Power trained before its foundation is the classic route to the dramatic injury.

At the apex sit *sport- or task-specific demands* — the particular movements, loads, and contexts of the person's actual activity, whether that is a sport, an occupation, or a hobby. These are layered on last, once everything beneath supports them.

Two principles govern the whole structure. First, *order is not optional*: you may move up only as each level becomes solid, and you may need to return to a lower level if pain or instability reappears. Second, the pyramid is *individualised* — its dosage, its mobility/stability emphasis, and its pace depend on the person's type, anatomy, mechanism, and goals. For most people, the great error is impatience: the desire to feel strong or explosive quickly, which pulls them up the pyramid before the base supports them. The discipline McGill teaches is to respect the order, build the unglamorous base properly, and let the top of the pyramid be earned. A foundation laid well carries everything above it; a foundation skipped collapses under the first real load.

## How to Apply
1. **Lay the base: pain-free movement patterns.** Groove the hinge, squat, push, pull, carry with a neutral spine before loading. Remove pain triggers first.
2. **Build stability and endurance.** The Big Three and whole-body stabilising work, trained for endurance, until the spine is controllable under sustained demand.
3. **Add strength.** Progressive loading — carries, loaded hinges, pushes, pulls — only on top of stability.
4. **Then speed, power, agility.** Explosive work once patterns and stability are automatic.
5. **Apex: task/sport-specific demands.** Layer the real-world specifics last. Move up only as each level becomes solid; drop back if pain returns. Individualise throughout.

## Examples
**Situation:** A returning exerciser starts with heavy deadlifts and explosive kettlebell swings and flares their back.
**Application:** They began at the top of the pyramid. Rebuild from the base — pattern grooving and stability — then progress to strength and only later to explosive work.
**Result:** A solid base that supports the heavy and explosive work without flaring.

**Situation:** A weekend athlete wants to sprint and jump in their sport but has poor trunk control.
**Application:** Speed and power require an automatic stable base. Build stability and endurance first, then strength, before adding the explosive sport demands.
**Result:** The explosive qualities are built on a foundation that can absorb them.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Skip to strength or power.
**Why:** Loading and accelerating an uncontrolled spine is the classic recurrent-injury pattern. Build the base first.

**Don't:** Reinforce a faulty pattern with load.
**Why:** Load amplifies whatever pattern it sits on. Groove the pattern before loading it.

**Don't:** Treat the pyramid as one-size-fits-all.
**Why:** Dosage, emphasis, and pace must match the individual's type, anatomy, mechanism, and goals.
