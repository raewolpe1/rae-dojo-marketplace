# The Big Three (Modified Curl-Up, Side Plank, Bird-Dog)

## When to Use
- When the user wants core or back exercises and you need to give them the safe foundation.
- When replacing harmful "core training" (sit-ups, loaded crunches) with spine-sparing alternatives.
- When building the stabilising base before any strength or sport-specific training.
- When someone with a recovered or recovering back needs a daily maintenance routine.

## Fails When
- **The pain mechanism hasn't been established.** The Big Three are safe for most, but if a specific movement provokes a given person, that must be screened first.
- **The spine moves during the exercise.** The entire point is a still, neutral spine. A curl-up that flexes the lumbar spine has become the very exercise it replaces.
- **They are trained for maximal effort.** These are endurance exercises. Grinding out a one-rep-max plank misses the mechanism.

## Core Concept
The Big Three are three exercises selected, after years of measuring spine load in the laboratory, to build the muscular stiffness that stabilises the lumbar spine while imposing the lowest possible compressive and shear penalty on the discs and joints. They are not chosen for how hard they feel or how they look. They are chosen because the ratio of stabilising benefit to spinal cost is exceptional. Together they train the full muscular corset — the front, the sides, and the back of the trunk — so that the spine can be held rigid and unbuckling under the demands of daily life.

**The modified curl-up** trains the rectus abdominis and the abdominal wall without flexing the lumbar spine. The trick is the hands placed under the small of the back to preserve the natural inward curve — you do not flatten the back into the floor, because flattening is flexion. One knee is bent and one leg straight to lock the pelvis. The head and shoulders rise only a centimetre or two, the whole upper torso moving as a rigid block. The spine itself does not move. This is the deliberate opposite of a sit-up, which repeatedly flexes the loaded spine.

**The side plank** (side bridge) trains quadratus lumborum and the obliques — the lateral guy-wires that stop the spine shearing sideways — at a remarkably low level of spinal compression compared with the stability it builds. Supported on one forearm with the body in a straight line and hips lifted, it loads the lateral system while the spine stays neutral. It can be regressed to the knees and progressed by stacking the feet or adding movement.

**The bird-dog** trains the back extensors and the deep multifidus through a whole-body anti-rotation, anti-extension pattern. On hands and knees, the opposite arm and leg extend while the spine is held dead neutral and the hips and shoulders stay square — no sag, no twist, no hip drop. This grooves the coordinated stiffening of the posterior chain in a functional cross-body pattern.

The dosage matters as much as the exercises. The stability that protects the spine through a long day is an *endurance* quality, not a maximal-strength one. So the Big Three are trained with short holds — roughly ten seconds — repeated in a descending pyramid (for example six holds, then four, then two), rather than long single holds or heavy load. A modest abdominal brace is maintained throughout. They can be done daily, and they make an excellent morning routine — though not first thing on waking, when the discs are fully hydrated and most vulnerable to bending; give it an hour after rising. The Big Three are the floor of the house, not the whole house — but no other floor I have measured is as sound.

## How to Apply
1. **Modified curl-up:** Lie supine, one knee bent, one leg straight. Hands under the lumbar curve — preserve it, don't flatten it. Lift head and shoulders a centimetre or two as a rigid block. Hold ~10s. The spine does not move.
2. **Side plank:** On the forearm, body straight, hips lifted, neutral spine. Hold ~10s each side. Regress to knees; progress by stacking feet.
3. **Bird-dog:** On hands and knees, extend opposite arm and leg, spine neutral, hips and shoulders square. No sag or twist. Hold ~10s, switch.
4. **Dose for endurance:** Descending reps (e.g. 6/4/2 holds of ~10s), not long maximal holds. Maintain a modest brace.
5. **Timing:** Daily is fine. Avoid first thing on waking; the discs are most flexion-vulnerable then.

## Examples
**Situation:** A 50-year-old has been doing 100 sit-ups a day for "core strength" and has a grumbling lower back.
**Application:** The sit-ups are repeatedly flexing a loaded spine — the disc-injury mechanism. Stop them. Replace with the Big Three for endurance. Within weeks the trunk is more stable and the grinding flexion is gone.
**Result:** Equal or better trunk stability, with the daily flexion load removed.

**Situation:** A recovering back-pain patient asks what to do to prevent recurrence.
**Application:** The Big Three, daily, trained for endurance, as the permanent maintenance base — built on top of corrected sitting and lifting habits.
**Result:** A durable stabilising habit that costs ten minutes and spares the spine.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Flatten the lower back into the floor during the curl-up.
**Why:** Flattening is lumbar flexion — exactly what the exercise exists to avoid.

**Don't:** Chase long maximal holds or add heavy load.
**Why:** Spinal stability is an endurance quality. Maximal effort raises spinal load without improving the protective capacity you actually need.

**Don't:** Let the hips sag or rotate in the bird-dog.
**Why:** The benefit is in holding the spine and pelvis perfectly still. Movement there defeats the purpose and can provoke pain.
