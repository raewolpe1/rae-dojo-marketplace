# Addiction as Attempted Solution

## When to Use
- When someone is struggling with addiction (substance or behavioural) and needs understanding rather than judgment.
- When someone wants to understand why addiction has such a grip despite the person's desire to stop.
- When the standard "addiction is a brain disease" or "addiction is a choice" framings feel inadequate.

## Core Concept
Maté's central thesis on addiction is both simple and radical: addiction is not a disease and not a choice. It is an attempted solution to the problem of unbearable pain. Every addicted person — whether their substance is heroin, alcohol, food, gambling, sex, work, or their smartphone — is trying to solve a problem that they have no other means of solving. The addiction provides something essential: relief from pain, a temporary sense of comfort, a momentary experience of aliveness, a brief respite from an inner world that is otherwise intolerable.

The implication is that treating addiction by removing the substance — through detox, prohibition, or willpower — without addressing the underlying pain is like treating pneumonia by suppressing the cough. The cough is not the disease. It is the organism's attempt to deal with the disease. Remove the cough without treating the infection and the person gets sicker.

Maté's clinical experience in Vancouver's Downtown Eastside — working with some of the most severely addicted populations in North America — confirmed what his reading of the research literature suggested: virtually every person with severe addiction had a history of childhood trauma. Not merely difficult childhoods, but childhoods marked by abuse, neglect, abandonment, or the emotional absence of attuned caregivers. The addiction began as self-medication — the child, and later the adult, found a substance or behaviour that temporarily filled the void, soothed the pain, or provided the sense of connection that their development had failed to provide.

This framework does not excuse the damage that addiction causes. It does explain it. And critically, it redirects intervention from punishment to healing — from the question "How do we stop this behaviour?" to the question "How do we address this pain?"

## How to Apply
1. **Ask "Why the pain?" before "Why the addiction?"** When someone presents with addiction, explore what the substance or behaviour does for them. What feeling does it produce? What feeling does it alleviate? The answers point toward the underlying wound.
2. **Recognise that behavioural addictions follow the same logic.** Workaholism, compulsive exercise, internet use, shopping, pornography — these are not lesser addictions. They serve the same function: relief from inner distress through external stimulation or numbing.
3. **Do not conflate understanding with permissiveness.** Understanding why someone is addicted does not mean endorsing the addiction or its consequences. It means addressing the cause rather than only the symptom.
4. **Support harm reduction.** If the addiction is the person's best available solution to their pain, removing it without providing an alternative is cruel and ineffective. Meet people where they are, reduce harm, build trust, and gradually offer better solutions.

## Examples
**Situation:** A woman in her fifties has been a functioning alcoholic for twenty years. She drinks daily, moderately by her assessment, but cannot stop. She has tried willpower, moderation programmes, and a brief period of AA. Nothing sticks.
**Application:** Maté would not ask why she drinks. He would ask what the drinking does. What state does the first glass create? What feeling does it relieve? Almost certainly, the alcohol is managing an internal state — anxiety, loneliness, a persistent sense of not being enough, the emotional residue of a childhood in which her needs were secondary to someone else's. The drinking is her most reliable, most available form of self-care. It has been her companion for two decades because it works — temporarily, destructively, but reliably. Recovery does not begin with removing the alcohol. It begins with understanding the pain and developing genuine alternatives: therapeutic relationship, body awareness, the recovery of the authenticity she sacrificed to maintain attachment in childhood.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Tell an addicted person they are weak, broken, or choosing to be sick.
**Why:** Shame is the accelerant of addiction. Every dose of judgment adds to the pain the person is already medicating.

**Don't:** Assume that understanding the roots of addiction means the person should not also pursue practical recovery tools (12-step, medication-assisted treatment, harm reduction).
**Why:** Maté is not anti-treatment. He is anti-treatment-without-understanding. The practical tools and the compassionate understanding work together.
