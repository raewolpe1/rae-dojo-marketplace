# Maté Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When someone has absorbed the conceptual framework and is asking: what do I actually do with this?
- When someone wants daily practices that support the recovery of authenticity, the regulation of the nervous system, and the healing of the mind-body connection.
- When the gap between understanding and lived change needs to be bridged.

## Core Concept
Maté's framework, if it remains purely intellectual, reproduces the very dissociation it critiques — understanding in the head what has not been felt in the body. The translation from concept to practice requires embodied, daily engagement with the principles he teaches. This is not a programme or a protocol. It is a way of living that gradually rewires the patterns established in childhood.

The practices cluster around several principles. First, **self-awareness** — the ongoing practice of noticing what you feel, what your body is telling you, where you are suppressing, and where you are performing. This is the personal application of compassionate inquiry, directed inward. Second, **boundary practice** — the daily, often uncomfortable work of saying no when you mean no, stating needs when they arise, and tolerating the anxiety that follows. Third, **body reconnection** — regular practices that bring attention back to the body: movement, breath work, somatic awareness, or simply the discipline of asking "What am I feeling in my body right now?" several times a day. Fourth, **relational authenticity** — bringing increasing honesty to relationships, starting with the safest ones and gradually expanding. Fifth, **the refusal of numbing** — noticing the moments when you reach for a substance, a screen, or a distraction, and sitting with the feeling underneath instead.

These practices are not dramatic. They do not require retreats or breakthroughs. They require consistency and the willingness to be uncomfortable — because the authentic self, having been suppressed for decades, does not return without resistance.

## How to Apply
1. **Morning check-in.** Before the day's demands take over, spend a few minutes in stillness. Ask: What am I feeling? What does my body need? What am I avoiding? This is compassionate inquiry applied to the self, and it requires no training — only honesty.
2. **Boundary practice.** Identify one situation today where you would normally say yes against your true feeling and consider saying no — or at least pausing before automatically agreeing. Notice the anxiety that arises. Let it be there. It is the old attachment fear, not current reality.
3. **Body awareness throughout the day.** Set periodic reminders to check in with the body. Where is there tension? What is the breath doing? What emotion is being held in the shoulders, the jaw, the stomach? The body often knows what the mind has not yet acknowledged.
4. **Emotional journalling.** At the end of the day, write not about events but about feelings. What did I feel today that I did not express? What did I express that was not true? Where did I suppress to maintain connection? This is not self-criticism. It is self-knowledge.
5. **The craving pause.** When you notice a craving — for food, alcohol, your phone, shopping, work — pause before acting on it. Ask: what am I really needing right now? What feeling is this craving trying to manage? You do not have to resist the craving. You simply need to understand it before acting.
6. **Relational honesty.** In one conversation today, say something genuinely true that you would normally edit for social safety. Not a confession or a confrontation — a simple act of being real. "I'm actually struggling today." "I don't agree with that." "I need some time alone." Small truths build the capacity for larger ones.
7. **Regular therapeutic relationship.** Maté's framework is relational. The practices listed above are supported by, not substitutes for, an ongoing therapeutic relationship — whether with a therapist, a group, or a trusted other who can witness and hold the emerging authentic self.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man in his fifties — a consultant, a helper, a chronic overworker — has read Maté, understands the attachment-authenticity framework intellectually, and recognises himself in every description. But nothing has changed. He still says yes to everything, still works evenings, still numbs with wine and television.
**Application:** The gap between understanding and change is the gap between the mind and the body. He understands with his intellect what he has not yet experienced in his nervous system. The practice is not more reading or more understanding. It is embodied action — starting with the smallest possible acts. Tomorrow, when someone asks him to take on an additional project, he pauses. He notices the automatic yes forming. He notices the anxiety that arises when he considers saying no. He sits with that anxiety for thirty seconds. He says: "Let me think about it and get back to you." That is the entire practice for that day. Over time, the pauses become longer, the nos become easier, and the nervous system gradually learns that authenticity does not produce the abandonment it has feared for five decades. The key is that he does not do this alone. A therapist, a group, a partner who understands — someone who can witness his emerging self and not require the performance — is essential.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Turn the practices into another form of self-improvement perfectionism.
**Why:** The adapted self is excellent at co-opting healing practices into new performance metrics. If the morning check-in becomes another item on the to-do list that produces guilt when missed, the practice has been captured by the very pattern it was meant to disrupt.

**Don't:** Expect the practices to work without relational support.
**Why:** The injury was relational. The healing must be relational. Daily practices support but do not replace the experience of being seen, accepted, and held by another person.
