# Attachment versus Authenticity

## When to Use
- When someone recognises they have been living a version of themselves designed to maintain connection with others at the expense of their own truth.
- When the people-pleasing, self-suppression, or chronic accommodation is producing symptoms — depression, resentment, illness, burnout.
- When someone asks why they cannot seem to express their genuine needs or feelings.

## Core Concept
Maté identifies two fundamental human needs: attachment (the need for connection, proximity, and belonging) and authenticity (the need to be true to one's own feelings, needs, and nature). In healthy development, these two needs are integrated — the child can be authentically themselves and maintain secure attachment with their caregivers. But when the caregiving environment is stressed, unavailable, or conditional, the child faces an impossible choice: be real and risk losing connection, or suppress the real self and maintain attachment.

The child will always choose attachment, because attachment is survival. An infant who loses its caregiver's bond does not merely feel sad — it faces literal death. So the child learns, with exquisite sensitivity, which aspects of selfhood are welcome and which are dangerous. Anger is dangerous if the parent withdraws from angry children. Need is dangerous if the parent is overwhelmed. Exuberance is dangerous if the parent requires quiet compliance. Each suppression is a small death of authenticity in service of attachment.

Over years, these suppressions accumulate into a constructed self — what Winnicott called the false self — that is organised around maintaining connection rather than expressing truth. The adapted adult is pleasant, accommodating, reliable, selfless, and emotionally unavailable to their own inner life. They are also, frequently, chronically ill, depressed, addicted, or exhausted — because the suppressed authentic self does not disappear. It goes underground and expresses itself through the body, through symptoms, through compulsions, and through a persistent, usually unnamed sense that something essential is missing.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify your adaptations.** What did you learn to suppress in childhood? Anger? Need? Sadness? Exuberance? Sexuality? Ambition? These suppressions are the map of your lost authenticity.
2. **Notice where attachment anxiety overrides authenticity now.** Where do you say yes when you mean no? Where do you silence yourself to avoid conflict? Where do you perform a version of yourself that is not true?
3. **Understand that recovery of authenticity feels dangerous.** The nervous system learned that authenticity threatens attachment. Expressing real feelings or real needs will initially activate attachment anxiety — the old fear that being real means being abandoned.
4. **Begin with small acts of authenticity.** State a genuine preference. Express a real feeling. Set a boundary. Tolerate the anxiety that follows. Each small act rewires the system, teaching it that authenticity need not mean abandonment.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man in his fifties — a consultant who is admired for his selflessness, his willingness to take on any project, his inability to say no — is exhausted, resentful, and suffering from chronic back pain. He cannot understand why, since he is doing everything right.
**Application:** He is doing everything right by the standards of the adapted self — the version of him that learned in childhood that his worth depended on his usefulness to others. His mother was overwhelmed; he became her helper. His father was critical; he became the achiever who never complained. The back pain is his body's rebellion — the weight of decades of carrying what was never his to carry. The resentment is the authentic self protesting its imprisonment. Recovery does not mean becoming selfish. It means learning that his needs are legitimate, that saying no is not abandonment, and that the people who genuinely care about him will not disappear when he stops performing.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Suggest that recovering authenticity means abandoning all social obligation.
**Why:** Authenticity is not narcissism. It is the capacity to be real while remaining in relationship. The goal is integration, not isolation.

**Don't:** Rush the process.
**Why:** The adaptations were built over decades and are wired into the nervous system. Recovering authenticity is a gradual process that must respect the pace of the person's readiness.
