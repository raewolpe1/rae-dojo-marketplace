# Compassionate Inquiry

## When to Use
- When someone wants a method for exploring the roots of their patterns — addiction, illness, relational difficulty — without shame or judgment.
- When a helper, therapist, or coach is looking for a trauma-informed approach to deep questioning.
- When someone is ready to move from "What's wrong with me?" to "What happened to me?"

## Core Concept
Compassionate inquiry is Maté's therapeutic method, developed over decades of clinical work and formalised as a trainable approach. It rests on a single shift: from diagnosis to understanding, from "What's wrong with you?" to "What happened to you?" — and, crucially, "What did you have to do to survive what happened to you?"

The method has three interlocking elements. First, compassion — the practitioner approaches the person's experience without judgment, understanding that every symptom, every defence, every destructive pattern began as the organism's best available response to an impossible situation. Second, inquiry — the practitioner asks questions that help the person see what they have been unable or unwilling to see, not by confrontation but by gentle, persistent curiosity. Third, presence — the practitioner remains emotionally present with whatever arises, modelling the attuned attention that was absent in the person's development.

The questions in compassionate inquiry are not formulaic. They arise from genuine curiosity about the person's inner experience. But they tend to follow a pattern: What are you feeling right now? What does that feeling remind you of? When did you first learn to respond this way? What did this pattern protect you from? What did it cost you? The questions move from the present experience backward toward the developmental origin, not to assign blame but to create understanding.

The method is distinguished from purely cognitive approaches by its emphasis on the body. Maté asks not only "What are you thinking?" but "Where do you feel that in your body?" The body holds information that the cognitive mind has suppressed or rationalised. Accessing that information through somatic awareness often produces breakthroughs that intellectual analysis cannot.

Compassionate inquiry is not a technique to be mechanically applied. It is a stance — a way of being with another person that communicates: I see you, I am curious about you, I do not judge you, and I believe that understanding your experience is the foundation of your healing.

## How to Apply
1. **Begin with presence, not questions.** Before asking anything, establish genuine contact. The person needs to feel seen and safe before they can explore what they have spent a lifetime avoiding.
2. **Follow the feeling, not the narrative.** When someone tells their story, notice where emotion appears — or where it conspicuously does not. The flat affect when describing childhood, the sudden shift to intellectualisation, the laugh that covers grief. These are the doorways.
3. **Ask body-based questions.** "Where do you feel that?" "What happens in your chest when you say that?" "If that tension could speak, what would it say?" The body often knows what the mind has censored.
4. **Move from present to past with curiosity, not assumption.** "When did you first learn that your anger was dangerous?" is different from "Your mother made you suppress your anger." The first invites exploration; the second imposes a conclusion.
5. **Respect defences.** If someone is not ready to go somewhere, do not push. The defence is there for a reason. Compassionate inquiry trusts the person's pace.

## Examples
**Situation:** A therapist is working with a client who intellectualises everything — brilliant analysis of his patterns, zero emotional contact with them. He can explain exactly why he avoids intimacy but the explanation has not changed anything.
**Application:** Maté would note that the intellectualisation is itself the pattern worth inquiring into. Rather than accepting the analysis, the practitioner might say: "You understand this very clearly. And yet nothing changes. What do you notice in your body right now as you tell me this story?" The question redirects attention from the mind — where the client is safe and controlled — to the body, where the suppressed experience lives. If the client reports nothing, that absence is itself significant. "You feel nothing. When did you learn that feeling nothing was the safest option?" The inquiry follows the trail of suppression back toward its origin — not to produce a dramatic revelation, but to begin the slow process of reconnecting the person with the emotional life they abandoned in childhood.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use compassionate inquiry as a technique divorced from genuine compassion.
**Why:** The method is inseparable from the stance. Questions asked without genuine curiosity and care become interrogation, which reproduces the very dynamic — being examined without being seen — that contributed to the person's injury.

**Don't:** Push past resistance in the name of healing.
**Why:** Resistance is the organism's protection. It has earned the right to be there. Compassionate inquiry works with resistance, not against it — understanding what the defence protects rather than demanding its removal.
