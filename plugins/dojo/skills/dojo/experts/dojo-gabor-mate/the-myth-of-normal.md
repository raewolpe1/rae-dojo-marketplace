# The Myth of Normal

## When to Use
- When someone has been diagnosed and pathologised for what may be a normal response to abnormal conditions.
- When the cultural context of someone's suffering needs to be named.
- When someone is ready to question the assumptions of their society about health, illness, and normality.

## Core Concept
Maté's most expansive argument, developed fully in *The Myth of Normal*, is that what Western culture considers normal — the way we live, work, parent, relate, consume, and cope — is itself pathological. The rates of chronic illness, addiction, mental health conditions, autoimmune disease, and childhood developmental disorders are not aberrations within an otherwise healthy system. They are the predictable products of a system that systematically violates human developmental needs.

The culture separates mothers from infants (through economic necessity, not choice). It prioritises productivity over relationship. It medicalises normal human distress — grief becomes "major depressive disorder," childhood energy becomes ADHD, justified rage becomes "anger management issues." It treats the body as a machine separate from the mind. It substitutes consumption for connection, medication for meaning, and diagnosis for understanding.

Maté is not arguing that individual illness is an illusion. He is arguing that individual illness exists within a context that produces it — and that treating individuals without examining the context is like mopping the floor while the tap is still running.

The implications are radical. If "normal" is itself pathological, then being well-adjusted to this culture is not evidence of health. The person who is productive, pleasant, uncomplaining, and medicated may be more damaged — and certainly more compliant — than the person whose symptoms are disrupting the smooth functioning of the machine.

## How to Apply
1. **Question the diagnosis.** Not dismissively, but critically. When someone is diagnosed with a disorder, ask: is this a disorder within the person, or is it a normal response to a disordered environment?
2. **Name the cultural context.** Depression in a person working eighty hours a week in a job that violates their values is not a chemical imbalance. It is an accurate response to an intolerable situation.
3. **Resist the individualisation of suffering.** When someone says "What's wrong with me?" consider the possibility that the answer is: nothing — but there may be something wrong with the conditions you are living in.
4. **Hold both individual and cultural responsibility.** The culture is toxic. You still have to heal. Both are true. The cultural critique does not excuse individual passivity, and individual healing does not excuse cultural acquiescence.

## Examples
**Situation:** A teacher is exhausted, depressed, and on antidepressants. She is told she has a chemical imbalance. She works twelve-hour days, carries sixty students' emotional needs, receives minimal institutional support, and has not had a full weekend off in two months.
**Application:** Maté would not begin by questioning the medication — it may be keeping her functional — but he would challenge the framing. Her depression is not a chemical imbalance that appeared from nowhere. It is the predictable result of chronic stress, emotional depletion, and a system that treats educators as expendable. The "treatment" that confines itself to adjusting her serotonin while leaving the conditions unchanged is, in Maté's terms, normal — and that is exactly the problem. Genuine treatment would include institutional change (workload, support, recognition), personal boundary-setting, and the recovery of activities and relationships that replenish what the work depletes.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use the cultural critique to dismiss individual therapeutic work.
**Why:** The culture is real. Your pain is also real. Changing the culture is necessary. Healing yourself is also necessary. They are parallel, not sequential.

**Don't:** Turn this into nihilism — "Everything is broken, so nothing can be done."
**Why:** Maté is a healer, not a cynic. His cultural critique serves understanding, which serves change — both personal and collective.
