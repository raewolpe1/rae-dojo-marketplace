# Preacher, Prosecutor, Politician vs Scientist

## When to Use
- When someone is defending a sacred belief, attacking another's, or telling the audience what it wants to hear.
- When a discussion is producing heat and entrenchment instead of learning.
- When the user wants a clean diagnostic for *how* they're thinking.
- When you need to move a person or team from winning the argument to getting it right.

## Fails When
- **The matter is a settled moral value**, not an open empirical question.
- **The user needs to advocate forcefully** for a well-tested position — persuasion has its place.
- **Speed matters more** than another round of examination.

## Core Concept
At the heart of *Think Again* is a typology of the mental modes we slip into when we reason. Grant borrows from psychologist Philip Tetlock the idea that we default into three professional mindsets, none of which actually learns:

- **Preacher mode.** When our sacred beliefs are in jeopardy, we deliver sermons to protect and promote them. We're not examining the belief; we're defending the cathedral.
- **Prosecutor mode.** When we spot a flaw in someone else's reasoning, we build a case to prove them wrong and win the argument. The goal is victory, not truth.
- **Politician mode.** When we want the approval of an audience, we campaign and lobby, telling people what they want to hear. We tell ourselves we're being responsive; we're really seeking applause.

All three *feel* like thinking. All three have the same fatal feature: the conclusion is fixed in advance, and the cognitive work goes into justifying it. Grant's point is that we shift among these modes constantly, often without noticing, and that they are the enemy of rethinking.

The fourth mode is the one that learns: the **scientist**. Thinking like a scientist means treating your beliefs as hypotheses to be tested, actively searching for reasons you might be wrong, running experiments, and updating in response to evidence. Crucially, the scientist mindset is an attitude available to anyone — you don't need a lab. "We move into scientist mode when we're searching for the truth: we run experiments to test hypotheses and discover knowledge."

The evidence that this matters is striking. Grant cites a study of Italian entrepreneurs who were randomly assigned to a training program that taught them to think like scientists — to treat their business strategy as a theory, their assumptions as hypotheses, and customer interactions as experiments. Compared with a control group, the scientist-trained entrepreneurs earned significantly more revenue, were quicker to pivot when their original idea wasn't working, and were less likely to persist with a failing strategy out of ego. Thinking like a scientist made them more flexible and more successful, not more hesitant.

Grant's own most-told story illustrates the prosecutor trap: he passed on investing in Warby Parker because he went into prosecutor mode, building the case against the founders (they were keeping their day jobs, they hadn't quit to go all-in) rather than into scientist mode, asking what evidence would tell him whether they'd succeed. He had the right information and the wrong mindset.

The practical work is twofold. First, **catch yourself in the mode** — notice when you're preaching, prosecuting, or politicking, because the tell is that you already know the answer you want. Second, **deliberately switch to scientist mode** by asking scientist questions: What's my hypothesis here? What evidence would change my mind? What's the cheapest experiment that would test this? You can also pull *others* out of preacher/prosecutor mode — not by counter-preaching (which entrenches them) but by asking genuine questions and inviting them to reason through their own view, which often surfaces its weaknesses without a fight.

For strategy and evaluation, the move is to hold the strategy as a theory and the programme as an experiment — exactly the entrepreneurs' lesson — rather than as a position to defend (preacher), a rival to demolish (prosecutor), or a pitch to win the donor (politician).

## How to Apply
1. **Name the mode.** Ask: am I preaching, prosecuting, or politicking? The tell is a conclusion fixed in advance.
2. **Switch to scientist questions.** "What's my hypothesis? What would change my mind? What's the cheapest test?"
3. **Hold strategy as theory.** Treat plans and programmes as experiments to run, not positions to win.
4. **Don't counter-preach.** To move someone out of a defensive mode, ask real questions rather than firing back sermons.
5. **Reward the pivot.** Treat changing course in response to evidence as success, the way the scientist-trained entrepreneurs did.

## Examples
**Situation:** Two senior staff are locked in an escalating argument about which programme model is "right."
**Application:** Both are in prosecutor mode — building cases to win. Reframe: "What's the hypothesis each model rests on, and what's the cheapest experiment that would tell us which holds here?" Convert the fight into a test.
**Result:** The energy shifts from winning to finding out, and the question becomes answerable.

**Situation:** A consultant notices she's assembling every argument for the recommendation she already favors.
**Application:** That's preacher mode. She asks the scientist question — "what evidence would make me drop this recommendation?" — and goes looking for it.
**Result:** The recommendation either survives a real test or improves before it reaches the client.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Mistake forceful defense or rebuttal for thinking.
**Why:** Preaching, prosecuting, and politicking all fix the conclusion in advance.

**Don't:** Try to move a preacher by preaching back.
**Why:** Counter-sermons entrench; genuine questions open.

**Don't:** Treat a strategy pivot as failure.
**Why:** Updating on evidence is the scientist's success, and it correlates with better outcomes.
