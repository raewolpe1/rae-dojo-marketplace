# Feedback as Care

## When to Use
- When the user must deliver hard feedback and wants it acted on, not resented.
- When critical feedback keeps being ignored or triggers defensiveness.
- When building a relationship in which honest critique is received as help.
- When coaching someone to develop, where feedback is the lever.

## Fails When
- **There's no genuine basis for "I believe in you."** The message must be true to work.
- **The problem is the content of the feedback**, not its framing.
- **What's actually needed is recognition**, not criticism.

## Core Concept
Grant reframes feedback from an act of judgment into an act of care — and shows that the framing is not soft sentiment but a mechanism that determines whether feedback gets *used*. The anchor is one of the most elegant findings in his repertoire, often called the **"wise feedback" study** (Cohen, Steele, Yeager and colleagues). Students received critical comments on their written work. For one randomly assigned group, a single sentence was appended: *"I'm giving you these comments because I have very high expectations and I know that you can reach them."* That one sentence dramatically increased the proportion of students who revised and improved their work — and the effect was especially large for students from groups who had reason to wonder whether criticism was biased against them. Identical criticism; different relationship; transformed response.

The mechanism is **trust**. People can absorb a great deal of hard truth if they trust that the person delivering it (a) has high standards and (b) genuinely believes they can meet them. Criticism without that signal is read as an attack or a verdict, and the natural response is to defend, dismiss, or disengage. Criticism *with* that signal is read as an investment, and the natural response is to act on it. So the job of anyone giving feedback is to make the underlying care legible: *I'm telling you this because I think you can do better, and I want you to.*

Grant connects this to several other strands. From his challenge-network work: the most valuable critics are disagreeable givers, whose blunt feedback is trustworthy precisely because they want you to succeed — and whose value is unlocked when the recipient understands that motive. From his hidden-potential work: feedback is the medium through which character skills and potential get developed, so a culture poor at feedback caps everyone's growth. And from his advice-seeking work: framing a request as "advice" rather than "feedback" pulls more candid, constructive, forward-looking input — so the *receiver* of feedback can improve its quality by how they ask for it.

Grant also addresses the giving side directly. He cautions against the "feedback sandwich" (burying criticism between praise), which often muddies the message and leaves people unsure what to act on. Better is to be direct about the critique while being explicit about the care and the standard — transparency about both the high bar and the belief that it's reachable. He emphasizes separating the person from the work ("this draft has a problem" rather than "you're careless"), being specific and actionable, and orienting feedback toward the future (what to do next time) rather than only litigating the past.

A further point concerns *receiving* feedback well, which Grant treats as a skill of the rethinking-minded: detaching ego from work so criticism is information rather than threat, actively soliciting it (especially through advice-framed questions), and thanking people who deliver hard truths so the behavior continues. The person who is known to receive feedback gracefully gets more of it, and therefore improves faster.

For evaluators, managers, and consultants, the operational core is simple: pair the hard message with the genuine signal of high expectations and belief. Whether delivering a tough evaluation finding to a partner, coaching a junior colleague, or giving a client an unwelcome recommendation, the feedback lands and gets acted on when the recipient trusts you want them to succeed. "I'm telling you this because I think you can do better" is not a nicety — it's the difference between feedback that's defended against and feedback that's used.

## How to Apply
1. **Make the care legible.** Pair hard feedback with the genuine signal: high expectations *and* belief they can meet them.
2. **Be direct, not sandwiched.** Skip burying criticism in praise; state the critique clearly alongside the standard.
3. **Separate person from work.** Critique the draft, the decision, the output — not the person's character.
4. **Orient to the future.** Emphasize what to do next time over relitigating what went wrong.
5. **Make receiving a skill.** Solicit feedback via advice-framed questions, detach ego, and thank candid critics.

## Examples
**Situation:** A manager's careful written feedback on a junior analyst's reports keeps getting nodded at and ignored.
**Application:** Add the missing signal. Preface the critique with a sincere "I'm giving you this detailed feedback because I have high expectations and I know you can hit them," then keep the comments specific and forward-looking.
**Result:** The analyst, now trusting the intent, acts on the feedback instead of defending against it — the wise-feedback effect in practice.

**Situation:** An evaluator must deliver an unwelcome finding to a partner organization that tends to get defensive.
**Application:** Frame the hard finding within genuine care and confidence — "I'm raising this because I believe this programme can work and you can fix this" — and orient it toward concrete next steps rather than blame.
**Result:** The partner receives the finding as an investment in their success and engages with the fix.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Deliver criticism without signaling belief in the person.
**Why:** The wise-feedback research shows the belief signal is what makes feedback get used.

**Don't:** Bury the message in a feedback sandwich.
**Why:** It muddies the critique and leaves people unsure what to act on.

**Don't:** Attack the person instead of the work.
**Why:** Character attacks trigger defense; specific, future-oriented critique invites action.
