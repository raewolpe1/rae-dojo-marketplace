# Defaults and Status-Quo Bias

## When to Use
- When an organization keeps doing something simply because it has always done it.
- When the user wants to legitimately surface or engineer dissent.
- When an inherited default is going unexamined and may be the thing worth changing.
- When "that's how it's done here" is being treated as a reason.

## Fails When
- **The default is genuinely well-tested** and questioning it is just churn.
- **The user wants change for novelty's sake**, not because the status quo underperforms.
- **The real problem is execution**, not inherited inertia.

## Core Concept
A recurring theme across Grant's work is that the most powerful force in any organization is the unexamined default — the practice, policy, or assumption that persists not because anyone chose it on the merits but because no one ever questioned it. Status-quo bias is our tendency to prefer things as they are; combined with the human discomfort of dissent, it means that most of what an organization does goes unchallenged indefinitely. "The hallmark of originality is rejecting the default and exploring whether a better option exists."

Grant's framing in *Originals* is that originality begins with **vuja de** — the inverse of déjà vu. Déjà vu is encountering something new that feels familiar; vuja de is looking at something familiar with fresh eyes and suddenly seeing it as strange and questionable. The original asks, of the things everyone takes for granted: *why is it this way, and does it have to be?* Most people never reach that question because the default is invisible — it's just "how things are."

The reason defaults persist is not only inertia but the social cost of challenging them. Speaking up against an established practice is risky: it can mark you as a troublemaker, a poor team player, or naïve about "how things really work." So people stay silent, and silence is read as consent, which reinforces the default. Grant emphasizes that **dissent has to be actively engineered** because the natural equilibrium suppresses it. Leaders who want better ideas must make it safe — and rewarded — to question the inherited way.

A critical practical finding concerns *how* to build in dissent. Grant argues that the standard tool — appointing a **devil's advocate** — is surprisingly weak. When everyone knows someone has been *assigned* to argue the other side, the dissent is discounted as artificial role-play and changes few minds. What works far better is to **unearth authentic dissenters**: find the people who genuinely hold a minority view and give them a protected platform. Real dissent, from people who mean it, forces genuine reconsideration; staged dissent is theater. If no genuine dissenter exists, that itself is a warning sign that the group has stopped thinking.

Grant also points to the value of seeking out and *amplifying* the people who challenge defaults — and of leaders modeling it themselves by inviting criticism of their own decisions. He cites organizational practices like soliciting problems and complaints actively (rather than waiting for them to surface), running structured exercises that force the group to articulate what they'd change, and rewarding the act of questioning rather than only the act of executing. The aim is to convert the unexamined default into an explicit choice that has actually been tested against alternatives.

For development and evaluation work, defaults are everywhere and especially dangerous: the indicator set inherited from the last programme, the logframe template nobody questions, the "this is how donors want it" assumption, the standard methodology applied regardless of fit. Grant's vuja de is a direct invitation to ask, of each inherited practice: would we choose this if we were designing from scratch today, knowing what we now know? Often the most valuable improvement is not a new activity but the retirement of an unexamined one.

## How to Apply
1. **Practice vuja de.** Look at familiar, inherited practices as if for the first time and ask why they exist.
2. **Make the default an explicit choice.** Ask "would we choose this today, from scratch?" — if not, examine it.
3. **Engineer dissent.** Treat the absence of challenge as a problem to solve, not a sign of agreement.
4. **Unearth real dissenters, not token ones.** Find and protect people who genuinely hold the minority view; skip the assigned devil's advocate.
5. **Reward questioning.** Recognize the people who challenge defaults, not only those who execute them.

## Examples
**Situation:** A monitoring system carries forty indicators inherited from three programmes ago, and no one can say why most of them are tracked.
**Application:** Apply vuja de: if you were designing the system today, which of these would you choose? Make each indicator justify itself rather than survive by inertia.
**Result:** The system shrinks to the handful of indicators that actually inform decisions, freeing effort for what matters.

**Situation:** A leader wants better scrutiny of a major decision and plans to assign someone to "play devil's advocate."
**Application:** Grant's research says assigned advocacy is discounted. Instead, find the staff who genuinely have doubts about the decision and give them a protected platform to make their real case.
**Result:** Authentic dissent surfaces the decision's true weaknesses, which staged dissent would not have.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat an inherited practice as justified simply because it persists.
**Why:** Defaults survive by inertia and social cost, not by merit.

**Don't:** Rely on an assigned devil's advocate to test a decision.
**Why:** Staged dissent is discounted; genuine dissenters change minds.

**Don't:** Read the absence of objection as agreement.
**Why:** Dissent is suppressed by default and must be actively engineered.
