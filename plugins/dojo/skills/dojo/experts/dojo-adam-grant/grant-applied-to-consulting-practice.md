# Grant Applied to Consulting Practice

## When to Use
- When the user wants Grant's frameworks translated into M&E and development-consulting practice.
- When designing evaluation interviews, managing partnerships, or building learning processes.
- When the context is programme strategy and the user wants concrete, operational moves.
- When connecting several Grant ideas into one applied workflow.

## Fails When
- **The user wants the underlying concept**, not its application — load the source topic file instead.
- **The context is unrelated** to consulting or evaluation.
- **A specific technical method is required** that Grant's organizational-psychology toolkit doesn't supply.

## Core Concept
This file translates Grant's frameworks into the specific texture of Rae's work: monitoring and evaluation, and economic-development consulting in and beyond Cape Town. Grant is unusually operational for a social psychologist — his findings come with handles — and several map directly onto evaluation and partnership practice.

**Evaluation interviewing via advice-seeking.** Grant's finding that asking for *advice* (forward-looking) elicits more candor than asking for *feedback* (backward-looking) is a precision instrument for evaluators. Guarded stakeholders — officials, implementers, beneficiaries wary of saying the wrong thing — open up when asked "If you were advising the team on how to make this work better, what would you tell them?" rather than "Is the programme working?" The advice frame lowers defensiveness, positions the evaluator as a humble learner rather than a judge, and pulls specific, constructive insight. It also flatters the respondent as a source of wisdom, building the rapport that surfaces honest accounts.

**Reading partnerships through reciprocity.** Development work runs on consortia, donor relationships, and implementing partnerships, each with a giver/taker/matcher signature. The partner who only appears when it needs data, intros, or staff time is a taker; recognizing it early lets the consultant shift from open giving to generous tit-for-tat and protect their own capacity. The giver-with-boundaries posture — generous by default, self-protective enough to be sustainable — is the durable stance for a consultant whose reputation and network are the business.

**Challenge networks for honest review.** Evaluation is supposed to surface hard truths, but it's often embedded in performance cultures that reward green dashboards. A standing challenge network of trusted, candid peers — including the disagreeable givers — who critique a design or a finding before it ships protects the work from capture and groupthink far better than a friendly internal review. The same logic applies inside the consultant's own firm: build the circle that will tell you your framework is flawed before the client does.

**The scientist mindset for programme strategy.** Grant's preacher/prosecutor/politician-vs-scientist frame is tailor-made for theories of change. A theory of change is a set of hypotheses, not a creed; a programme is an experiment, not a position to defend. Holding strategy as testable theory — writing down what you expect, what evidence would disconfirm it, and what the cheapest test is — is exactly the discipline the Italian-entrepreneur study rewarded, and it guards against the preacher's defense of a beloved model and the politician's pitch to the donor.

**Feedback as care in client and partner relationships.** Delivering unwelcome evaluation findings is core to the job. The wise-feedback signal — "I'm raising this because I believe this programme can work and you can fix it" — is what turns a hard finding from something defended against into something acted on. Pairing candor with genuine confidence in the partner is the difference between a report that's shelved and one that's used.

**Cultures of rethinking in M&E systems.** Many evaluation functions sit inside performance cultures that punish honest accounts of failure, destroying the learning evaluation exists to produce. Grant's prescription — model fallibility from the top, reward honest learning over green dashboards, run blameless reviews — is a direct agenda for making an M&E system actually drive adaptation rather than just report compliance.

Two cautions keep this honest. First, Grant is a generalist organizational psychologist, not a measurement methodologist: route technical poverty measurement, causal inference, or participatory methodology elsewhere. Second, his frames are tools, not doctrine — apply the one the situation calls for rather than running the whole kit reflexively.

## How to Apply
1. **Interview by asking for advice.** Replace "is it working?" with "what would you advise the team to do differently?" to get candor.
2. **Map the reciprocity of every partnership.** Identify givers, takers, matchers; match the takers, sustain generosity with boundaries.
3. **Stand up a challenge network.** Route designs and findings through candid peers before they reach the client.
4. **Hold strategy as hypothesis.** Write the theory of change as testable claims with disconfirming evidence and a cheap test.
5. **Pair hard findings with belief.** Deliver unwelcome results inside genuine confidence that the partner can act on them.

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant is scoping interviews for a politically sensitive programme evaluation where respondents are guarded.
**Application:** Build the protocol around advice-framed questions ("what would you advise someone trying to improve this?") and position herself as a learner, not a judge — the candor-eliciting move from Grant's advice-seeking research.
**Result:** Respondents offer specific, honest, forward-looking insight they'd have withheld from evaluative questions.

**Situation:** A firm keeps defending its signature methodology to clients regardless of fit.
**Application:** That's preacher mode applied to a product. Reframe each engagement as a hypothesis test — what would tell us this method *isn't* right here? — and stand up an internal challenge network to pressure-test designs.
**Result:** The firm adapts its approach to context and catches mismatches before clients do.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Run evaluation interviews with backward-looking judging questions.
**Why:** Advice-framed questions elicit far more candor and constructive insight.

**Don't:** Give endlessly to a taker partner to keep the relationship warm.
**Why:** Generous tit-for-tat protects capacity; open giving to takers is slow exploitation.

**Don't:** Apply Grant's whole toolkit reflexively or to technical measurement questions.
**Why:** His frames are situational tools; route methodology questions to the right specialist.
