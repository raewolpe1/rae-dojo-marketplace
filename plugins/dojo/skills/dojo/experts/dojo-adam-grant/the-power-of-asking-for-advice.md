# The Power of Asking for Advice

## When to Use
- When the user wants to influence or persuade someone.
- When people need to open up — in evaluation interviews, stakeholder consultations, or partnerships.
- When building a relationship across a power or status gap.
- When the user wrongly thinks asking for help signals weakness.

## Fails When
- **The ask is transparently instrumental.** Manipulative advice-seeking backfires; it must be genuine.
- **The user needs to make a decision**, not gather more input.
- **Asking would abdicate** a judgment that is properly the user's to own.

## Core Concept
Grant calls asking for advice one of the most underused tools for influence and learning — and one that most people avoid because they wrongly believe it makes them look incompetent. The research says the opposite. Seeking advice tends to make you look *more* capable, not less: in studies Grant cites, people who asked for advice were rated as *more* competent than those who didn't, because asking good questions signals thoughtfulness, and the people asked feel flattered rather than superior.

Grant identifies several mechanisms that make advice-seeking quietly powerful. First, **it gets you better information** — the obvious benefit, often overlooked because people would rather appear self-sufficient. Second, **it builds relationships and likeability**: when someone gives you advice, they become invested in you and your success; the very act of helping increases their commitment (an echo of the Ben Franklin effect, where doing someone a favor makes you like them more). Third, and most counterintuitively, **it is persuasive** — when people help you think a problem through, they often talk themselves into supporting your direction, because they now have ownership of the reasoning. Asking for advice converts a potential adversary into a collaborator.

This connects to Grant's reciprocity work. Advice-seeking is a giver's tool, not a taker's manipulation — and the distinction matters enormously. Grant is careful to note that *strategically* asking for advice as a manipulation tactic tends to fail, because people sense insincerity. The power comes from *genuinely* wanting and using the input. Authentic advice-seeking flatters by treating the other person as a valued source of wisdom; fake advice-seeking insults by treating them as a mark.

A related Grant point concerns the difference between asking for **advice** and asking for **feedback**. When you ask for feedback, people tend to look backward and judge what you did, often softening criticism to be polite. When you ask for *advice*, people look forward and become more candid and constructive, offering specific suggestions rather than vague evaluations. So the framing of the question shapes the quality of what you get back: "How could I improve this?" or "What would you do differently next time?" elicits more useful input than "What did you think?"

Grant also ties advice-seeking to leadership and learning cultures. A leader who genuinely asks for advice — including from more junior people — models that not having all the answers is acceptable, which builds psychological safety and surfaces ideas that hierarchy would otherwise suppress. It is a way of distributing the cognitive work of the organization and of signaling that status doesn't require omniscience.

For evaluators and consultants, this is a precision instrument. The asking-for-advice technique is one of the most effective ways to get stakeholders — especially powerful or guarded ones — to open up in interviews. "What would you advise someone trying to make this programme work better?" invites candor and forward-looking insight in a way that "Is the programme working?" does not. It also positions the evaluator as a humble learner rather than a judge, lowering defensiveness. And in client relationships, asking the client for advice on framing or approach builds the buy-in that makes recommendations land.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask, don't just tell.** Open influence attempts by genuinely asking for the other person's advice.
2. **Mean it.** Authentic advice-seeking persuades; manipulative advice-seeking is sensed and backfires.
3. **Ask for advice, not feedback.** Forward-looking "what would you do?" elicits more candor than backward-looking "what did you think?"
4. **Use it to open people up.** In interviews, "what would you advise someone trying to improve this?" beats "is it working?"
5. **Model it as a leader.** Ask junior people for advice to build safety and surface suppressed ideas.

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluator can't get a guarded senior official to say anything candid about a struggling programme.
**Application:** Switch from judging questions to advice-seeking: "If you were advising the team on how to make this work better, what would you tell them?" The forward, advice frame invites candor and lowers defensiveness.
**Result:** The official offers specific, honest insight he'd never have given to "is it working?"

**Situation:** A consultant needs a skeptical client to buy into a new approach.
**Application:** Rather than pitching it, ask the client's advice on how to frame and sequence it. In helping shape it, the client becomes invested in its success.
**Result:** The client co-owns the approach and champions it instead of resisting it.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat asking for help as a sign of weakness.
**Why:** Advice-seekers are rated as more competent, not less, and gain influence.

**Don't:** Use advice-seeking as a manipulation tactic.
**Why:** Insincerity is sensed and the effect reverses; it must be genuine.

**Don't:** Default to asking for "feedback."
**Why:** "Advice" elicits more candid, forward-looking, useful input than "feedback."
