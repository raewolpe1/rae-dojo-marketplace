# Org Cultures of Rethinking

## When to Use
- When the user wants to build a learning organization or psychological safety.
- When a culture claims to value learning but punishes the people who surface problems.
- When teams need the capacity to revise course rather than defend decisions.
- When designing norms, incentives, and rituals — not just coaching one person.

## Fails When
- **The user wants to lower the bar** in the name of safety; that's not what this is.
- **The problem is one individual's mindset**, better handled with the rethinking-cycle file.
- **The organization already learns well** and the diagnosis adds nothing.

## Core Concept
Grant extends rethinking from the individual to the collective: how do you build a *culture* where people, teams, and whole organizations revise their views? His answer centers on the difference between two kinds of culture. A **performance culture** prizes getting results and looking good, which quietly punishes the admission of error and the surfacing of problems. A **learning culture** prizes growth and improvement, which makes it safe to say "I was wrong" and "this isn't working." The substrate of a learning culture is **psychological safety**.

Grant draws heavily on Amy Edmondson's research on psychological safety — the shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. A crucial corrective Grant emphasizes: psychological safety is **not** lowering standards, being nice, or avoiding accountability. Edmondson's data show that the highest-performing teams combine **high psychological safety with high accountability**. Safety without standards produces a comfortable, complacent team; standards without safety produce anxiety and concealment. Only the combination produces a team that takes on hard problems, admits mistakes early, and learns fast. The common misreading — that safety means going easy — is exactly backwards.

A second core idea is that psychological safety is **built from the top by modeling fallibility**. When leaders openly admit their own mistakes and say "here's what I got wrong," they license everyone else to do the same. Grant describes leaders and organizations that institutionalize this — for example, the practice of leaders sharing their own failures, or systematically inviting criticism of their decisions. The opposite — leaders who project infallibility — teaches everyone that error is shameful and must be hidden, which is precisely how small problems become catastrophes (he returns to the NASA cases, where the inability to surface concern proved fatal).

Grant also stresses the design of **rituals and structures** that institutionalize rethinking. Examples he discusses include conducting regular "rethinking" reviews; building in mechanisms to surface authentic dissent (rather than token devil's advocacy); separating the people who make a decision from the people who evaluate it; running pre-mortems (imagining a future failure and asking why) and blameless post-mortems (examining what went wrong without punishing the messenger); and rewarding the *process* of good decision-making rather than only good outcomes — because in an uncertain world, good decisions sometimes have bad outcomes and vice versa. Tying recognition to outcomes alone teaches people to avoid intelligent risks and to hide failures.

A further point is that learning cultures treat **information and feedback as gifts owed to the group**, not as personal property or threats. Grant connects this to his feedback-as-care work: cultures where critical feedback flows freely and is received as help, not attack, learn faster. And he notes that scale matters — large organizations drift toward performance cultures by default, so a learning culture must be actively maintained against that gravitational pull.

For development organizations, donors, and evaluation units, this is directly relevant and often painfully absent. Many M&E systems are embedded in performance cultures that reward reporting success and punish honest accounts of failure — which destroys the very learning that evaluation is supposed to produce. Grant's frame argues for building the conditions under which negative findings can be told and used: leaders who model "here's what we got wrong," recognition for honest learning rather than only for green dashboards, and blameless review structures that treat a failed pilot as information rather than as a black mark.

## How to Apply
1. **Pair safety with accountability.** Aim for high safety *and* high standards; neither alone produces learning.
2. **Model fallibility from the top.** Leaders should openly own mistakes to license everyone else to.
3. **Reward decision process, not just outcomes.** Recognize good reasoning even when results are mixed, so people take intelligent risks.
4. **Institutionalize rethinking.** Build pre-mortems, blameless post-mortems, and authentic-dissent mechanisms into routine.
5. **Make honest failure tellable.** Especially in M&E, create conditions where negative findings can surface and be used.

## Examples
**Situation:** An NGO's M&E unit consistently reports success, and staff quietly admit that failures never make it into reports.
**Application:** That's a performance culture punishing honesty. Leaders should model fallibility ("here's what our flagship got wrong"), reward honest learning, and adopt blameless reviews so failed pilots become information, not black marks.
**Result:** Negative findings start surfacing early enough to act on, and the evaluation function begins to actually drive learning.

**Situation:** A leader thinks building psychological safety means being gentler and easing deadlines.
**Application:** Correct the misreading: the highest-performing teams pair safety with *high* accountability. Keep the standards; change what's punishable — make it safe to admit error and raise problems while holding the bar high.
**Result:** The team takes on harder problems and surfaces issues earlier, without any drop in standards.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Equate psychological safety with lower standards or niceness.
**Why:** The best teams combine high safety with high accountability.

**Don't:** Reward only outcomes.
**Why:** It teaches people to avoid intelligent risks and hide failures; reward the decision process too.

**Don't:** Expect a learning culture to maintain itself.
**Why:** Organizations drift toward performance cultures by default; rethinking must be actively built.
