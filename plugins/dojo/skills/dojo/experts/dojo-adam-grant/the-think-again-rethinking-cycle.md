# The Think Again Rethinking Cycle

## When to Use
- When someone digs into a position even as they suspect it's wrong.
- When a belief, plan, or strategy has fused with the person's sense of who they are.
- When the user wants the *skill* of updating, not a verdict on a specific question.
- When intelligence is being used to defend a view rather than examine it.

## Fails When
- **The question is a core moral commitment.** Don't A/B test whether to treat people with dignity.
- **The user reads rethinking as flip-flopping.** Distinguish evidence-driven updating from drift.
- **The moment demands a decision**, not another round of reconsideration.

## Core Concept
*Think Again* (2021) makes a simple, uncomfortable claim: we spend enormous effort acquiring knowledge and almost none learning to question it. Rethinking — the willingness to revise our views in light of new evidence — is a distinct skill, and most people are bad at it, not because they're unintelligent but because rethinking is *uncomfortable*. In fact, intelligence can make it worse: the smarter you are, the better you are at constructing arguments for what you already believe. Grant calls this the curse of being good at being wrong.

The root problem is identity. We treat our opinions like possessions — things we own and don't want to lose. Worse, we let our beliefs become part of who we are, so that questioning a belief feels like questioning the self. The central discipline of rethinking is therefore **detaching your identity from your opinions**: "you are not your beliefs; you are the person examining them." When your sense of self is anchored in *values* (curiosity, learning, honesty) rather than *opinions* (a particular strategy or forecast), you can change your mind without feeling like you're dying.

Grant illustrates the cost of failing to rethink with the case of wildland firefighters who died because they wouldn't drop their heavy tools and run — the tools had become part of their identity as firefighters, and letting go felt like ceasing to be one. The metaphor runs throughout the book: the things that made us successful become the things we can't let go of, even when they're killing us.

A counterintuitive finding anchors the practice: research shows that the people most resistant to updating are often the most confident, and that **expertise can entrench rather than liberate** — experts sometimes become more closed because they've invested so much in their current view. The antidote is to cultivate the joy of being wrong. Grant describes scientists and forecasters who actually *enjoy* discovering errors because each one is a step toward being right. The best forecasters in Philip Tetlock's research weren't the ones with the most knowledge; they were the ones who updated their views most frequently and in the smallest increments — the "foxes" who held many loosely-held views over the "hedgehogs" with one big confident theory.

The rethinking cycle, in Grant's framing, runs: humility (I might be wrong) → doubt (let me question this) → curiosity (what else could be true?) → discovery (I've learned something). Its opposite — the overconfidence cycle — runs: pride → conviction → confirmation → validation, spiraling deeper into a belief regardless of evidence. The practical aim is to recognize which cycle you're in and to install habits that nudge you toward the first.

A specific, testable practice Grant cites: when people were asked to argue the *opposing* view before making a decision, they made better decisions — the exercise loosened the grip of their initial position. Another: scheduling regular "rethinking" check-ins, treating opinions as drafts with version numbers, and asking "what would change my mind?" as a routine question rather than a rhetorical one.

For evaluators and strategists, this is directly operational. A theory of change is a set of hypotheses, not a creed. A programme strategy is a draft, not an identity. The professional who can say "here's what I believed, here's what the data showed, here's what I now think" is demonstrating the highest form of rigor, not weakness.

## How to Apply
1. **Anchor identity in values, not opinions.** Be "someone who follows the evidence," not "the person who believes X."
2. **Ask the diagnostic question.** "What would change my mind?" If the answer is "nothing," you've found a belief you're defending, not testing.
3. **Argue the other side.** Before deciding, build the strongest case against your own view; it improves the decision.
4. **Treat views as drafts.** Give opinions version numbers; expect to revise them.
5. **Find the joy of being wrong.** Reframe each error as a discovery — the moment you got smarter, not the moment you lost.

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme lead has championed a particular delivery model for years and won't engage with data suggesting it underperforms.
**Application:** The model has become identity. Detach the two: "you're not the model — you're the person who wants the best outcomes for these communities. The model is a hypothesis you've been testing, and the test is now giving you information."
**Result:** The lead can examine the evidence without it feeling like self-betrayal.

**Situation:** A team is about to make a major commitment and everyone is confident.
**Application:** Assign people to argue the opposite position before deciding — the move shown to improve decisions by loosening the initial view.
**Result:** Hidden weaknesses surface before the commitment, not after.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Frame changing your mind as inconsistency or weakness.
**Why:** Evidence-driven updating is the highest form of rigor; stigmatizing it blocks learning.

**Don't:** Apply the hypothesis frame to core moral values.
**Why:** Some commitments aren't experiments; treating them as such is its own error.

**Don't:** Let confidence stand in for having tested the belief.
**Why:** The most confident are often the most resistant to updating — confidence is not evidence.
