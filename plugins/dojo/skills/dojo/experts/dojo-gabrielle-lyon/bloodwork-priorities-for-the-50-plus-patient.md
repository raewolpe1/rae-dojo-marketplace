# Bloodwork Priorities for the 50-Plus Patient

## When to Use
- When someone asks which labs to request.
- When a patient's standard physical came back "normal" but something is off.
- When preparing for a doctor's visit and deciding what to ask for.
- When teaching why the standard panel misses the early story.

## Fails When
- **The user wants a diagnosis.** Labs inform; a clinician diagnoses and treats. Flag abnormal results for medical review.
- **You hand out exact personal targets as prescriptions.** Give general ranges and note that optimal values are individualized.
- **A result is alarming.** That goes to a physician promptly, not to a dojo.

## Core Concept
The standard physical draws a thin panel - a basic metabolic panel, a standard lipid panel, maybe an HbA1c - and if those land in the lab's reference range, the patient is told they're "normal." The problem is that those reference ranges are built around the average population, which in midlife is itself metabolically unwell, and the standard panel omits the markers where the early story actually lives. "Normal" on a thin panel can sit on top of a decade of developing dysfunction. The goal of comprehensive bloodwork is to catch the trajectory early enough to change it.

The single most important add is **fasting insulin.** Glucose and HbA1c are lagging indicators - they rise only after the pancreas has been compensating with extra insulin for years. Fasting insulin reveals that compensation directly, often a decade before glucose moves. Paired with fasting glucose it lets you estimate insulin resistance (HOMA-IR). A "normal" glucose with a high-normal or elevated fasting insulin is one of the most common and most missed findings in midlife, and it is exactly the signal muscle-centric medicine wants, because it points straight back to the glucose sink.

On cardiovascular risk, the key upgrade is **ApoB.** A standard lipid panel reports total, LDL, and HDL cholesterol, but cardiovascular risk tracks better with the *number* of atherogenic particles, and ApoB counts them directly. Two people with the same LDL can have very different ApoB and very different risk. Add **Lp(a)** at least once in a lifetime - it is largely genetic, under-tested, and important when elevated. And add **hs-CRP**, a marker of systemic inflammation that adds independent risk information.

Then the supporting panel that rounds out the midlife picture: **HbA1c** and **fasting glucose** (the glucose story alongside insulin); a **full lipid panel** including triglycerides, where a high triglyceride-to-HDL ratio is itself a useful insulin-resistance clue; **vitamin D**, frequently low and relevant to muscle, bone, and immune function; **homocysteine**, tied to cardiovascular and cognitive risk and often responsive to B-vitamin status; **ferritin** and iron studies; a **thyroid panel** (TSH, and ideally free T4/T3); **comprehensive metabolic panel** for liver and kidney function; **CBC**; and **sex hormones** - testosterone (total and free) in men, and the relevant panel in women, because the hormonal terrain of midlife shapes everything from body composition to recovery.

The interpretive stance matters as much as the list. Lyon does not read these against the lab's wide "normal" range; she reads them against *optimal* ranges and, crucially, against the patient's own trend over time. A value drifting year over year toward the edge of normal is information you act on now, not after it crosses a line. The labs are not the goal - they are instrumentation for the trajectory, and they tell you where to apply the protein-training-supplement levers and whether those levers are working. One caution I always give: this is what to measure and why, but the interpretation of *your* results, and any treatment, belongs with a clinician who can see the whole person. Order the right tests, read them as a trend against optimal, and use them to steer - that is the third leg of the triangle doing its job.

## How to Apply
1. **Add the early-signal markers.** Insist on fasting insulin (with glucose for HOMA-IR), ApoB, hs-CRP, and Lp(a) at least once.
2. **Round out the panel.** HbA1c, full lipids with triglycerides, vitamin D, homocysteine, ferritin, thyroid, CMP, CBC, sex hormones.
3. **Read against optimal, not just normal.** Compare to optimal ranges and to the patient's own year-over-year trend.
4. **Connect labs to levers.** Use findings to direct protein, training, and supplement decisions, then re-test to confirm movement.
5. **Refer for interpretation.** Hand abnormal or personal-target decisions to a clinician; the dojo names what to measure and why.

## Examples
**Situation:** A 55-year-old says "my doctor said everything's normal" but feels flat and is gaining fat.
**Application:** Ask what was drawn. Usually no fasting insulin, no ApoB, no hs-CRP. Request those plus vitamin D, a full lipid panel with triglycerides, thyroid, and total/free testosterone. A normal glucose with high fasting insulin would explain a lot - and points straight to building the muscle sink.
**Result:** The hidden metabolic and hormonal picture surfaces, and the levers become obvious.

**Situation:** A patient has LDL "in range" and wants to know if their heart risk is fine.
**Application:** LDL in range doesn't settle it. Get ApoB to count the atherogenic particles and Lp(a) once. If ApoB is high despite a reassuring LDL, the risk is real and worth a cardiology/lipid conversation.
**Result:** A truer read on cardiovascular risk than the standard panel gave.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Accept "normal" without asking what was measured.
**Why:** The standard panel omits fasting insulin, ApoB, and hs-CRP - exactly where the early signal lives.

**Don't:** Treat lab reference ranges as targets.
**Why:** They're built around an unwell population. Read against optimal ranges and the individual's trend instead.
