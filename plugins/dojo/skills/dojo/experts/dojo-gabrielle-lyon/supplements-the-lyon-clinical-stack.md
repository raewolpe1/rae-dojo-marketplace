# Supplements - the Lyon Clinical Stack

## When to Use
- When someone asks what supplements are actually worth taking.
- When a patient is overwhelmed by supplement marketing and wants a short, defensible list.
- When teaching the principle that supplements support, not replace, food and training.
- When tying supplementation to lab values.

## Fails When
- **The user needs medical dosing.** Anything therapeutic for a condition belongs with a clinician, ideally guided by labs.
- **You drift into megadoses or fads.** The whole point of the stack is discipline; reaching for unproven compounds breaks it.
- **You let supplements eclipse the basics.** They are the small last layer on protein, training, and sleep - never a substitute.

## Core Concept
The supplement industry sells a fantasy: that the next compound is the lever. It isn't. Supplements are the smallest layer of the model, sitting on top of protein, resistance training, sleep, and a real diet - and the discipline of a good stack is keeping it short and justifying each item by mechanism and, ideally, by a lab value. If a supplement can't be defended that way, it doesn't belong in the cabinet. The test is not "could this help?" - almost anything could. The test is "is there a real mechanism and a real reason for *this* person?"

A small number of items earn their place for almost everyone in midlife. **Creatine monohydrate** is the most evidence-backed performance and muscle supplement there is - roughly 3-5g daily, no loading required. It supports strength, training output, and lean mass, and the evidence increasingly points to cognitive and healthy-aging benefits as well. It is cheap, safe, and well-studied; for a midlife adult trying to build and defend muscle, it is close to a default. **Vitamin D3** earns its place because deficiency is common and it matters for muscle, bone, and immune function - but here the discipline shows: you dose it to a measured blood level, not blindly, often with vitamin K2 alongside. **Omega-3 (EPA/DHA)** supports cardiovascular and anti-inflammatory status and may modestly help blunt anabolic resistance; quality and dose matter, and an omega-3 index test can guide it. **Magnesium** is commonly under-consumed and supports sleep, muscle function, and glucose metabolism.

Beyond that core, additions are *targeted* - driven by labs or a specific gap, not by enthusiasm. If bloodwork shows low vitamin D, you correct it and re-test. If homocysteine is elevated, B-vitamins (B12, folate, B6) may be indicated. If iron or ferritin is low, that's addressed specifically. **Protein powder** - whey in particular - is best thought of as a food tool rather than a supplement: a clean, leucine-dense way to hit the 30g-per-meal threshold, especially at breakfast or around training, when whole-food protein is inconvenient. It supplements the diet's protein target; it doesn't replace eating.

The disciplined stance also means saying no. Lyon is skeptical of the sprawling longevity-supplement cabinet - the rotating cast of compounds with thin human evidence, the megadoses, the proprietary blends. That skepticism is a feature, not a gap: it's what separates muscle-centric medicine from the functional-medicine and influencer versions of the same vocabulary. A supplement with strong mechanism and strong human data (creatine) is in; a fashionable molecule with a few mouse studies and a good marketing budget is out, or at most "interesting, not established."

So the practical answer to "what should I take?" is short and almost boring, which is the point. For most midlife adults: creatine daily, vitamin D dosed to a measured level (with K2), omega-3, and magnesium - then anything specific your labs reveal. And the framing I always attach: this is the last 5% of the result. If your protein and training and sleep aren't handled, no stack will save you; if they are handled, a tight, evidence-based stack adds a real but modest edge. Fix the basics first, then add the few supplements that have actually earned it. As always, run dosing and any condition-specific choices past a clinician - especially anything you're taking to a therapeutic level.

## How to Apply
1. **Lead with the basics.** Confirm protein, training, and sleep are handled before discussing any supplement.
2. **Use the core stack.** Creatine 3-5g/day, vitamin D3 dosed to a measured level (with K2), omega-3, magnesium.
3. **Target the rest by labs.** Add B-vitamins, iron, etc. only when bloodwork or a clear gap justifies it, then re-test.
4. **Treat protein powder as food.** Use whey to hit the 30g threshold when whole food is inconvenient - not as a meal replacement by default.
5. **Refuse the fads.** Exclude megadoses and thin-evidence compounds; demand mechanism plus human data.

## Examples
**Situation:** A 55-year-old asks for "a longevity stack" after seeing a dozen products online.
**Application:** Strip it down. Creatine, vitamin D (to a measured level, with K2), omega-3, magnesium - and whey to hit breakfast protein. Skip the rotating cabinet of trendy molecules until labs or evidence justify them. Reaffirm that this is the last 5% on top of protein and training.
**Result:** A short, affordable, defensible stack instead of an expensive, unproven one.

**Situation:** A patient's labs show vitamin D at the low end.
**Application:** Dose D3 specifically to bring the level into an optimal range, pair with K2, and re-test in a few months rather than guessing a dose forever. That's the disciplined, lab-guided approach.
**Result:** Corrected deficiency, confirmed by a follow-up draw.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Build a big stack of trendy compounds.
**Why:** Most have thin human evidence. A short, mechanism-and-data-justified stack is the disciplined approach.

**Don't:** Let supplements substitute for protein, training, or sleep.
**Why:** They're the last 5%. Without the basics, no stack delivers; with them, it adds only a modest edge.
