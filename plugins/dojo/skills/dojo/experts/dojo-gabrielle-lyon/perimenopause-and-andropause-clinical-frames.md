# Perimenopause and Andropause Clinical Frames

## When to Use
- When someone in midlife asks how hormones are affecting their body composition or recovery.
- When the topic is perimenopause/menopause or low testosterone/andropause.
- When framing how hormonal decline modulates the protein-training-bloodwork triangle.
- When a patient is considering hormone therapy and wants the lay of the land.

## Fails When
- **The user wants an HRT/TRT prescription.** Hormone therapy decisions belong with a qualified clinician; this is the frame, not the script.
- **You make definitive treatment calls.** Present considerations and route to medical care for individualized decisions.
- **You let hormones eclipse the basics.** Protein and training remain the foundation regardless of hormonal status.

## Core Concept
Midlife is, among other things, a hormonal transition, and the transition differs sharply by sex - which is why it gets its own frame. In women, perimenopause and menopause bring a relatively abrupt decline in estrogen over a few years. In men, andropause is a gradual decline in testosterone, on the order of roughly 1% per year from around age 30, accumulating into something significant by the fifties and sixties. Both shifts directly affect the tissue this whole model cares about - muscle - along with fat distribution, bone, recovery, and metabolic risk. The frame here is not to treat the hormones in place of the muscle, but to understand how the changing hormonal terrain modulates how the triangle is applied.

For **women**, estrogen is more metabolically protective than it's usually credited for. Its decline through perimenopause is associated with accelerated loss of muscle and bone, a shift of fat toward the visceral (abdominal) depot, rising insulin resistance, worsening lipids, and changes in recovery and sleep. Body composition can change quickly and frustratingly even when behavior hasn't. The crucial clinical message is that this window is precisely when the triangle matters *most*, not least: protein at the per-meal threshold to defend muscle against an environment now working against it; resistance training to protect bone (estrogen loss accelerates bone loss, raising osteoporosis risk) and muscle and insulin sensitivity; and comprehensive bloodwork to track the metabolic shifts as they happen. Menopausal hormone therapy is a legitimate medical option for many women, with implications for symptoms, bone, and possibly body composition - but it is an individualized decision for a knowledgeable clinician, and it sits *alongside*, never instead of, the protein-and-training foundation.

For **men**, the testosterone decline is slower but real, and it interacts with the model in a feedback loop worth naming. Lower testosterone is associated with loss of muscle and strength, increased fat (especially visceral), reduced drive and recovery, and worse metabolic markers. But the loop runs both ways: being under-muscled, over-fat, under-trained, poorly slept, and insulin-resistant *further suppresses* testosterone. This matters enormously, because it means the first-line response to low-normal testosterone in midlife men is usually not a prescription - it's fixing the inputs. Building muscle, losing visceral fat, training, sleeping, and correcting metabolic dysfunction often raises testosterone meaningfully on their own. Testosterone replacement therapy is a real medical tool for genuinely deficient, symptomatic men, but it should be a clinical decision made on proper labs (total and free testosterone, plus the supporting workup) and after the lifestyle inputs have been addressed - not a shortcut around them.

So the synthesis Lyon would offer is consistent for both sexes: the hormonal transition of midlife raises the stakes and changes the urgency, but it does not change the foundation. It makes protein, resistance training, and targeted bloodwork *more* important, not less, because the body's own anabolic and protective signaling is weakening and the lifestyle levers have to compensate harder. Hormone therapy - HRT for women, TRT for men - is a legitimate consideration to discuss with a clinician, informed by proper labs, and always built on top of the foundation rather than used to avoid it. I have patients of both sexes who arrived blaming hormones for everything; in most, getting protein, training, sleep, and fat loss right transformed the picture, and the hormonal conversation that remained was clearer and better-informed for it. Frame the hormones honestly, run the labs, and hold the foundation first.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the transition by sex.** Abrupt estrogen decline (women) vs gradual testosterone decline (men); both hit muscle, fat, bone, recovery.
2. **Raise the urgency, not a new model.** Treat midlife as when the triangle matters most - protein, training, bloodwork intensify, not change.
3. **Protect bone in women.** Emphasize resistance training and protein against accelerated menopausal bone loss; consider DEXA.
4. **Fix inputs before hormones in men.** Address muscle, visceral fat, training, sleep, and insulin resistance - they often raise testosterone.
5. **Route hormone therapy to a clinician.** Frame HRT/TRT as legitimate, lab-informed, individualized decisions built on the foundation.

## Examples
**Situation:** A 52-year-old woman in perimenopause is gaining abdominal fat and losing strength despite no change in habits.
**Application:** Validate that the hormonal shift is real and is working against her, then intensify the foundation: hold protein at the per-meal threshold, prioritize progressive resistance training for muscle and bone, and run a full panel. Raise HRT as a legitimate option to discuss with her clinician, on top of - not instead of - this.
**Result:** She defends muscle and bone through the transition and has an informed hormone conversation.

**Situation:** A 55-year-old man with low-normal testosterone wants TRT.
**Application:** Get proper labs (total and free testosterone plus workup), but first address the inputs: build muscle, drop visceral fat, train, sleep, fix insulin resistance - these often raise testosterone and break the suppressive loop. If he's genuinely deficient and symptomatic after that, TRT is a clinical conversation.
**Result:** Often a meaningful natural rise; if not, a properly informed medical decision.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Prescribe or definitively recommend hormone therapy.
**Why:** HRT/TRT are individualized medical decisions requiring proper labs and a clinician; the dojo frames, it doesn't prescribe.

**Don't:** Let "it's my hormones" excuse skipping the foundation.
**Why:** The hormonal shift makes protein and training more important, and in men fixing inputs often improves the hormones themselves.
