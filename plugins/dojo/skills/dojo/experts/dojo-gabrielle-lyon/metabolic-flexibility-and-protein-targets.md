# Metabolic Flexibility and Protein Targets

## When to Use
- When someone asks how to set all their macros, not just protein.
- When a patient is caught between competing diet ideologies (keto vs high-carb, etc.).
- When teaching metabolic flexibility - the ability to switch fuels efficiently.
- When tailoring carbohydrate to activity and metabolic health.

## Fails When
- **The user only wants the protein dose.** Route to the 30g rule; this file is the surrounding structure.
- **There's a condition needing supervised dietary therapy.** That belongs with a clinician/dietitian.
- **You turn it into dogma.** The whole point is flexibility around a fixed anchor, not another rigid ideology.

## Core Concept
Most diet debates argue about the wrong thing. Keto versus high-carb, low-fat versus low-carb - these fights are about the variable macros while ignoring the one that should be fixed. In muscle-centric medicine, protein is the anchor, and carbohydrate and fat flex around it. Set protein first, to a real target, and a great deal of the ideological noise falls away.

Start with the anchor. Protein is non-negotiable and goes first: roughly 1 gram per pound of ideal body weight, distributed so each meal clears the leucine threshold (about 30g, more in older adults). Protein is prioritized not only because it builds and defends muscle, but because it is the most satiating macronutrient and has the highest thermic effect - your body spends more energy digesting it - so a protein-anchored diet tends to regulate appetite and body composition almost as a side effect. Once protein is set, the remaining calories are divided between carbohydrate and fat according to the person's activity, training, and metabolic health. That division is where flexibility lives, and where it should.

This is where **metabolic flexibility** comes in - the capacity to switch efficiently between burning carbohydrate and burning fat depending on availability and demand. A metabolically flexible person uses fat well at rest and during easy activity, and taps carbohydrate efficiently for harder efforts; their fuel system is responsive. Metabolic *in*flexibility - being stuck, unable to access fat, riding glucose swings - travels with insulin resistance and the under-muscled, under-trained state. The goal of the model is to restore flexibility, and the two biggest levers are exactly the ones already in play: building muscle (a larger, healthier glucose sink and a better fat-oxidizing engine) and training (which directly improves mitochondrial function and fuel-switching).

Carbohydrate, then, is dosed to the person rather than demonized or mandated. An active, lean, insulin-sensitive person who trains hard can use and benefit from more carbohydrate - it fuels performance and replenishes muscle glycogen, and pairing carbs with training puts them where the working muscle can dispose of them. A sedentary, insulin-resistant person with a small glucose sink does better with fewer, higher-quality carbohydrates until training and muscle have enlarged the sink. Carbohydrate quality and *timing relative to activity* matter more than a single universal number. Fat then fills the remainder - enough for hormones, satiety, and fat-soluble vitamins, with attention to quality.

This is why Lyon is not dogmatic about carbohydrate and is explicitly not a carnivore evangelist. The model accommodates a range of eating patterns - lower-carb or higher-carb, more or less animal protein - as long as the protein anchor is hit at quality and the carbohydrate load matches the person's activity and metabolic state. What it does not accommodate is the common failure mode: a diet that under-hits protein while arguing passionately about everything else. Fix the anchor, restore flexibility through muscle and training, and dose carbs to the life the person actually leads. I have patients who thrive on more carbs because they train hard and have the muscle to handle them, and patients who do better lower-carb because they're rebuilding insulin sensitivity from a sedentary start. Both are correct, because both got the protein right first and then flexed the rest to fit. That is the framework: anchor protein, build the engine, and let carbohydrate and fat move to match.

## How to Apply
1. **Set protein first.** ~1g/lb ideal body weight, distributed to clear the threshold each meal. This is fixed before anything else.
2. **Flex carbs to activity and metabolic health.** More for the lean, trained, insulin-sensitive; fewer (and higher quality) for the sedentary or insulin-resistant.
3. **Time carbs to training.** Place more carbohydrate around hard sessions, where working muscle disposes of it.
4. **Build flexibility with muscle and training.** Use resistance training and added muscle to restore efficient fuel-switching.
5. **Fill the rest with quality fat.** Enough for hormones, satiety, and fat-soluble vitamins; mind the quality.

## Examples
**Situation:** A 55-year-old is torn between keto and a higher-carb plan.
**Application:** Reframe: both can work, but only after protein is anchored at ~1g/lb and distributed. Then, given he's just starting to train and is insulin-resistant, start lower-carb with quality sources, and add carbohydrate around training as his muscle and insulin sensitivity improve.
**Result:** He stops fighting the ideology war and runs a flexible, protein-anchored plan tuned to his state.

**Situation:** An active patient who trains hard fears carbs.
**Application:** With ample muscle and high activity, he has a large glucose sink and benefits from carbohydrate to fuel and recover - placed around training. Carb-phobia here costs him performance and recovery for no metabolic benefit.
**Result:** Carbs are dosed to his training; performance and recovery improve.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Argue carbs vs fat before protein is set.
**Why:** Protein is the anchor; the variable macros only make sense once it's fixed.

**Don't:** Apply one carbohydrate prescription to everyone.
**Why:** Carb tolerance depends on muscle, training, and insulin sensitivity - it must be dosed to the individual.
