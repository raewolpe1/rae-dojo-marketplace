# Anabolic Resistance and the Aging Muscle

## When to Use
- When someone asks why building or keeping muscle gets harder with age.
- When a routine that worked at 35 has stopped working at 55.
- When justifying why midlife requires more protein and harder training, not less.
- When teaching sarcopenia prevention.

## Fails When
- **The user needs the protein dose.** This explains why the dose must rise; route to the 30g rule for the number.
- **The user needs the training plan.** Route to resistance training for the prescription.
- **You frame it as doom.** Anabolic resistance is an input problem you solve by escalating inputs, not an inevitability.

## Core Concept
Anabolic resistance is the central biological fact of the aging muscle, and understanding it changes everything about how you eat and train in midlife. The phenomenon is this: as you age, the same dose of protein and the same bout of resistance training produce a smaller muscle-building response than they did in your twenties. The anabolic machinery has, in effect, gone harder of hearing. To get the same signal through, you have to speak louder.

Mechanistically, several things shift. The muscle's sensitivity to leucine - the amino acid that triggers muscle protein synthesis - declines, so a dose that flipped the synthetic switch easily at 25 may only partially flip it at 60. There is also blunted blood flow and nutrient delivery to muscle after a meal, reduced sensitivity to the anabolic actions of insulin, low-grade chronic inflammation ("inflammaging") that interferes with signaling, and often simply less protein eaten and less loading done. Layered on top is the steady background of sarcopenia - the age-related loss of muscle mass and strength that, untreated, accelerates from roughly the fourth decade onward and picks up speed after 60.

The critical point - and the one that flips people's intuition - is what the correct response is. Because the tissue responds less, the temptation is to conclude that effort is futile and to do less. That is exactly backwards. The clinical answer to a receptor that has gone quiet is to turn up the volume on both inputs. On protein: raise the per-meal dose to overcome the higher leucine threshold - which is precisely why the 30-gram-per-meal figure is a floor for older adults and many do better at 35-40+, and why the first meal after the overnight catabolic fast matters so much. On training: the muscle still responds to mechanical loading throughout life - the capacity to build muscle is preserved into old age - but it needs a genuine, progressive stimulus, not token movement. Adequate load plus adequate protein still works; under-dosing either one does not.

This is also why midlife is the moment to escalate rather than coast. Anabolic resistance begins to bite around the fifth decade - exactly when many people are getting busier, training less, and assuming their old habits will carry them. They won't. The 1,800-calorie, low-protein, cardio-only pattern that maintained a 35-year-old will let a 55-year-old quietly lose muscle year after year, and that lost muscle is the reserve and the metabolic protection they will need most later. The decline people attribute to "just getting older" is, to a substantial degree, anabolic resistance going unmet by adequate inputs.

So the framing I give patients is hopeful but demanding. Your muscle has not stopped listening - it has turned the volume down. You can still build and defend it at 55, 65, even 75. But you have to meet the resistance with more deliberate inputs than you needed before: more protein per meal, especially at breakfast; harder, progressive resistance training; and the consistency to keep doing both. I have seen patients in their sixties add real muscle and strength because they finally fed and loaded the tissue at the dose it now requires. The resistance is real. It is also answerable. You answer it by doing more of the right things, not by giving up.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the mechanism.** Explain that the muscle responds less per unit input - leucine sensitivity and anabolic signaling decline with age.
2. **Raise the protein dose.** Treat 30g/meal as a floor; push to 35-40+ to clear the higher threshold, protecting breakfast.
3. **Increase the training stimulus.** Progressive, genuinely challenging resistance work - token movement won't overcome the resistance.
4. **Escalate, don't coast.** Frame midlife as the time to do more of the right things, because old habits now lose ground.
5. **Hold consistency.** The response is smaller per session, so the win comes from showing up repeatedly over months.

## Examples
**Situation:** A 54-year-old says the routine that kept him lean at 38 isn't working anymore.
**Application:** That's anabolic resistance plus drifting inputs. His old protein and training are now sub-threshold for his physiology. Raise protein to ~1g/lb ideal weight at 35-40g/meal, add progressive resistance training 3x/week, and give it months of consistency.
**Result:** The plateau breaks - not with a trick, but by meeting the higher input requirement.

**Situation:** A 62-year-old believes it's "too late" to build muscle.
**Application:** It isn't. The capacity to build muscle is preserved into old age; the response is just smaller per session. With adequate protein and progressive loading, untrained older adults make substantial gains. The answer to a quieter signal is louder inputs, not surrender.
**Result:** Real strength and lean-mass gains, and a reframe from resignation to agency.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Conclude that less response means less effort is warranted.
**Why:** It's the opposite - a quieter anabolic signal calls for larger protein doses and harder training.

**Don't:** Present aging muscle loss as inevitable.
**Why:** Much of it is unmet anabolic resistance. Met with adequate inputs, the tissue still builds and defends well into old age.
