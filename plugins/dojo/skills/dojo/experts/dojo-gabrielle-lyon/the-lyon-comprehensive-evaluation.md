# The Lyon Comprehensive Evaluation

## When to Use
- When someone wants the structure of a complete midlife assessment.
- When assembling the whole model into an end-to-end intake.
- When a patient asks "what should a real check-up actually include?"
- When you need to organize body composition, function, labs, and lifestyle into one picture.

## Fails When
- **The user needs one component.** Route to the specific file (bloodwork, training, protein) for depth.
- **It's offered as a substitute for an exam.** This is the assessment framework; an in-person clinical workup still matters.
- **You list only labs.** The functional and body-composition assessment is half the value - don't skip it.

## Core Concept
A muscle-centric evaluation is broader than the standard annual physical, because it is built to assess the things that actually predict how a person will age - and most of those things the standard visit never measures. The evaluation has four domains: body composition, function and strength, comprehensive bloodwork, and lifestyle inputs. Together they produce a picture of the muscle and the metabolism, and a clear list of levers to pull.

**Body composition** comes first, because the scale is nearly useless on its own. What matters is how much lean (muscle) mass the person carries, how much fat - and especially visceral fat - and the ratio between them. A DEXA scan is the gold standard, giving lean mass, fat mass, visceral fat, and bone density in one measurement; bioimpedance and circumference measures are reasonable lower-cost proxies. Tracking lean mass over time tells you whether the model is working in the way that matters, not just whether weight changed.

**Function and strength** is the domain the standard physical ignores entirely, and it's a powerful predictor of healthy aging. Grip strength is a simple, validated marker that tracks with mortality. Other useful measures include leg strength and lower-body power, a sit-to-stand or chair-rise test, gait speed, balance, and VO2 max or a cardiorespiratory proxy. These tell you about real-world capacity and fall/frailty risk - the things that determine independence later - and they give concrete baselines to train against and re-test.

**Comprehensive bloodwork** is the third domain, detailed in its own file: fasting insulin and glucose (with HOMA-IR), HbA1c, ApoB and a full lipid panel, Lp(a) at least once, hs-CRP, vitamin D, homocysteine, ferritin/iron, thyroid, a comprehensive metabolic panel, CBC, and sex hormones. Read against optimal ranges and the person's own trend, not just the lab's wide "normal." This is the metabolic instrumentation that catches the trajectory early.

**Lifestyle inputs** complete the picture, because the labs and composition are outputs of these. Assess actual protein intake (total and per-meal distribution - not "do you eat healthy" but grams), the real training program (frequency, type, intensity, progression - distinguishing genuine resistance training from "I'm active"), sleep quantity and quality, alcohol, stress, and daily movement. This is where the causes of the outputs live, and where most of the intervention will happen.

The output of the evaluation is not a grade; it's a prioritized plan. You synthesize the four domains into the few levers that will move the most: almost always some combination of raising protein to target and distribution, installing or upgrading resistance training, correcting specific lab findings, and fixing the most broken lifestyle input (usually sleep or protein). Then you set baselines and a re-test cadence - labs and body composition at sensible intervals, strength markers tracked through training - so progress is measured, not assumed. The discipline is to assess comprehensively but intervene on the vital few. I run a new midlife patient through all four domains precisely so the plan that follows is targeted rather than generic - and so that six months later we can prove, in lean mass and lab values and grip strength, that it worked. As always, this framework organizes the assessment; an in-person clinician handles examination, diagnosis, and anything the labs flag.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess body composition.** Lean mass, fat mass, visceral fat, bone density - DEXA ideally, proxies otherwise; track lean mass over time.
2. **Measure function and strength.** Grip strength, lower-body strength/power, sit-to-stand, gait, balance, VO2 max - the physical's blind spot.
3. **Run comprehensive labs.** The full panel from the bloodwork file, read against optimal ranges and personal trend.
4. **Audit lifestyle inputs.** Quantify protein (total + distribution), real training, sleep, alcohol, stress, daily movement.
5. **Synthesize and re-test.** Prioritize the vital-few levers, set baselines, and schedule re-measurement to prove progress.

## Examples
**Situation:** A 55-year-old wants "a proper check-up, not just the usual."
**Application:** Run all four domains: DEXA for body composition and bone, grip and lower-body strength plus VO2 proxy for function, the full comprehensive panel, and a quantified audit of protein, training, and sleep. Synthesize into three or four priority levers and set a six-month re-test.
**Result:** A complete baseline and a targeted plan, with the means to measure whether it's working.

**Situation:** A patient has good labs but has never had strength or body composition measured.
**Application:** Add the missing domains. Good labs with low lean mass and weak grip is still a problem the standard physical would have called "fine." Measure composition and function, then train against those baselines.
**Result:** Hidden risk surfaces; the plan addresses muscle and strength, not just chemistry.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce the evaluation to a lab panel.
**Why:** Body composition and functional strength are half the predictive value and the standard physical's biggest blind spot.

**Don't:** Assess without re-testing.
**Why:** Baselines only matter if you re-measure. The re-test cadence is what proves the levers are working.
