# Business Model Innovation

## When to Use
- When an innovation pillar is built on R&D, patents, and technology transfer.
- When asking how ecosystem firms scale.
- When designing innovation support or innovation metrics.

## Fails When
- **The object is a science and technology innovation system.** R&D and patents are the right instruments there. This is about the other thing.

## Core Concept

**Entrepreneurial ecosystems are distinguished by their emphasis on business model innovation — and on scale-up through disruptive business model innovation.**

This is the departure from the innovation-systems tradition, and it is the one that most reliably breaks an innovation pillar.

**The innovation-systems picture:** innovation originates in R&D; it is embodied in technology; it is protected by patents; it diffuses through universities, labs and technology transfer; it is measured by R&D spend, patent counts, and publications.

**The entrepreneurial-ecosystem picture:** innovation is in **how value is created, delivered and captured** — the business model. The technology may be entirely off the shelf. The novelty is in the configuration: who pays, for what, when, through what channel, at what unit economics, with what asset base. Uber invented no technology. Nor did most of what an ecosystem produces.

**Why this follows from the digital affordance.** Digitalisation collapsed the cost of trying a configuration. When you can test a business model against real customers in weeks, using components built by strangers, at near-zero marginal distribution cost, the scarce activity stops being *invention* and becomes *configuration*. That is why ecosystems emphasise business model innovation: the affordance made it the binding activity.

**Three consequences for a strategy.**

**The finance is different.** R&D grants fund invention. Business model innovation needs runway to iterate against customers, which is a different instrument with a different risk profile and a different assessment method — you cannot peer-review a business model, you can only watch it meet a market.

**The support is different.** Technology transfer offices, IP support and lab access serve invention. Business model innovation needs customers, iteration, and generic knowledge from people who have configured something before.

**The metrics are different, and this is where strategies fail visibly.** A patent count will not see any of it. Neither will R&D intensity. An ecosystem can be producing substantial business model innovation and score zero on every indicator in a conventional innovation pillar — at which point the strategy concludes it has an innovation problem when it has a measurement problem.

**The honest scope.** Business model innovation is not superior to technology innovation; it is a different object with a different apparatus. A region needs both, and confusing them means funding one and measuring the other. **Note also the local relevance:** much innovation in developing contexts is **new to the market rather than new to the world** — which is precisely business model innovation's territory, and which conventional innovation metrics are constructed not to count.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask where the novelty is.** In the technology, or in the configuration?
2. **Split the innovation pillar.** Technology innovation and business model innovation, each with its own instruments and metrics.
3. **Do not measure business model innovation with patents.** It will read as zero.
4. **Fund runway, not research**, on the business model side.
5. **Support with operators, not IP lawyers**, on the business model side.
6. **Count new-to-market as innovation.** Conventional metrics do not, and in this context it is most of it.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy's innovation pillar targets R&D intensity, patent applications, and university technology transfer.

**Application:** That pillar measures and funds the innovation system. It is legitimate, and it will report near-zero movement from an entrepreneurial ecosystem, because ecosystems innovate in business models, using largely off-the-shelf technology. The strategy will then conclude that its ecosystem is not innovative. It is not measuring. Recommendation: split the pillar. Keep R&D, patents and technology transfer for the innovation system, targeted at the universities and research-intensive firms that actually do that. Add a business model innovation track: runway finance, iteration support, operator mentoring, and metrics that count new-to-market configurations, new revenue models, and firms reaching scale on unchanged technology.

**Result:** An innovation pillar that can see both kinds of innovation.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Measure ecosystem innovation with patents.
**Why:** The technology is off the shelf. The novelty is in the model, and no patent office records it.

**Don't:** Fund business model innovation with R&D grants.
**Why:** It needs runway to iterate against customers, not research funding to invent.

**Don't:** Treat new-to-market as not-innovation.
**Why:** In a developing context it is most of what happens, and it is business model innovation's home ground.
