# Generic versus Industry Knowledge

## When to Use
- When enterprise support is organised by sector or value chain.
- When asking what an ecosystem actually trades in.
- **This is the distinguishing test.** Use it to settle the cluster-or-ecosystem question.

## Fails When
- **The object genuinely is an industrial cluster.** Then industry knowledge is exactly right and this file's critique does not apply.

## Core Concept

**Entrepreneurial ecosystems are the only cluster type facilitating generic business knowledge, instead of industry knowledge or technology knowledge.**

This is the sharpest of the four distinguishing claims, because it is directly observable and it has an immediate architectural consequence.

**What circulates where:**

| Object | Knowledge commodity | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Industrial cluster** | **Industry knowledge** | How this process works; who supplies this input; what this buyer specifies |
| **Knowledge cluster** | **Technology knowledge** | This science; this technique; this frontier |
| **Regional innovation system** | **Technology knowledge**, institutionally mediated | Universities, labs, R&D |
| **Entrepreneurial ecosystem** | **Generic business knowledge** | How to raise a round; how to make a first sales hire; how to price; when to pivot; what this investor actually wants |

**Why generic knowledge is the differentiator.** It **travels across sectors**. How to hire your first salesperson is the same problem in agri-tech and fintech. How to price a subscription is the same problem in health and logistics. That portability is what makes an entrepreneurial ecosystem **heterogeneous in sector and homogeneous in method** — the opposite shape from a cluster, which is homogeneous in sector and heterogeneous in nothing much.

**The architectural consequence, which is severe.** **A support architecture organised by sector cannot deliver generic knowledge.** It is not that it delivers it badly; it is structurally prevented. Sector programmes convene people who share an industry, and what they exchange is industry knowledge — which is the cluster commodity. The founder who needs to know how to structure a seed round learns nothing from a room full of people in the same industry at the same stage, and everything from a room of people at different stages across many industries. **The venue determines the commodity.**

**The corollary for who should be in the room.** Generic knowledge flows **from those further along to those behind**, and sector is irrelevant to that flow. Which means the ecosystem's most valuable participants are experienced founders and operators — regardless of what industry they were in — and the sector expert is the cluster's asset, not the ecosystem's.

**The diagnostic.** Look at what your programmes convene. If the room shares an industry, you are running a cluster. If the room shares a *stage* and differs in industry, you are running an ecosystem. Most strategies convene by industry and call the result an ecosystem.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask what circulates.** Industry, technology, or generic business knowledge? This settles the taxonomy.
2. **Look at how the rooms are composed.** By sector, or by stage across sectors?
3. **If you want an ecosystem, convene across sectors and within stages.**
4. **Value the experienced operator over the sector expert** in an ecosystem context. Reverse it in a cluster.
5. **Do not fight the architecture.** A sector-organised department cannot deliver generic knowledge by adding a workstream. It needs a cross-cutting venue.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy delivers enterprise support through sector programmes — agri-processing, tourism, oil and gas, tech — each with its own incubation, BDS and market access offering.

**Application:** That is four cluster programmes. Each will circulate industry knowledge, which is the right commodity for a cluster and the wrong one for an ecosystem. If the strategy also claims to build an entrepreneurial ecosystem, it structurally cannot: the only cluster type that facilitates generic business knowledge is the entrepreneurial ecosystem, and generic knowledge circulates in venues composed across sectors and within stages, which this architecture never creates. Recommendation: keep the sector programmes and be honest that they are cluster development. Then add **one** cross-sector, stage-based venue — founders at similar stages, different industries, plus operators who are further along — and that is where the ecosystem, if there is one, will live.

**Result:** Cluster policy and ecosystem policy running side by side, each with an architecture that can deliver its commodity.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Expect a sector programme to build an ecosystem.
**Why:** The venue determines the commodity. Sector rooms circulate industry knowledge.

**Don't:** Staff an ecosystem programme with sector experts.
**Why:** The ecosystem's knowledge is generic and portable. The person you need has done the thing, in any industry.

**Don't:** Read "generic" as "basic".
**Why:** Generic means portable across sectors — how to raise, hire, price, pivot. It is the hardest knowledge in entrepreneurship, not the simplest.
