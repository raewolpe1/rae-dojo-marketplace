# On Freedom: The Five Forms

## When to Use
- When the question is about the specific components of freedom in *On Freedom* (2024).
- When someone wants the constructive framework — what freedom is made of — not just the positive/negative distinction.
- When freedom is being treated as a developmental good to be built, and you need the building blocks.
- When designing institutions or programmes toward substantive freedom.

## Fails When
- **The question is only the core distinction.** Route to positive-freedom-vs-libertarian-freedom for the foundational contrast.
- **You present the forms as rigid doctrine.** Snyder offers an interlocking account, not a checklist; the forms support one another.

## Core Concept
*On Freedom* (2024) is Snyder's constructive statement: having spent years analysing how freedom is lost, he sets out what freedom positively consists of. He frames freedom as a structured, shared condition built through institutions and relationships — not the empty space left when government withdraws — and articulates it through five interlocking forms. The forms are best understood as the conditions under which a human being can become a sovereign, self-determining person, and as a sequence that builds on itself: the earlier forms make the later ones possible.

**Sovereignty.** The capacity of a person to govern themselves — to know their own values and act on them. Sovereignty is not innate; it is developed, above all in childhood, through the care, security, education, and health that let a young person grow into someone who can reflect, judge, and choose rather than merely react. A society that wants free adults must build the conditions of childhood sovereignty. The unfree adult is often one who was never given the conditions to become sovereign in the first place.

**Unpredictability.** The freedom to be more than a predictable, classifiable, manipulable unit. Snyder treats unpredictability as central because the authoritarian and the data-extractive both depend on rendering people predictable — knowable, nudgeable, sortable. To be free is to retain the capacity to surprise, to do the unexpected, to escape the categories others impose. This connects to his concern with surveillance and with the reduction of persons to profiles: a person fully known and predicted is a person who can be managed, which is to say, unfree.

**Mobility.** The freedom to move — physically, socially, and across the boundaries that would confine a person to one place, one class, one fate. Mobility requires infrastructure (roads, transport, the physical means of movement) and the social conditions that make movement real rather than nominal. A person who cannot move is not free regardless of formal permission; mobility is a substantive capacity, not just an absence of legal barriers.

**Factuality.** The freedom that comes from access to a reliable, shared world of facts — the condition of being able to trust one's own perception of reality and to act on knowledge rather than manipulation. Factuality is freedom because a person living in a manufactured or unknowable reality cannot make genuine choices; their choices are products of whoever controls the information environment. This is the point at which *On Freedom* connects to Snyder's defence of truth: factuality is not just a public good but a personal freedom.

**Solidarity.** The recognition that one person's freedom depends on others' freedom — that freedom is not a possession of the isolated individual but a shared condition that must be built and defended together. Solidarity is the form that makes the others sustainable, because the institutions and public goods that produce sovereignty, mobility, and factuality are collective achievements. A "freedom" that pits each against all, in Snyder's account, is not freedom at all but a war of position that the strong win.

The forms are designed to interlock. Childhood sovereignty depends on a factual world and on solidarity-built public goods; mobility extends sovereignty into the world; factuality protects the judgement that sovereignty exercises; solidarity sustains the institutions that make all of it possible. Against the libertarian picture of freedom as the individual's emancipation from collective structures, Snyder's five forms describe freedom as something a society constructs for and with its members — which is exactly why it can be developed, measured, invested in, and, conversely, neglected and lost.

For development and governance work, the five forms offer a richer lens than either rights-talk or growth-talk alone. They suggest concrete questions: Are we building childhood sovereignty (health, education, security)? Are we preserving people's unpredictability against extractive surveillance? Are we building real mobility, not just formal permission? Are we defending the factual environment? Are we acting on the recognition that these are shared conditions requiring solidarity? It is a freedom agenda that a practitioner can actually design toward.

## How to Apply
1. **Use the five forms as a diagnostic.** Ask which forms a policy, programme, or society is building and which it is neglecting.
2. **Treat the forms as interlocking.** Show how a deficit in one (e.g. factuality) undermines the others (e.g. sovereignty).
3. **Anchor freedom in childhood sovereignty** as the developmental foundation.
4. **Connect factuality to the truth agenda** and solidarity to institutional defence.
5. **Translate into design questions** a practitioner can act on, rather than abstract principle.

## Examples
**Situation:** A government claims a deregulation drive will "maximise freedom."
**Application:** Test it against the five forms. Does it build childhood sovereignty, mobility, factuality? Or does it remove obstacles for the already-capable while leaving the conditions of substantive freedom unbuilt for everyone else? Deregulation may expand one negative freedom while leaving four positive forms unaddressed.
**Result:** The "freedom" claim is assessed against a richer account than absence-of-rules.

**Situation:** A development programme is being designed to "empower" a community.
**Application:** Translate "empowerment" into the five forms: which of sovereignty (health/education/security), unpredictability, mobility, factuality, and solidarity does it actually build? The framework converts a vague aspiration into specific, designable conditions of freedom.
**Result:** A diffuse goal becomes a structured set of freedom-building objectives.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the five forms as a rigid checklist.
**Why:** They interlock and reinforce; the value is in the relationships among them, not box-ticking.

**Don't:** Reduce freedom to the negative form when invoking the framework.
**Why:** The whole point of *On Freedom* is that the positive, built forms are what make freedom real.

**Don't:** Forget solidarity.
**Why:** It is the form that sustains the others; a freedom agenda that omits it collapses into a war of the strong against the weak.
