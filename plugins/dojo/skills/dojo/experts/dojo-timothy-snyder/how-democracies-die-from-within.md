# How Democracies Die From Within

## When to Use
- When diagnosing whether a democracy is eroding, and by what mechanism.
- When someone describes institutional capture, emergency powers, or the normalisation of the abnormal.
- When reading a political trend or country for backsliding risk.
- When the question is "how does this actually happen?"

## Fails When
- **The situation is normal democratic alternation.** Losing an election, unpopular policy, or partisan conflict is not erosion. Reserve the diagnosis for genuine attacks on the system itself.
- **The case is a classic external coup.** This frame is about the gradual, legal-in-form internal route, which is the characteristic modern path.

## Core Concept
The defining insight of Snyder's civic work is that modern democracies usually die from within, gradually, and in legal form — not by foreign conquest or a sudden putsch on day one. The image of tyranny arriving as tanks in the street is misleading and, worse, disarming: it teaches people to wait for a dramatic signal that never comes, while the actual erosion proceeds through a thousand undramatic steps that each seem survivable.

The mechanism has recognisable components. **Anticipatory obedience** comes first: officials, businesses, media, and citizens begin to adjust their behaviour to what they imagine the new power wants, before they are compelled to. This is the most important early phase, because it teaches the aspiring authoritarian what is possible and supplies most of the power he will use. **Normalisation** follows: each transgression that is not resisted becomes the new baseline, so that the next transgression is measured against an already-lowered standard. What would have been a scandal a year ago is now routine. The shifting of the baseline is precisely what makes the erosion hard to see from inside it.

**Institutional capture** is the structural work: the hollowing of the bodies that constrain power — courts, electoral commissions, auditors, independent media, the civil service — while their outward forms are preserved. The genius of the modern method is that it is often legal. Judges are not abolished; they are replaced or packed. Elections are not cancelled; they are tilted through gerrymandering, voter suppression, media dominance, and control of the count. The press is not shut down; it is bought, starved, intimidated, or drowned in state-aligned competitors. The shell of democracy remains while the substance is removed, which lets the regime claim democratic legitimacy precisely as it dismantles democracy.

**The manufactured crisis** is the accelerant. The classic move is to use — or manufacture — a terror event, an emergency, a threat, as the pretext for assuming extraordinary powers that are then never relinquished. Snyder draws this directly from the European twentieth century, where emergency decrees became the legal hinge on which republics turned into dictatorships. "Be calm when the unthinkable arrives" is a lesson precisely because the moment of shock is the moment the grab is designed for.

Running through all of this is **the assault on truth**, because a captured factual environment is what allows the other captures to proceed unchallenged. If citizens cannot establish what is happening, they cannot resist it; if every account is "just a narrative," then the regime's account is as good as any.

The reason the internal route is so dangerous is that each step is individually deniable and survivable, and resistance is always more costly tomorrow than today. The cost curve of resistance rises steeply: in the early phase, refusal is cheap and effective; by the late phase, the institutions that would protect the resister have already been captured. This is why Snyder's emphasis falls so heavily on early recognition. The whole value of understanding the pattern is to act while action is still cheap — to defend the court before it is packed, the count before it is rigged, the finding before it is suppressed.

## How to Apply
1. **Look for anticipatory obedience first.** Are people complying before they are ordered to? That is the leading indicator.
2. **Re-mark the baseline.** Name what was normal before, so the normalisation is visible.
3. **Check the constraining institutions.** Are courts, electoral bodies, auditors, and press independent, captured, or being captured?
4. **Watch for the crisis-pretext.** Ask who benefits from an emergency and who would gain the powers.
5. **Locate the phase and act early.** The cost of resistance rises with time; recognition is only valuable if it converts to early action.

## Examples
**Situation:** A government replaces the head of the electoral commission with a loyalist, citing "efficiency," while insisting elections will continue.
**Application:** This is textbook capture with the shell preserved. Elections continuing is not reassurance; the integrity of the count is the target. Name it as capture now, while the appointment can still be contested, rather than after the next tilted election.
**Result:** The move is identified as erosion in its early, contestable phase.

**Situation:** Observers reassure themselves that a country "is still a democracy" because elections are held and courts exist.
**Application:** The existence of forms is exactly what the modern method preserves. The question is not whether the institutions exist but whether they still constrain power. Examine the substance, not the shell.
**Result:** False reassurance from formal appearances is dismantled.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Wait for a dramatic, unambiguous signal before naming erosion.
**Why:** The modern method is gradual and legal; the dramatic signal is designed never to come until it is too late.

**Don't:** Mistake the survival of institutional forms for the survival of democracy.
**Why:** Capture preserves the shell precisely to launder the removal of the substance.

**Don't:** Diagnose erosion in ordinary political defeat.
**Why:** Inflating the diagnosis trains people to ignore it when it is real.
