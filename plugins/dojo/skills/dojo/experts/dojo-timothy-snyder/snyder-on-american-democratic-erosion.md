# Snyder on American Democratic Erosion

## When to Use
- When the specific case is the United States.
- When American exceptionalism — the "it can't happen here" belief — is the issue.
- When the European twentieth-century lens needs to be applied to American politics.
- When the question is whether and how US democracy is eroding.

## Fails When
- **The question is about another country.** Route to the African-backsliding or general erosion file.
- **You treat it as partisan commentary.** Snyder's analysis is structural — about mechanisms of erosion — not a recitation of party talking points.

## Core Concept
Snyder applies the European historian's eye to the United States with a deliberately disorienting premise: America is not exceptional in its immunity to authoritarianism, and the belief that it is constitutes a specific vulnerability. *On Tyranny* was written precisely as an intervention in the American moment after 2016, translating what Europeans learned in the 1920s and 1930s into lessons for Americans who had been raised to believe their republic could not fail.

The central American vulnerability he identifies is **exceptionalism itself** — the conviction that the United States is uniquely protected by its Constitution, its founding, its national character, so that the patterns which destroyed other democracies simply cannot operate here. Snyder treats this as the most dangerous belief in American politics, because it is precisely the belief that disarms vigilance. Every democracy that fell had citizens who were sure it could not fall. The Constitution is not a self-enforcing machine; it is a set of institutions that work only if people defend them. Faith that the document will save you regardless of what you do is a form of the politics of inevitability — the comfortable assumption that the good outcome is guaranteed and requires nothing of you.

He reads American politics through the inevitability-to-eternity transition. The post-Cold-War American story was a textbook politics of inevitability: liberal democracy had won, history had ended, capitalism and freedom would spread and endure automatically. When that story failed for many Americans — after the 2008 crash, amid deepening inequality and the hollowing of regions and livelihoods — the ground was prepared for a politics of eternity: a mythologised past greatness, a narrative of national victimhood at the hands of internal and external enemies, a leader whose failures are reinterpreted as proof of the conspiracy against him, and a relationship to truth in which loyalty replaces evidence. Snyder sees the American case not as unique but as a national variant of a pattern he documented in Russia and Europe.

The mechanisms he watches in the American context are the familiar ones: anticipatory obedience by officials, businesses, and media; the normalisation of conduct that would once have been disqualifying; the pressure on and attempted capture of constraining institutions — courts, the civil service, electoral administration, the independent press; the deployment of manufactured or amplified crisis as a pretext; and the sustained assault on factuality, including the importation of the managed-unreality techniques perfected in Russia. He pays particular attention to the contesting of elections and the peaceful transfer of power, which he regards as the load-bearing wall of a democracy: the moment a losing party refuses to concede a legitimate result, the ordinary machinery of democratic competition has been turned against democracy itself.

Snyder's stance is deliberately non-partisan in structure while being unflinching about asymmetry. His analysis is not "both parties are equally threatening democracy"; it is an analysis of where the specific erosion pressures are coming from, named accurately, in defiance of the both-sides reflex he regards as a form of euphemism. At the same time, his prescriptions are addressed to all citizens regardless of party: defend institutions, believe in truth, do not obey in advance, support the independent press, practice corporeal politics, defend the electoral process. The remedy is civic, not partisan.

For a non-American observer — including a development professional watching from Cape Town — the American case is instructive precisely because it shows that wealth, age, constitutional pedigree, and self-confidence confer no immunity. If the world's most self-assured democracy is vulnerable to the patterns Snyder traces, then the lesson generalises: no democracy is safe by virtue of its self-image, and the work of defending one is never finished.

## How to Apply
1. **Name exceptionalism as the core vulnerability.** "It can't happen here" is the belief that disarms the defence.
2. **Read the inevitability-to-eternity transition** in the American case specifically.
3. **Track the standard mechanisms** — anticipatory obedience, normalisation, institutional capture, crisis-pretext, assault on truth.
4. **Watch the transfer of power** as the load-bearing wall.
5. **Keep the remedy civic, not partisan**, while refusing false symmetry about where the pressure originates.

## Examples
**Situation:** Someone insists the US is fundamentally different and its institutions guarantee it can never become authoritarian.
**Application:** That is exceptionalism, and it is the precise belief Snyder identifies as most dangerous. The Constitution is not self-enforcing; every fallen democracy had citizens certain of their immunity. The confidence is the vulnerability.
**Result:** The reassurance is reframed as the disarming belief it is.

**Situation:** A news outlet frames an attempt to overturn an election result as a "partisan dispute" with two legitimate sides.
**Application:** This is the both-sides euphemism. The contesting of a legitimate transfer of power is an attack on the load-bearing wall of democracy, not an ordinary partisan disagreement. Naming it accurately is required; manufactured symmetry launders the attack.
**Result:** The euphemism is refused and the act is named for what it is structurally.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the Constitution as a self-enforcing guarantee.
**Why:** Institutions work only if defended; faith in automatic protection is the politics of inevitability.

**Don't:** Collapse the analysis into partisan cheerleading.
**Why:** Snyder's tools are structural; their authority comes from describing mechanisms, not scoring points.

**Don't:** Accept false symmetry to appear balanced.
**Why:** Symmetry where there is none launders the more powerful side's erosion; accuracy sometimes requires naming asymmetry.
