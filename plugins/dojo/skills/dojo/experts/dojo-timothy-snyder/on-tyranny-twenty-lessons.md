# On Tyranny: Twenty Lessons

## When to Use
- When someone faces a concrete decision to comply with, or resist, an authoritarian pressure.
- When the question is "what do I actually do?" rather than "what is happening in theory?"
- When early-stage erosion is present and the cost of resistance is still low.
- When teaching the practical repertoire of democratic self-defence.

## Fails When
- **The question is purely academic.** "What were the causes of Weimar's collapse?" is a history question; the lessons are for action.
- **The situation is ordinary politics.** Not every defeat, regulation, or disliked policy is tyranny. Crying wolf devalues the lessons. Reserve them for actual erosion.

## Core Concept
*On Tyranny* (2017) distils how Europeans lost their democracies in the twentieth century into twenty short lessons, each one a thing a citizen can do. The book's premise is that the people of the 1930s were not unusually weak or wicked; they made ordinary choices that, in aggregate, surrendered their freedom — and so the corrective is also a set of ordinary choices, made differently and early.

The lessons most load-bearing for a working professional:

**1. Do not obey in advance.** Most authoritarian power is freely given by people who anticipate what is wanted and offer it unbidden. Anticipatory obedience teaches power what is possible. Refuse to pre-comply; make demands be made openly.

**2. Defend an institution.** Institutions do not protect themselves; they survive only if specific people defend the particular one they belong to — a court, a newspaper, a union, a professional association, an election. Choose one and be its partisan.

**3. Beware the one-party state.** The parties that destroyed democracies exploited the ordinary mechanisms of democratic competition until competition itself was gone. Defend the rules of the contest, not just your side of it.

**4. Take responsibility for the face of the world.** The symbols of hatred and exclusion that appear in public space matter; do not let them become ordinary. Remove the swastika; do not look away from it.

**5. Remember professional ethics.** Tyranny needs lawyers who will draft the decree, doctors who will sign the certificate, accountants who will cook the books, civil servants who will administer the injustice. Professions with real ethical codes are a bulwark — if their members keep them when it counts.

**6. Be wary of paramilitaries and the mixing of force with politics.** When armed men loyal to a leader rather than a constitution appear, the danger is acute.

**7. Stand out.** Someone has to be first to refuse. The person who breaks the spell of conformity makes refusal thinkable for others.

**8. Believe in truth.** To abandon facts is to abandon freedom; the assault on truth precedes the assault on liberty.

**9. Investigate. Establish your own picture of the world.** Read long-form, support real reporting, subscribe, verify.

**10. Make eye contact and small talk.** The social fabric — knowing your neighbours, being known — is a form of resistance, because tyranny depends on isolation and mutual suspicion.

**11. Practice corporeal politics.** Get into unfamiliar places with unfamiliar people; show up in person; the screen is not enough.

**12. Be calm when the unthinkable arrives.** Manufactured terror events are the classic pretext for emergency power. The most important moment to keep your head is the moment designed to make you lose it.

The through-line is that freedom is defended in the unspectacular present, by people who refuse the small surrenders, before the surrenders become irreversible. The lessons are deliberately modest in scale because that is the scale at which freedom is actually lost and saved.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the specific lesson in play.** Most live situations map to one or two — usually "do not obey in advance" and "defend an institution."
2. **Locate the cost curve.** Early resistance is cheap; name how much cheaper it is now than it will be later.
3. **End in an act.** Translate the lesson into a concrete next step the person can take this week.
4. **Reserve the lessons for real erosion.** Do not deploy them against ordinary political loss; that cheapens them.

## Examples
**Situation:** A civil servant is told to stop publishing a routine dataset that has become politically inconvenient.
**Application:** "Defend an institution" and "do not obey in advance." The dataset is a small institution of accountability. Continue publishing under the existing mandate; require any halt to come as a written, signed instruction. Make the suppression visible and attributable rather than quiet and deniable.
**Result:** Either the dataset survives, or its suppression is on the record — which is itself a form of resistance.

**Situation:** A professional association is asked to issue a statement endorsing a government's "national unity" framing of a contested election.
**Application:** "Remember professional ethics." The association's value is its independence; spending it on a political endorsement is the surrender. Decline, citing the ethical code, before the request becomes a demand.
**Result:** The association keeps the credibility that makes it worth capturing — and worth defending.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use the twenty lessons as a label to attach to every political outcome you dislike.
**Why:** Inflation of the language of tyranny is itself a service to tyranny; it trains people to tune it out.

**Don't:** Stop at quoting the lesson.
**Why:** The lessons are imperatives. An unconverted lesson is decoration. Name the act.

**Don't:** Treat the lessons as American-specific.
**Why:** They are drawn from European history and apply wherever democracy erodes — including the contexts Rae works in.
