# Institutional Defence and Corporeal Politics

## When to Use
- When the question is what a single person or organisation can actually do.
- When an institution under pressure needs defenders, and you must identify who and how.
- When someone feels isolated, paralysed, or convinced resistance is pointless.
- When countering the substitution of online gesture for embodied political action.

## Fails When
- **The question is purely diagnostic.** If the task is to understand what is happening, route to the erosion file; this one is about the response.
- **You offer vague encouragement.** "Stand up for democracy" is empty. Name the specific institution and the specific act.

## Core Concept
Snyder's most distinctive practical contribution is the insistence that abstract freedom is defended through concrete, embodied, particular acts — above all, the defence of specific institutions by the specific people who belong to them, and the practice of "corporeal politics," politics conducted with the body, in person, alongside others.

The institutional argument runs counter to a common assumption that institutions are self-sustaining structures that will protect us. They are not. An institution — a court, a newspaper, a professional association, an electoral commission, a university, a union — is only as strong as the people inside it who choose to defend it when defending it is costly. "Defend an institution" is lesson two of *On Tyranny* for a reason: it is the structural heart of resistance. And the defence must be particular. You cannot defend "democracy" in the abstract; you can defend this court, this newspaper, this election, this dataset. Snyder's instruction is to pick the institution you are part of or close to, and become its partisan — to take responsibility for the specific thing within your reach rather than dissipating concern across an abstraction you cannot affect.

The reason institutions need defending early is the cost curve. In the early phase of erosion, defending an institution is relatively cheap and highly effective, because the institution still has independence and standing. In the late phase, after capture, the same defence is dangerous and often futile, because the institution that would have protected the defender has already been hollowed. The person who defends the court before it is packed has leverage; the person who waits until after has none. This is why anticipatory obedience is so destructive and why early, particular institutional defence is so valuable: it is the leverage point, and the leverage decays with time.

"Corporeal politics" addresses the medium of resistance. Snyder argues that tyranny depends on isolation — on citizens who are atomised, who know politics only through screens, who never act in physical concert with others. The screen is a place of passive consumption and of surveillance; it can simulate participation while producing none. Real political action has a bodily, social, in-person dimension: showing up to a meeting, a protest, a polling station; making eye contact and small talk so that the social fabric holds; getting into unfamiliar places among unfamiliar people so that solidarity across difference becomes possible. These embodied acts matter because they rebuild the connective tissue that authoritarianism works to dissolve, and because they are harder to ignore, suppress, or fake than a click. A thousand bodies in a square is a fact in the world; a thousand likes is a metric.

This connects to the broader theme of solidarity and the building of relationships before they are needed. Snyder emphasises "make eye contact and small talk" not as etiquette but as infrastructure: the networks of trust and recognition that let people act together in a crisis are built in ordinary times, through ordinary contact. A society of strangers is easy to rule; a society where people know and can rely on one another is not. Corporeal politics is, in part, the patient maintenance of that fabric.

For a working professional, the lessons are concrete and immediate. The institution to defend is usually the one you are responsible for — your firm's standards, your professional association, the integrity of the evaluations or audits or research your work produces, the independence of the body you sit on. The corporeal politics is the choice to show up, to be visible, to act with named others rather than retreating into private opinion or online gesture. The antidote to paralysis is always particular: not "what can be done about all this?" but "what is the specific institution within my reach, and what is the specific act that defends it this week?"

## How to Apply
1. **Pick the particular institution.** Identify the specific court, press body, association, election, or record within reach — not "democracy" in general.
2. **Defend it early.** Name how much cheaper and more effective defence is now than after capture.
3. **Choose embodied action over gesture.** Show up in person; act with named others; do not let the screen substitute for presence.
4. **Maintain the social fabric in ordinary times.** Build the relationships of trust before the crisis that will require them.
5. **Convert paralysis to a specific act.** Replace "what can anyone do?" with "what is my institution and my act?"

## Examples
**Situation:** A researcher feels overwhelmed by national-level democratic decline and unsure any individual action matters.
**Application:** The antidote is particularity. The national abstraction is not actionable; the specific institution is. What body, standard, or record is within this person's reach — a professional ethics committee, the integrity of their own published work, a local electoral observation effort? Defend that, early, with others.
**Result:** Paralysis converts into a concrete, leverageable act.

**Situation:** A group wants to "raise awareness online" about an institutional capture in progress.
**Application:** Online awareness is not nothing, but it is not corporeal politics and can substitute for it. Ask what embodied act accompanies it — showing up to the hearing, organising the affected professionals in person, physical presence at the body under threat. The screen alone is the medium of isolation the tactic should be countering.
**Result:** The action gains a bodily, social dimension that has real weight.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Tell people to "defend democracy" in the abstract.
**Why:** Abstraction is unactionable; defence must be of a particular institution within reach.

**Don't:** Treat online gesture as a substitute for embodied action.
**Why:** The screen is the medium of isolation; tyranny thrives on it. Presence is what has weight.

**Don't:** Wait to defend an institution until the threat is undeniable.
**Why:** By then the institution that would have given the defender leverage has been captured.
