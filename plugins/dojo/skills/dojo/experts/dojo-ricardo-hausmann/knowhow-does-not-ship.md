# Knowhow Does Not Ship

## When to Use
- When training or technology transfer is proposed as the route to a missing capability.
- When a strategy assumes capability will follow investment.
- As the explanation for *why* diversification is local.

## Fails When
- **The capability is genuinely codified** — a procedure, a standard, a piece of software. Some things do ship. The framework is about the things that do not.

## Core Concept

**Three properties of knowhow, and each one has a policy consequence.**

**1. It is tacit.** It cannot be fully written down. The person who knows how to run the line has knowledge that does not survive being put in a manual — which is why the manual exists and the line still fails without them.

**2. It is collective.** No individual holds enough of it to make anything complex. It exists only in the **combination** of many people, each holding a fragment, arranged so the fragments interlock. This is the property people miss. It means you cannot acquire the knowhow by hiring one expert; you need the arrangement.

**3. It lives in brains.** Not in the machine, the manual, the patent or the licence.

**Therefore: it does not ship.** You cannot download it, buy it in a container, or read it into a place. **It moves when the people carrying it move**, and not otherwise.

**Why training does not substitute.** Training transfers **know-what** and **know-why** — codified knowledge, the kind that fits in a curriculum. The binding input is tacit collective knowhow, which is not in the curriculum. A trained workforce with no experienced core is a workforce that knows the principles and cannot make the thing. (Lundvall's four knows are the same distinction from the other side: know-how and know-who are the tacit pair.)

**Why technology transfer does not substitute.** It transfers an artefact. **Sutton's version of this is the sharpest: if you don't start out with a firm that has the appropriate capabilities, installing capital equipment won't help.** The machine does not contain the knowhow. The history of technology transfer is largely the history of discovering this repeatedly.

**The convergence worth noting.** Three of your experts arrive here independently: **Hausmann** (knowhow is tacit and collective, so it moves with people), **Sutton** (capability is revealed and historically acquired; the equipment is downstream), and **Lundvall** (the DUI mode generates tacit know-how through doing and interacting). Different literatures, same finding. **When three independent traditions converge, the claim is unusually safe to build on.**

**What follows for policy — and it is unwelcome.** If knowhow moves only with people, then the fastest capability acquisition channel is **moving brains**: attracting people who hold it, or sending people to where it is held and getting them back. That makes migration and diaspora policy an industrial policy instrument. See `people-as-the-transmission-mechanism.md`. Almost no strategy accepts this, because attracting foreign talent is politically hard in a way that buying machines is not — and machines are available.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask where the knowhow is, in whose brains.** Not "how do we build capability" but "who currently has it".
2. **Test the training answer.** Does the curriculum contain the tacit part? It cannot.
3. **Test the equipment answer.** Would the firm be able to use it? If not, it is a monument.
4. **Look for the combination, not the individual.** One expert does not carry a collective capability.
5. **Follow the convergence.** Sutton and Lundvall agree from different directions; use all three.
6. **Accept the migration implication or say why not.** It is the mechanism.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy proposes to build a provincial capability in a new manufacturing sub-sector through equipment grants and a skills programme.

**Application:** Both instruments transfer the wrong thing. The equipment is an artefact; the skills programme delivers know-what and know-why. The binding input — tacit, collective knowhow held by experienced people who have worked together — is transferred by neither. The diagnostic question is: **who, anywhere, currently holds this knowhow, and can any of them be brought here?** Answers might include: a multinational already operating in the sector (Sutton's FDI-as-capability-channel), returning diaspora with sector experience, or foreign managers. If the honest answer is that nobody reachable holds it, the sub-sector is not adjacent and the strategy is proposing a jump.

**Result:** A capability plan built on the mechanism that actually transfers capability.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Answer "how will we get the capability?" with "training".
**Why:** Training moves know-what. The missing thing is tacit and collective.

**Don't:** Assume the equipment contains the capability.
**Why:** It does not. Sutton's Tanzanian factory is the canonical demonstration.

**Don't:** Try to acquire a collective capability by hiring one expert.
**Why:** It exists in the arrangement of many people, not in any of them.
