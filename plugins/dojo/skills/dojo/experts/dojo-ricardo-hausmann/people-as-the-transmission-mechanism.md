# People as the Transmission Mechanism

## When to Use
- When asking how to acquire a capability the place does not have.
- When skills, migration or diaspora policy is being designed.
- When an industrial strategy has no people-movement component — which is most of them.

## Fails When
- **The capability already exists locally.** Then the question is coordination or finance, not transmission.

## Core Concept

**The syllogism is short and its conclusion is politically unwelcome.**

1. Prosperity is collective knowhow.
2. Knowhow is tacit and collective and lives in brains.
3. Therefore knowhow moves **only when the people carrying it move.**
4. Therefore **moving people is the fastest capability acquisition channel available.**
5. Therefore **migration and diaspora policy is industrial policy.**

**The three channels:**

**Inward.** Attract people who already hold the knowhow — foreign managers, skilled migrants, experienced operators, technical staff of a multinational. Note the convergence with **Sutton**: his Enterprise Map finding that the FDI stock could generate as many jobs as the entire domestic industrial frontier is the same mechanism observed from the outside. An FDI project is, among other things, **a delivery of brains.**

**Return.** The **diaspora** — nationals abroad who have accumulated knowhow in other economies and could bring it back. This is the cheapest channel and the most neglected. They already have the language, the networks and the reason to come.

**Outward-and-back.** Send people to where the knowhow is held and get them back. Slow, and it works.

**Why almost nobody does it.** Attracting foreign talent is politically difficult in a way that buying machines is not. Machines are available, procurable, photographable and uncontroversial. So machines get bought, and the capability does not arrive, and the conclusion drawn is that more machines are needed.

**The collective property complicates it.** Because knowhow is collective, **one expert is not enough** — you need enough people to constitute the arrangement. This is why a single returning specialist often achieves little and a multinational's arrival achieves a lot: the multinational brings a *team* that already knows how to work together. **Capability arrives in groups.**

**For a provincial strategy, specifically.** A province does not set immigration policy — that is national, and it is a framework condition in Stam's sense. But a province can: run a **diaspora programme**; make itself attractive to firms that will bring teams; support **returning nationals**; and use its investment promotion function to attract not investment volume but **capability content** — which is precisely Sutton's argument about reading the FDI register as a capability inventory rather than a finance statistic.

**The honest tension.** This sits uncomfortably beside an employment-focused mandate. A strategy whose purpose is jobs for local people, told that the fastest capability channel is bringing in foreigners, has a real political problem and it should not be waved away. **The resolution is that capability arriving in a few brains creates employment for many** — but that is an argument to be made, not assumed, and it should be made explicitly rather than smuggled in.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask where the knowhow is, in whose brains, and whether they would come.** That is the capability plan.
2. **Read the FDI register as a capability inventory.** Who is here, what do they know, who could learn from them?
3. **Build the diaspora channel.** Cheapest, most neglected, already motivated.
4. **Think in teams, not individuals.** Capability is collective; it arrives in groups.
5. **Accept that migration is national** — and find the provincial levers: attraction, return, investment promotion for capability content.
6. **Make the political argument explicitly.** Few brains in, many jobs out. Do not smuggle it.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy's skills chapter proposes to build capability in a target sub-sector through training and bursaries.

**Application:** Training delivers know-what and know-why. The missing input is tacit collective knowhow, which no bursary transmits. The question the chapter does not ask: **who currently holds this knowhow, and can any of them be brought here?** Candidates: a multinational in the sub-sector (bring the firm, get the team); Western Cape diaspora with relevant sector experience abroad; experienced foreign operators. Then the training makes sense in a way it did not before — **as the complement to an experienced core**, giving the trained people someone to learn the tacit part from. Training plus an experienced core is a capability plan. Training alone is a bursary scheme with a capability label.

**Result:** A skills chapter with a transmission mechanism attached to it.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Build capability through training alone.
**Why:** No tacit transmission. The trained workforce knows the principles and cannot make the thing.

**Don't:** Import one expert and expect a capability.
**Why:** It is collective. It arrives in teams.

**Don't:** Smuggle the migration argument.
**Why:** It sits badly with an employment mandate. Make the case — few brains in, many jobs out — explicitly or not at all.
