## THE EVIDENCE

### The instrument and the source
- **Study:** Hausmann, R., Hidalgo, C. A., Bustos, S., Coscia, M., Simoes, A. & Yildirim, M. A., *The Atlas of Economic Complexity: Mapping Paths to Prosperity* (MIT Press, 2011; 2nd edn 2013). Live at **atlas.hks.harvard.edu**. **Version 10.0 released 2025**, featuring a new Product Space visualisation, updated global trade data, and additional country profiles.
- **What it does:** Attempts to **measure the amount of productive knowledge countries hold** and how they can accumulate more by making more complex products. Locates each country in the **product space**; provides complexity and growth-potential rankings; offers country pages detailing current capabilities and diversification options.
- **Finding:** The **Economic Complexity Index (ECI)** captures the **diversity and sophistication of the productive capabilities embedded in a country's exports**.
- **Caveat:** **It is inferred from trade data.** Complexity is not observed directly; it is deduced from the pattern of who exports what. That is elegant and it is a real limitation — non-traded activity, services, and informal production are largely invisible to it. For a provincial economy with a large services and informal component, this bites.

### Complexity explains income
- **Study:** As above.
- **Finding:** The ECI **closely explains differences in country incomes**. Reported in the Atlas: for countries where **natural resource exports are below 10% of GDP**, the ECI accounts for **78% of the variance** in income per capita `[CHECK]`.
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]` on the 78% figure — read from the Atlas text and **must be verified before use**. Note the conditioning: the relationship is reported for **non-resource-intensive** countries. Countries with high natural resource exports **tend to be significantly richer than their complexity would predict** — though the ECI still correlates with income within that group. **South Africa's resource position makes this caveat directly relevant.**

### Complexity predicts growth
- **Study:** Hausmann et al. (2014 edn) and subsequent Growth Lab work; Chacua, C. & Hartog, M. (2026), "Complexity: Hausmann-Hidalgo Economic Complexity", Growth Lab Working Paper (Harvard Kennedy School). `[CHECK]` — working paper number unverified.
- **Finding:** The **ECI robustly predicts future economic growth**, and **often outperforms traditional predictors of growth such as educational attainment or institutional quality**. The predictive power is most visible in **the gap between a country's complexity and its income**: in the early 2000s **China, Vietnam and Poland** exhibited ECI levels well above what their per capita income predicted, and each subsequently experienced sustained growth.
- **Caveat:** The complexity–income gap is the operative variable, not the level. And this is a **prediction claim on a cross-country panel**, not a causal claim that raising measured complexity raises growth. Do not present it as the latter.

### The rankings
- **Study:** Growth Lab, Atlas releases.
- **Finding:** The most complex countries reported as: **Japan, South Korea, Switzerland, Singapore, Taiwan, Germany**. Recent rankings find that **places making gains in complexity, led by East and Southeast Asia, will lead growth in the coming decade**.
- **Caveat:** **Date every ranking.** The Atlas is versioned and the data updates; a ranking quoted without a data year is not a citation.

### The capability mapping — the newest instrument
- **Study:** **Innovation Capabilities Navigator**, an interactive dashboard launched by **WIPO** and **Harvard's Growth Lab**. `[CHECK]` — launch date unverified.
- **Finding:** Maps **over 2,500 capabilities across 193 economies**.
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]`. **This is the most directly relevant recent instrument for a capability-based provincial strategy and it should be examined directly** — it moves the framework from products toward capabilities, which is closer to what an MSME strategy needs.

### The sub-national question — the live one for you
- **Study:** Growth Lab sub-national atlases, e.g. **Colombia Atlas of Economic Complexity (Datlas), 2014**. `[CHECK]` — coverage and method unverified.
- **Finding:** The Growth Lab has built sub-national complexity tools.
- **Caveat:** **This is the load-bearing uncertainty for a Western Cape application.** The ECI is built on international trade data. A province does not have a trade account with the rest of its own country, so sub-national complexity requires a different data source — typically employment or firm-level data — and different methods. **Before proposing a Western Cape complexity analysis, establish what data would support it.** Do not assume the national method scales down.

## How to Apply

1. **Date every figure.** The Atlas is versioned.
2. **State the resource caveat** in a South African document. Resource exporters are richer than complexity predicts, and the 78% figure `[CHECK]` is conditioned on non-resource-intensive countries.
3. **Use the gap, not the level.** Complexity above income predicts growth.
4. **Do not claim causality.** Predictive power on a panel is not a claim that raising the index raises growth.
5. **Establish the sub-national data question first.** A province has no trade account. This is the first thing to resolve, not an implementation detail.
6. **Look at the Innovation Capabilities Navigator.** `[CHECK]` — but it is the closest thing to what you need.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy proposes to commission a Western Cape economic complexity analysis to guide value chain selection.

**Application:** Sound instinct, but resolve the method question before the procurement. The ECI is inferred from **international trade data** — a country's export basket reveals its knowhow. A province has no separate trade account with the rest of South Africa, so the national method does not scale down directly; sub-national work uses employment or firm-level data and different techniques. The Growth Lab has built sub-national atlases (Colombia's Datlas) `[CHECK]`, so it is possible — but the data foundation is the whole question. Second: a large share of Western Cape economic activity is **services and informal production**, both of which trade-based complexity sees poorly. Expect the analysis to describe the tradeable formal goods economy well and the rest badly, and scope the claims accordingly.

**Result:** A complexity analysis with a resolved data foundation and honestly bounded claims.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Quote an ECI ranking without a date.
**Why:** The Atlas is versioned and updates. An undated ranking is not a citation.

**Don't:** Present complexity as a causal lever.
**Why:** It predicts growth on a panel. It is not a demonstration that raising the index raises income.

**Don't:** Assume the national method scales to a province.
**Why:** No sub-national trade account. Different data, different method — resolve it first.
