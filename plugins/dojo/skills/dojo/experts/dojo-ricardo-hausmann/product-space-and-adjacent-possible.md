# The Product Space and the Adjacent Possible

## When to Use
- Whenever sectors, products or value chains are being selected.
- When the list contains what wealthy countries currently make.
- When a strategy proposes becoming a "hub" for something.

## Fails When
- **The targets were derived from adjacency already.** Then engage the derivation.

## Core Concept

**Countries do not diversify at random. They move into products that are *near* the ones they already make** — near in the specific sense of **requiring similar collective knowhow.**

**The product space** is the map: a network showing the **proximity between economic activities**, inferred from the observed pattern of which activities co-occur in the same economies. If two products are made by the same set of countries, they probably need similar knowhow, so they are close. The map is empirical, not theoretical.

**The adjacent possible** is the set of activities near what a place already makes. That is the derived candidate list.

**Why adjacency is not modesty.** This is the part that gets misread. Adjacency matters because **it makes the missing knowhow small enough to acquire.** If you already make most of the fragments a product needs, you need a few more, and a few more is achievable. If you propose to jump somewhere distant, you need hundreds of fragments you do not have, held by people who are not here — and the jump does not happen, regardless of the subsidy. The constraint is not ambition. It is that knowhow is tacit and collective and does not ship (see `knowhow-does-not-ship.md`).

**Derivation versus ambition — the diagnostic.** Ask of any sector list: **how was each item derived?**

| Produced by | Contains | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Stakeholder consultation | What participants would like to grow | Ambition list |
| Benchmarking the frontier | What rich countries make now | Ambition list — reflects *their* knowhow |
| Growth rates / employment share | What you already do most of | Describes the present |
| **Product space adjacency** | **What is near what you already make** | **Derived** |

**The derivation, concretely.** Take the actual export basket at fine product granularity. That reveals what the place knows. Consult the product space: which activities are proximate? That set is the candidate list — **less exciting than the current list and far more achievable.**

**Then three questions per candidate:**
1. **Where is the missing knowhow, in whose brains, and would they come?** If nowhere reachable, it is not adjacent whatever the map says.
2. **Is it chicken-and-egg?** Then it needs a coordinator, not a subsidy.
3. **Is there a self-discovery externality?** Then a bounded, time-limited pioneer incentive is right.

**The honest limits.** The map is inferred from **trade data** — so it sees tradeable goods well, services poorly, and informal production essentially not at all. It is built at **country** scale; a province has no separate trade account. And it is a map of proximity, **not a prohibition**: distant jumps happen, occasionally, usually when a large number of brains arrive at once. Do not present the product space as deterministic.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask how the list was derived.** That question does most of the work.
2. **Start from the export basket**, at fine granularity, not from the sector taxonomy.
3. **Derive the candidates from adjacency.** Expect a less exciting and more achievable list.
4. **Run the three questions** on each candidate.
5. **Bound the claim.** Tradeable goods yes; services and informal, poorly.
6. **Do not treat the map as deterministic.** Distant jumps happen when brains arrive.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial strategy lists eight priority sectors, produced through stakeholder consultation, including advanced manufacturing and biotechnology.

**Application:** Ask the derivation question. Consultation selects for what participants want, and what participants want is what wealthy economies currently make — which reflects *their* accumulated knowhow at a distance from yours that is exactly the distance you cannot cross. Re-derive: take the province's actual export basket at product granularity; identify what is proximate in the product space; that is the candidate set. Then per candidate, ask where the missing knowhow is and whether those brains can be brought. Expect the derived list to overlap partially with the current one — agro-processing adjacencies will survive, advanced manufacturing probably will not — and to contain two or three items nobody proposed, which is the point of deriving rather than wishing.

**Result:** A sector list with a derivation instead of a provenance.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Target what rich countries make now.
**Why:** That reflects their knowhow. The distance is the thing you cannot cross.

**Don't:** Read adjacency as a counsel of modesty.
**Why:** It is about the missing knowhow being small enough to acquire. It is a constraint, not a preference.

**Don't:** Apply the product space to services or informal production.
**Why:** It is inferred from goods trade data. It cannot see them.
