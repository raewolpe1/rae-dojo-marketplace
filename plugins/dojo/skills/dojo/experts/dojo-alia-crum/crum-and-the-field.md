# Crum and the Field

## When to Use
- When positioning Crum's work relative to Dweck, Yeager, Kabat-Zinn, or other researchers.
- When addressing critiques of mindset-physiology research.
- When someone asks "how does this relate to X?" about adjacent bodies of work.

## Core Concept
Crum occupies a distinctive position in the field. She took Dweck's "mindset" construct — which was primarily about learning and achievement — and pushed it into the body. Where Dweck asks "what do you believe about intelligence?", Crum asks "what do you believe about stress, food, and exercise, and how do those beliefs change your hormones, cardiovascular function, and metabolism?" This is a genuine extension, not a simple application.

**Relation to Carol Dweck.** Dweck established the mindset construct and its effects on learning, motivation, and achievement. Crum extended it into physiology, demonstrating that mindsets about experiences beyond the classroom — stress, food, exercise — produce measurable biological changes. The two are complementary: Dweck shows that mindsets shape psychological and behavioural outcomes; Crum shows they shape physiological outcomes. The implication of Crum's work is that Dweck's construct is even more powerful than originally theorised — it operates not just in the mind but in the body.

**Relation to David Yeager.** Yeager and Crum are deeply complementary. Yeager's synergistic mindsets research (Nature, 2022) integrates Crum's stress-is-enhancing mindset with Dweck's growth mindset, showing that the combination protects adolescents from stress more than either alone. Yeager focuses on scaling interventions and testing moderation; Crum focuses on physiological mechanisms. Together they represent the most complete current picture of how mindsets operate across psychological and biological levels.

**Relation to the placebo literature.** Crum's work extends and reframes the placebo research tradition. Classical placebo research treats expectation effects as confounds. Crum treats them as windows into the causal power of beliefs. Her contribution is to move expectation effects from the clinical trial context (does this drug work?) into everyday experience (how do beliefs about food, stress, and exercise change the body's response?). She should be understood as building on Fabrizio Benedetti's neuroscience of placebo, not as inventing the field from scratch.

**Relation to Jon Kabat-Zinn.** Kabat-Zinn's mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) addresses the subjective experience of stress through attention and awareness practices. Crum's approach is different: she targets the cognitive appraisal of stress rather than the attentional relationship to it. MBSR says: "observe your stress without judgement." Crum says: "change what you believe about your stress." Both produce measurable physiological changes, but through different mechanisms — attentional regulation versus cognitive reappraisal.

**Critiques.** The primary critique is generalisability: much of the research has been conducted with relatively small samples in controlled settings. The housekeeping study had 84 participants. The milkshake study had 46. While the effects are physiologically measured and the mechanisms are plausible, the question of whether these effects operate at scale, across diverse populations, and in real-world conditions remains partly open. A second critique concerns the boundary between mindset effects and genuine positive thinking: critics argue that the distinction between "accurate reappraisal" and "wishful thinking" is harder to maintain in practice than in theory. A third critique — the structural one — has persistent force: mindset interventions risk individualising systemic problems, suggesting that workers, patients, or students should think differently rather than that conditions should change.

**Evidence base.** The specific studies — milkshake, housekeeping, stress mindset — are well-designed, published in high-quality journals, and use physiological outcome measures that strengthen causal claims. The broader framework — that mindsets about all experiences shape the body's response to those experiences — is well-supported by the pattern of evidence but less directly tested as a universal claim. The honest assessment: the specific findings are robust; the general principle is well-reasoned but still being tested at its boundaries.

## Examples
**Situation:** A colleague says: "Crum's work is basically just the placebo effect rebranded. How is this different from what we've known for decades?"
**Application:** Crum would say: "The placebo literature established that expectations about a treatment change the treatment's effects. That is important work, and I build on it. But the classical placebo framework treats expectation as a confound — something to control for in order to isolate the 'real' treatment effect. My work reframes it: the expectation is not a confound, it is a mechanism. And crucially, it extends beyond the clinical context. The milkshake study is not about a medical treatment. It is about food. The housekeeping study is about daily activity. The stress research is about an everyday experience. What I am showing is that the placebo mechanism — expectations shaping biology — operates not just in the doctor's office but in every experience where appraisal mediates the body's response. The contribution is not the discovery that expectations matter. It is the demonstration that they matter far more broadly than the clinical placebo literature had shown, and that they operate through identifiable hormonal and cardiovascular pathways in everyday life."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present Crum as having invented the mind-body connection.
**Why:** The placebo literature, psychoneuroimmunology, and stress physiology all precede Crum's work. Her contribution is extension and reframing, not invention. Overstating novelty invites legitimate criticism.

**Don't:** Dismiss critiques about sample size and generalisability.
**Why:** The concern is valid. The foundational studies are well-designed but small. Larger-scale replications and real-world tests are still needed. Engage the critique honestly rather than defensively.
