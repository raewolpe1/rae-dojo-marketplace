# Crum and Measurement

## When to Use
- When designing evaluations or research studies that need to capture mindset effects.
- When choosing between subjective and objective outcome measures for mindset interventions.
- When someone asks how to demonstrate that a mindset shift produced a biological change.

## Core Concept
Measurement is where Crum's research gains its distinctive credibility. The milkshake study did not rely on participants reporting that they felt more full. It measured ghrelin — a gut peptide. The housekeeping study did not rely on housekeepers reporting that they felt healthier. It measured weight, blood pressure, and body fat percentage. The stress mindset research measures cortisol profiles and cardiovascular response patterns, not just self-reported stress levels.

This commitment to physiological outcome measures is strategic. It addresses the most common critique of mindset research — "it's all subjective" — by demonstrating effects in biological systems that are not under conscious control. You cannot will your ghrelin to decline. You cannot consciously manipulate your cortisol profile. When these measures change in response to a belief manipulation, the effect cannot be dismissed as demand characteristics or social desirability.

For practitioners designing evaluations of mindset-informed programmes, the implication is that measurement strategy must match the claim. If the claim is that a stress mindset intervention improves health, measuring only self-reported stress is insufficient. Physiological markers — blood pressure, cortisol, heart rate variability, inflammatory markers — provide stronger evidence. If physiological measures are impractical, behavioural measures (health-seeking behaviour, medication adherence, performance metrics) provide intermediate evidence. Self-report measures of mindset are useful for confirming that the intervention actually shifted the target belief, but they are not sufficient evidence of downstream effects.

However, self-report measures have a legitimate role that should not be dismissed. Subjective experience — how people feel about their stress, their health, their work — matters intrinsically, not just as a pathway to physiological outcomes. The measurement strategy should include both: self-report to confirm the mindset shift, and objective measures to demonstrate downstream effects.

## How to Apply
1. **Match measures to claims.** "If you claim your programme changes physiological outcomes, measure physiological outcomes. If you claim it changes subjective experience, self-report is appropriate. The measure must match the claim."
2. **Include the mediator.** "Measure the mindset itself — the target belief — as well as the outcome. This lets you test whether the mindset actually shifted and whether the shift mediates the outcome change."
3. **Use multiple levels.** "Ideal design: self-report of mindset (did the belief shift?), behavioural measures (did behaviour change?), physiological measures (did biology change?). Each level strengthens the evidence."
4. **Design for timing.** "Mindset effects may operate on different timescales for different outcomes. Hormonal responses can shift immediately (milkshake study). Body composition changes take weeks (housekeeping study). Design measurement points to match the expected timescale of the effect."

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluator is designing a study of a workplace stress intervention that includes a mindset component. She asks: "What should I measure to show the mindset part is working?"
**Application:** Crum would say: "Three levels. First, measure the mindset itself — use the Stress Mindset Measure (SMM), which I developed specifically for this purpose. Administer it before and after the intervention. If the score does not shift, the intervention did not change the belief, and any outcome changes are not attributable to the mindset component. Second, measure physiological stress responses. If you have access to salivary cortisol or heart rate variability monitoring, use them — they are the gold standard for demonstrating that a mindset shift produced a biological change. If you do not, measure blood pressure and resting heart rate as cheaper proxies. Third, measure performance and behavioural outcomes: sick days, self-reported engagement, performance ratings. Then run mediation analysis: did the intervention shift the mindset, and did the mindset shift mediate the outcome change? Without this chain, you cannot attribute the outcome to the mindset component specifically. And be honest about what you find. If the mindset shifted but outcomes did not, you have found a boundary. That is valuable information."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Measure only self-report and claim physiological effects.
**Why:** Self-report of feeling less stressed is not evidence that cortisol profiles changed. The credibility of Crum's work rests on physiological measures. Evaluators who measure only self-report and claim biological effects are overstating their evidence.

**Don't:** Dismiss self-report as meaningless.
**Why:** Subjective experience matters. How people feel about their stress is important independently of their cortisol levels. The point is not that self-report is worthless but that it is insufficient evidence for biological claims.
