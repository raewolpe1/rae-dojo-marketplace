# Crum and Ethics

## When to Use
- When someone raises ethical concerns about changing people's beliefs without their full awareness.
- When designing mindset interventions and needing to navigate the ethics of persuasion versus manipulation.
- When discussing the power dynamics inherent in mindset research and practice.

## Core Concept
The same mechanisms that make mindset interventions powerful make them ethically complex. If beliefs about stress change cortisol profiles, then changing someone's beliefs about stress changes their physiology. This raises questions that the field must confront rather than avoid.

The first ethical dimension is consent. In research, participants consent to studies but may not fully understand that their beliefs are the target of the manipulation. In practice — a manager reframing stress for a team, a programme relabelling food, a health worker installing expectations about treatment — there is often no formal consent process at all. The question is whether changing someone's mindset without their explicit awareness constitutes manipulation.

The second dimension is accuracy. Crum's framework provides a partial answer: if the mindset shift is accurate — if stress genuinely has enhancing properties, if the housekeepers' work genuinely qualified as exercise — then the intervention is correcting a misconception, not installing a false belief. The ethical weight is lighter when the shift directs attention to real but overlooked aspects of experience. It would be heavier if the shift required people to believe something false.

The third dimension is power. Who decides which mindsets are "accurate" and should be installed? An employer who uses stress mindset research to justify overwork is exercising power through psychological framing. A government that uses food mindset research to avoid addressing food insecurity is using science to deflect structural responsibility. The question of who benefits from the mindset shift — and who bears the costs if the shift proves inadequate — is irreducibly ethical.

The fourth dimension is the alternative. If the current information environment is already installing mindsets — and it is, constantly — then the question is not whether to shape beliefs but whether to do so accurately and transparently rather than leaving it to media, culture, and commercial incentives that may not have people's wellbeing as their primary concern.

## How to Apply
1. **Default to transparency.** "When possible, share the science openly. 'Research shows that beliefs about stress affect your body's response. We are going to share the evidence and let you decide how to use it.' Transparent mindset education is ethically cleanest."
2. **Ensure accuracy.** "Only design interventions around mindset shifts that are genuinely accurate. If the stress in your organisation is genuinely unsustainable, a stress-is-enhancing intervention is not accurate — it is gaslighting."
3. **Check the power dynamics.** "Who benefits from this mindset shift? If the primary beneficiary is the institution (lower healthcare costs, higher productivity) rather than the individual (better health, better experience), the ethical calculus shifts."
4. **Provide structural support alongside.** "The ethical obligation is not just to change beliefs but to ensure that the conditions match the beliefs. If you tell people stress is enhancing, you are ethically obligated to ensure the stress is manageable."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme designer asks: "Is it ethical to design our youth programme to shift participants' stress mindsets without explicitly telling them that's what we're doing?"
**Application:** Crum would say: "The ethics depend on three things. First, is the mindset shift accurate? If your programme involves genuine challenge — demanding but achievable — and you frame that challenge as the body preparing to grow, you are telling the truth. The ethical weight is light. If your programme involves exploitative conditions and you reframe them as 'growth opportunities,' you are lying. The ethical weight is heavy. Second, would transparency undermine the intervention? In some cases, explaining 'we are trying to change your beliefs about stress' creates a reactance that prevents the shift. In that case, embedding the accurate frame naturally — through how you describe the programme, how you respond to difficulty, how you model stress responses — may be more effective and is not deceptive as long as the frame is accurate. Third, who benefits? If participants benefit through better health, performance, and experience, the intervention serves their interests. If the institution benefits primarily through reduced complaints and higher productivity while participants bear unsustainable conditions, the intervention serves the institution at the participant's expense. My recommendation: be as transparent as possible while maintaining effectiveness. Share the science. Let people engage with it knowingly. And ensure the conditions warrant the mindset you are trying to create."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss ethical concerns as theoretical.
**Why:** The power to change beliefs that change physiology is real power. It can be used well or badly. Treating the ethical dimension as an afterthought is irresponsible.

**Don't:** Assume accuracy resolves all ethical questions.
**Why:** Even accurate mindset shifts raise questions about autonomy and informed consent. Accuracy is necessary but not sufficient for ethical practice.
