# Social Mindsets

## When to Use
- When examining how mindsets about stress, health, food, or exercise are transmitted through culture, media, and institutions.
- When designing communications that will shape population-level mindsets.
- When understanding why a group shares a particular harmful or helpful belief about an experience.

## Core Concept
Mindsets are not purely individual. They are shaped by the information environment: what media communicates about stress, what health campaigns say about diet and exercise, what managers tell their teams about pressure, what cultures teach about suffering and resilience. Crum's work has increasingly addressed how these social channels install mindsets in populations, often without anyone intending to do so.

The cultural narrative about stress in Western industrialised societies is overwhelmingly debilitating: stress is toxic, stress kills, stress must be reduced. This narrative is transmitted through news media, wellness marketing, workplace communications, and public health messaging. It installs the stress-is-debilitating mindset in entire populations, with measurable physiological consequences. The irony is that the public health messaging designed to protect people from stress may be contributing to the harm by installing the mindset that makes stress more harmful.

Similarly, food culture transmits mindsets about what eating means. "Guilt-free" labels, "cheat day" language, and the moral framing of food as virtuous or sinful all install mindsets that shape metabolic responses. Exercise culture transmits mindsets about what "counts" as activity — and by defining exercise narrowly as structured gym workouts, it devalues the physical activity most people already do.

The practical implication is that any intervention designer, programme manager, or communicator is a mindset installer — whether they intend to be or not. The question is not whether you are shaping mindsets but whether you are doing so accurately and constructively.

## How to Apply
1. **Audit the information environment.** "Before designing an intervention, map the mindset messages the target population already receives about the experience. What do they believe about stress? Where did that belief come from?"
2. **Design messaging as intervention.** "Your programme communications are not packaging around the 'real' intervention. They are part of the intervention. Craft them with the same rigour you bring to the programme design itself."
3. **Correct cultural narratives with accuracy.** "The stress-is-debilitating narrative is incomplete, not wrong. Stress can be debilitating. But the narrative excludes the enhancing properties. Correct by completing, not by denying the harmful aspects."
4. **Consider the messenger.** "Who delivers the mindset message matters. A manager who says 'stress is part of growth' while visibly modelling burnout sends a contradictory message. The visible behaviour of respected figures shapes group mindsets more powerfully than formal communications."

## Examples
**Situation:** A development organisation notices that its staff across multiple country offices share a pervasive belief that "this work will burn you out eventually — it's just a matter of time." New staff absorb this narrative within months of joining.
**Application:** Crum would say: "You are seeing social mindset transmission in action. The 'burnout is inevitable' narrative is being installed through informal channels — stories senior staff tell, the way departures are narrated, the visible exhaustion that is worn as a badge of commitment. This narrative is not neutral. It installs the stress-is-debilitating mindset across your organisation, which produces the very cortisol profiles, cardiovascular responses, and cognitive narrowing that accelerate burnout. To change this, you need to address the narrative, not just the workload. First, ensure the workload is actually manageable — you cannot reappraise your way out of genuinely unsustainable conditions. Second, change the stories. Find people who have sustained long careers in development work and are thriving — they exist. Make their narratives visible. Not as propaganda, but as accurate counter-examples to the 'burnout is inevitable' story. Third, train your managers. Every time a manager says 'I know this is burning you out,' they are reinforcing the debilitating mindset. Train them to say instead: 'This work is demanding, and the energy you feel is your body rising to meet something important.'"

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat social mindset transmission as conspiracy.
**Why:** No one decided to install the stress-is-debilitating mindset in Western culture. It emerged from well-intentioned health messaging, media incentives toward alarming stories, and a cultural tendency to frame suffering as purely negative. Understanding the mechanism does not require identifying a villain.

**Don't:** Blame individuals for absorbing cultural mindsets.
**Why:** If the entire information environment communicates that stress kills, it is rational for individuals to believe it. The intervention is in the information environment, not in the individual's failure to resist it.
