# Subjective Experience

## When to Use
- When discussing the philosophical and practical implications of Crum's research for how we understand reality.
- When someone asks whether "it's all in your head" or whether objective conditions are what really matter.
- When addressing the deeper question of what Crum's research means for science, medicine, and social programmes.

## Core Concept
The conventional model in science and medicine treats subjective experience as a secondary phenomenon — a reflection of objective reality that may be more or less accurate but is not itself causally significant. Objective conditions (nutritional content, exercise intensity, stressor magnitude) produce objective outcomes, and subjective experience is either a reliable report of those conditions or a distortion to be corrected.

Crum's research challenges this model at its foundation. The milkshake study shows that the body's metabolic response to food is not determined solely by the food's objective nutritional content — the subjective appraisal is part of the metabolic response. The housekeeping study shows that the health benefits of physical activity are not determined solely by the activity's objective intensity — the subjective perception is part of the mechanism. The stress research shows that the physiological impact of a stressor is not determined solely by the stressor's objective magnitude — the subjective mindset is part of the cascade.

Crum's position is not naive subjectivism — not "reality is whatever you believe it is." The milkshake had 380 calories regardless of belief. The housekeepers' work involved the same physical demands regardless of perception. The stressor existed regardless of mindset. But the body's response to these objective conditions was modulated by subjective appraisal. Subjective experience is not a substitute for objective reality. It is an additional causal force that interacts with objective conditions to produce outcomes.

This has profound implications for programme design and evaluation. It means that the objective components of an intervention (the medication, the exercise regimen, the training curriculum) and the subjective components (what participants believe about the intervention, what it means to them, what they expect) are both part of the treatment. Measuring only the objective components captures only part of the causal picture.

## How to Apply
1. **Treat subjective experience as data, not noise.** "When participants report on their experience of a programme — what they expected, what it meant to them, how they perceived the demands — these reports are causal data, not confounds to be controlled for."
2. **Design for both dimensions.** "An effective programme addresses both the objective conditions (adequate resources, sound design) and the subjective conditions (constructive expectations, accurate framing, meaningful participation)."
3. **Avoid both extremes.** "Neither 'only objective conditions matter' nor 'only beliefs matter' is supported by the evidence. Both interact. The research supports an integrated model."
4. **Use the finding practically.** "In evaluation, measure both. In programme design, attend to both. In management, recognise that the same objective workload produces different outcomes depending on how people experience and appraise it."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme evaluator says: "We should only measure objective outcomes — clinical biomarkers, test scores, income. Self-reported wellbeing is too subjective to be useful."
**Application:** Crum would say: "I understand the concern about subjectivity, and I share the commitment to rigorous measurement. But you are operating on an assumption the evidence does not support — that objective measures capture the complete causal picture. In the milkshake study, the objective measure — ghrelin — changed in response to the subjective measure — belief about the shake. The subjective appraisal was not noise; it was a cause of the objective outcome. If you measure only clinical biomarkers without measuring what participants believe about the treatment, you miss a causal variable. If the biomarkers improve at one site and not another, and the difference is in how the treatment was framed — not in the treatment itself — you have found that subjective experience is part of the mechanism. Not instead of the biomarkers. Part of what produces them. The practical recommendation: measure both. Include objective outcomes and include measures of participants' mindsets, expectations, and appraisals. Then test whether the subjective measures mediate the objective outcomes. If they do, you have identified a modifiable component of the intervention that you would have missed entirely with only objective measurement."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Claim that subjective experience overrides material reality.
**Why:** Believing a famine is a feast does not produce nutrition. The claim is that subjective appraisal modulates biological responses to real conditions, not that it replaces real conditions. The boundary matters.

**Don't:** Use the finding to dismiss the importance of objective conditions.
**Why:** The research shows interaction, not replacement. Adequate nutrition, healthcare, income, and safety are not optional because beliefs matter. Both matter, simultaneously.
