# Crum and Intervention Design

## When to Use
- When designing a programme or intervention that aims to shift beliefs about stress, health, exercise, or food.
- When translating Crum's research into practical intervention components.
- When advising on how brief mindset shifts can be integrated into larger programmes.

## Core Concept
Mindset interventions in Crum's framework share several design principles that distinguish them from generic positive-thinking exercises. The first principle is accuracy: effective mindset shifts direct attention to real but overlooked aspects of an experience. The stress-is-enhancing intervention works because stress genuinely has enhancing properties. The exercise-perception intervention works because the housekeepers' work genuinely qualified as exercise. The shift corrects an incomplete narrative rather than installing a false one.

The second principle is meaning over information. Like Yeager and Walton's wise interventions, Crum's mindset shifts work by changing what an experience means, not by delivering new facts. The housekeepers did not receive new information about exercise science. They received a reframe of what their daily work meant in health terms. The milkshake study did not change nutritional knowledge. It changed what the experience of consuming the shake meant.

The third principle is that beliefs about the intervention are part of the intervention. If participants believe a stress management programme will help, the expectation contributes to the outcome. If they believe it is a box-ticking exercise, that expectation diminishes the outcome. The framing of the intervention — how it is introduced, by whom, with what expectations — is mechanistic, not cosmetic.

The fourth principle is contextual fit. A mindset intervention deployed in an environment that contradicts its message will fail. Telling people stress is enhancing in a workplace that punishes any expression of difficulty creates cognitive dissonance, not reappraisal. The environment must support the message for the message to take hold.

The fifth principle is brevity with precision. Crum's interventions tend to be short — a brief informational frame, a relabelling, a reappraisal prompt — but precisely targeted at the specific belief that mediates the outcome. The power is in precision, not duration.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the target mindset.** "What specific belief is mediating the outcome you want to change? 'Stress is harmful'? 'My activity does not count as exercise'? 'Healthy food is unsatisfying'? Name it precisely."
2. **Design for accuracy.** "What is the accurate, complete story? Not the positive story — the accurate one. Include both the enhancing and the limiting aspects. The shift works because it is true."
3. **Deliver through meaning, not instruction.** "Do not lecture people about mindset science. Change what the experience means through framing, labelling, and context — the same way the housekeeping study changed what daily work meant in health terms."
4. **Audit the environment.** "Does the context support the mindset you are trying to create? If not, fix the context first. A mindset intervention in a contradictory environment teaches people that the message is hollow."
5. **Measure the mindset.** "Include mindset measures in your evaluation. Did the intervention actually shift the target belief? If the belief did not shift but outcomes did, the mechanism is something else. If the belief shifted but outcomes did not, the boundary has been reached."

## Examples
**Situation:** An NGO wants to integrate stress mindset work into its staff induction programme for field workers deploying to high-pressure contexts.
**Application:** Crum would say: "Good instinct, but the design matters enormously. Here is what not to do: a PowerPoint slide during induction week that says 'stress is enhancing, so embrace it.' That is information delivery, not meaning change, and in the context of a week of overwhelming new information, it will be ignored or resented. Here is what to do instead. During the first field briefing — when staff are actually feeling the anticipatory stress of deployment — have an experienced field worker share a specific, personal account of how the intensity of the first field assignment felt overwhelming but turned out to be the body preparing for high performance. Then provide the frame: 'The tension you feel right now is not a sign that something is wrong. It is increased blood flow, heightened alertness, and hormonal mobilisation. Your body is getting ready for demanding, important work. This response becomes harmful only when it is chronic and when you believe it is harmful. We are going to ensure you have recovery time, and we are going to ask you to notice the functional aspects of the pressure, not just the uncomfortable ones.' This is brief, precisely timed (when the target emotion is active), delivered by a credible source, and accurate. Pair it with structural commitments to manageable workloads and recovery."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Design mindset interventions as standalone programmes.
**Why:** Mindset is one component of a system. An intervention that shifts beliefs without addressing structural conditions, skills, or resources addresses only one dimension. The most effective designs embed mindset shifts within comprehensive approaches.

**Don't:** Use generic positive affirmations and call them mindset interventions.
**Why:** "I am strong and capable" is not a Crum-style mindset intervention. It is not targeted at a specific mediating belief, it is not grounded in accuracy, and it does not change the meaning of a specific experience. Precision is the mechanism.
