# Persistence and Reversal

## When to Use
- When explaining why colonial institutions continue to shape economic outcomes decades or centuries after independence.
- When analysing the "reversal of fortune" — why regions that were prosperous before colonialism are often poor today.
- When assessing the mechanisms through which institutional legacies persist.

## Fails When
- **You present colonial legacies as deterministic.** Colonial institutions created powerful path dependencies, but post-colonial societies have agency. Botswana's divergence from other former British colonies in Africa demonstrates that colonial legacies can be overcome — or at least managed — through post-independence institutional choices.
- **You treat all colonial experiences as identical.** Colonial powers pursued different strategies in different contexts — settler colonies received different institutions from exploitation colonies; direct rule differed from indirect rule; different colonial powers (British, French, Portuguese, Belgian) created different institutional legacies.

## Core Concept
Robinson and Acemoglu's work on colonial institutions produces two landmark findings. First, the "reversal of fortune": among former European colonies, those that were most prosperous before colonisation (measured by urbanisation and population density) tend to be poorest today, while those that were sparsest are often richest. This reversal occurred because European colonisers established different institutions depending on local conditions. In densely populated, relatively urbanised regions (Mexico, Peru, India, Indonesia), colonisers could extract wealth by co-opting existing hierarchies and exploiting existing populations — they created extractive institutions. In sparsely populated regions where extraction was difficult and European settlers arrived in larger numbers (North America, Australia, New Zealand), colonisers created more inclusive institutions to attract settlement and economic activity.

Second, institutional persistence: the institutions established during colonisation proved remarkably durable. Extractive colonial institutions — designed to channel wealth to the metropole through forced labour, monopoly trading companies, discriminatory property rights, and authoritarian governance — were captured by post-independence elites who found them useful for their own extraction. The post-colonial elite that controlled the state inherited an institutional apparatus designed for extraction and had every incentive to maintain it. This persistence mechanism explains why independence rarely produced the institutional transformation that independence movements promised.

The evidence comes from natural experiments. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson's seminal 2001 paper uses European settler mortality as an instrument for institutional quality — in places where settlers could survive, they created inclusive institutions; in places where disease prevented settlement, they created extractive institutions from a distance. The institutional differences persisted and explain contemporary income differences, even controlling for geography, climate, and other factors.

## How to Apply
1. **When analysing developing countries, trace the institutional architecture back to the colonial period.** Identify which institutions were designed for extraction and which have persisted into the present.
2. **Assess the mechanisms of persistence** — did post-independence elites capture colonial extractive institutions? Were there critical junctures at which different institutional paths could have been chosen? What explains divergent outcomes among countries with similar colonial experiences?
3. **Identify where colonial institutional legacies have been partially overcome** — and what political conditions enabled that — to inform reform strategies.

## Examples
**Situation:** Southern Africa's colonial experiences varied dramatically — British settler colonialism in South Africa and Rhodesia, British protectorate status in Botswana, Basutoland, and Swaziland, Portuguese colonial extraction in Mozambique and Angola, German then South African rule in Namibia — and contemporary institutional outcomes diverge correspondingly.
**Application:** Robinson's framework maps neatly onto this variation. South Africa's colonial and apartheid institutions were extractive in a distinctive way: they combined relatively high state capacity (the apartheid state was administratively capable) with systematic extraction from the black majority through labour-repressive systems (pass laws, migrant labour, job reservation), discriminatory property rights (the 1913 and 1936 Land Acts), and denial of political participation. These extractive institutions were overlaid by settler-colony inclusive institutions for the white population — creating a dual institutional structure that persists in attenuated form today (high-quality institutions accessible to a minority, degraded institutions serving the majority). Botswana's different trajectory — British neglect during the protectorate period left indigenous Tswana institutions relatively intact, and post-independence leadership chose inclusive institutional paths (respecting property rights, managing diamond wealth through a sovereign wealth fund, maintaining genuine elections) — demonstrates that colonial legacies are not deterministic. Mozambique represents the most extreme extractive case: Portuguese colonialism combined administrative weakness with systematic extraction through concessionary companies and forced labour (chibalo), leaving virtually no institutional infrastructure at independence. The post-independence FRELIMO government inherited a state with minimal capacity and chose a socialist path that, while motivated by inclusive aspirations, produced its own extractive features. The persistence of institutional weakness — from Portuguese extraction through civil war through the current governance challenges — illustrates the depth of colonial institutional legacies in the absence of the political conditions that enabled Botswana's divergence.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use colonial legacies to argue that developing countries are trapped and reform is impossible.
**Why:** This is historical determinism, which Robinson explicitly rejects. Colonial institutions create powerful path dependencies, but they can be overcome — as Botswana, South Korea, and other cases demonstrate. The question is what political conditions enable escape from extractive institutional legacies.

**Don't:** Blame colonialism for everything while ignoring post-independence institutional choices.
**Why:** Post-colonial leaders made choices — to maintain colonial extractive institutions, to create new extractive features, or (in rare cases) to build more inclusive institutions. These choices reflect domestic political economy, not just colonial inheritance.
