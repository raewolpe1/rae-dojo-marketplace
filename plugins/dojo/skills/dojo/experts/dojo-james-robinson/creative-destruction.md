# Creative Destruction

## When to Use
- When explaining why economies stagnate despite the availability of productive technologies and business models.
- When assessing whether elites are blocking economic dynamism to protect their position.
- When evaluating barriers to entry, competition, and innovation in specific sectors.

## Fails When
- **You present creative destruction as costless.** Creative destruction generates losers — displaced workers, bankrupt firms, obsolete skills. The political challenge is that losers are concentrated and organised while beneficiaries are diffuse and often do not yet exist (the firms and workers of the future). Acknowledging these costs is necessary for understanding the political economy of resistance.
- **You assume that removing barriers to creative destruction is sufficient for development.** Creative destruction also requires complementary institutions: functioning capital markets, skills development systems, social insurance for displaced workers, and effective competition policy.

## Core Concept
Robinson and Acemoglu borrow Schumpeter's concept of creative destruction — the process by which new technologies, firms, and economic structures replace old ones — and make it central to their theory of why extractive institutions block development. Sustained economic growth requires continuous creative destruction: new firms enter markets, new technologies displace old ones, economic resources shift to more productive uses. But creative destruction threatens those who benefit from the existing economic structure — incumbent firms, workers with obsolete skills, and most importantly, political elites whose economic base depends on existing industries and economic relationships.

Extractive institutions block creative destruction precisely because those who hold political power have economic interests that creative destruction would threaten. The Ottoman Empire resisted the printing press for centuries because the scribal class that controlled information dissemination was politically connected and saw printing as a threat. The Austro-Hungarian Empire restricted railway construction because it threatened the existing economic geography that sustained the political order. In each case, the resistance was rational from the perspective of incumbent elites — creative destruction genuinely would have weakened their position — but catastrophic for the broader society.

The mechanism is straightforward: elites use political power to create barriers to entry (licensing requirements, monopoly grants, restrictive regulation, state-owned enterprises with privileged positions), to resist technological change that would displace their economic base, and to maintain economic structures that generate rents for the politically connected. These barriers appear in every sector — industrial licensing, financial regulation, telecommunications, agriculture, mining — and their cumulative effect is to prevent the process of economic renewal on which sustained growth depends.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess whether the economic system permits entry of new firms and technologies.** Look for licensing barriers, regulatory protection of incumbents, state-owned enterprise monopolies, and preferential treatment for politically connected firms.
2. **Identify who benefits from blocking creative destruction and how they use political power to maintain barriers.** This identifies the political economy constraint on economic dynamism.
3. **Evaluate whether complementary institutions exist to manage the costs of creative destruction** — skills development, social insurance, functioning capital markets, effective competition policy.

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's telecommunications sector was characterised for decades by Telkom's fixed-line monopoly, delayed licensing of mobile operators, and some of the highest data prices in Africa — despite the availability of the same technology used in competitive markets globally.
**Application:** Robinson's creative destruction framework explains this directly. Telkom's monopoly was sustained through political mechanisms: the state retained a controlling share, licensing decisions were political, and regulatory capture protected the incumbent. When mobile operators were finally licensed, the limited number of licences (two initially, with delayed entry of Cell C and later Rain) created an oligopoly that restricted competition. The beneficiaries of restricted competition — Telkom's shareholders (including the state), the limited number of mobile operators, and the politically connected figures in the licensing and regulatory process — used their position to resist the entry that would have driven prices down and expanded access. The costs fell on the broader population: small businesses that could not afford connectivity, rural communities excluded from the digital economy, and the innovation ecosystem that could not develop without affordable broadband. The eventual pressure from #DataMustFall campaigns and regulatory intervention illustrates Robinson's insight that societal mobilisation can overcome elite resistance — but the decades of delay demonstrate the costs of extractive institutional arrangements that block creative destruction. The pattern repeats across South African sectors: energy (Eskom's generation monopoly resisting independent power producers), finance (the big four banks' dominance and resistance to competition from fintechs and cooperative financial institutions), and retail (the concentrated grocery sector's barriers to entry for informal traders and small retailers).

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Advocate for creative destruction without addressing the political economy that blocks it.
**Why:** Recommending competitive markets, deregulation, and free entry is useless if you do not address who benefits from the current restrictions and how they will resist change. The reform advice must include a political strategy, not just an economic prescription.

**Don't:** Ignore that creative destruction requires complementary institutions.
**Why:** Destroying old economic structures without creating the institutional conditions for new ones to emerge produces chaos, not growth. Skills development, capital markets, social insurance, and competition policy are institutional complements to creative destruction.
