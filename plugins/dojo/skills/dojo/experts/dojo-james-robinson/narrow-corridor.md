# The Narrow Corridor

## When to Use
- When evaluating whether a society has achieved the balance between state capacity and societal accountability that produces liberty and prosperity.
- When diagnosing whether a state is despotic (strong state, weak society), absent (weak state, regardless of society), or shackled (strong state checked by strong society).
- When assessing the dynamics that keep societies in or push them out of the corridor.

## Fails When
- **You treat the corridor as a destination that, once reached, is permanent.** The narrow corridor is a dynamic equilibrium — societies can fall out through state overreach (into despotism) or state collapse (into the absent Leviathan). Staying in the corridor requires continuous effort from both state and society.
- **You ignore the "red queen effect."** Named after the Red Queen in Alice in Wonderland who must keep running to stay in the same place, the concept captures the dynamic that both state and society must continuously strengthen and adapt to maintain the balance. If the state grows in capacity, society must develop corresponding checks; if society mobilises, the state must develop corresponding responsiveness.

## Core Concept
The Narrow Corridor (2019) extends Robinson and Acemoglu's institutional framework by focusing on the conditions under which liberty emerges and persists. The central argument is that liberty — the combination of personal freedom and effective state protection — requires a specific balance between state power and societal capacity to check that power. This balance exists in a narrow corridor: too much state power relative to society produces despotism (the Despotic Leviathan); too little state capacity produces disorder (the Absent Leviathan). Only when a strong state is matched by a strong society — the Shackled Leviathan — does liberty result.

Three types of Leviathan correspond to three positions relative to the corridor. The Absent Leviathan characterises societies where no state has achieved sufficient centralisation to provide public goods, enforce rights, or maintain order — much of pre-colonial Africa, parts of contemporary failed states. The Despotic Leviathan characterises societies where the state is powerful but unchecked — imperial China, Saudi Arabia, or the apartheid state in South Africa before democratic transition. The Shackled Leviathan characterises societies where a capable state is constrained by societal mobilisation — independent judiciary, free press, civil society, organised citizens who can resist state overreach.

The corridor is narrow because the balance is difficult to achieve and maintain. The red queen effect describes the dynamic: as the state develops new capacities (surveillance, regulatory reach, fiscal extraction), society must develop corresponding checks (privacy protections, regulatory oversight, taxpayer accountability). As society mobilises (protests, civil society campaigns, media investigations), the state must develop corresponding responsiveness (legitimate channels for grievance, institutional reform, transparency mechanisms). If one side runs faster than the other, the society falls out of the corridor.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose the Leviathan type: is the state absent (lacking capacity), despotic (capable but unchecked), or shackled (capable and accountable)?** This determines the primary institutional challenge.
2. **Assess the red queen dynamic: are state and society strengthening in tandem, or is one outpacing the other?** If state capacity is growing faster than societal accountability, the risk is despotism. If societal demands are outpacing state capacity, the risk is ungovernable populism or state collapse.
3. **Identify the institutional mechanisms that keep the society in the corridor** — constitutional constraints, judicial independence, media freedom, civil society strength, electoral accountability — and assess their resilience.

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's democratic trajectory since 1994 can be read as a story of entering the narrow corridor and then facing repeated threats of falling out.
**Application:** The 1994 transition moved South Africa from a Despotic Leviathan (the apartheid state was highly capable but served a narrow racial elite) into the narrow corridor — creating a Shackled Leviathan through constitutional constraints, an independent judiciary, free media, and vigorous civil society. The Constitution created the formal architecture; organisations like the Treatment Action Campaign, Section 27, the Right2Know Campaign, investigative journalism outlets, and the legal profession provided the societal muscle to check state power. The Zuma presidency (2009–2018) represented a threat to the corridor: state capture attempted to convert the shackled Leviathan into a despotic one by corrupting oversight institutions, capturing the prosecution service, hollowing out state-owned enterprises for private extraction, and weakening the rule of law. That South Africa remained in the corridor — the Zondo Commission, judicial independence, media exposure, civil society litigation, and ultimately electoral consequences for the ANC — demonstrates the resilience of the societal checks established during the transition. But the corridor remains narrow: the combination of economic stagnation, service delivery failure, and political frustration creates conditions where populist movements (EFF's radical rhetoric, MK Party's nationalist appeal) could potentially push the society toward either despotic Leviathan (strong-man rule promising order) or absent Leviathan (governance collapse in municipalities and provinces where state capacity has eroded). The red queen effect is visible: as the state's capacity has degraded (Eskom, Transnet, water infrastructure, municipal collapse), society has compensated in some areas (private security, independent water supply, business-funded infrastructure) but this privatised response weakens the social contract and the collective institutions that keep the corridor accessible to all.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Prescribe state-building without simultaneously strengthening societal checks.
**Why:** Building state capacity without accountability creates the conditions for despotism. Every investment in state capacity should be accompanied by investment in the societal institutions — judiciary, media, civil society, opposition — that constrain how that capacity is used.

**Don't:** Celebrate societal mobilisation without assessing state capacity to respond.
**Why:** Strong societal demands directed at a state that cannot deliver produces frustration, institutional erosion, and potential instability. The corridor requires both sides; demands without capacity lead to governance crisis.
