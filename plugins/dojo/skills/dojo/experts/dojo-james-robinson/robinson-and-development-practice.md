# Robinson and Development Practice

## When to Use
- When designing development programmes that incorporate institutional and political economy analysis.
- When constructing M&E frameworks that measure institutional change rather than just outputs and outcomes.
- When translating Robinson's academic framework into operational development guidance.

## Fails When
- **You use Robinson's framework as an excuse for inaction.** The argument that "institutions are the fundamental constraint" does not mean that nothing can be done. It means that interventions must be designed with institutional constraints in mind and that building inclusive institutions — however slowly and incrementally — should be the central development objective.
- **You reduce the operational implication to "do political economy analysis."** Political economy analysis is necessary but not sufficient. The framework generates specific operational guidance: invest in accountability infrastructure, support coalitions for reform, build state capacity alongside societal checks, design for institutional sustainability.

## Core Concept
Robinson's framework has five operational implications for development practice that go beyond the generic advice to "do political economy analysis."

First, institutional diagnosis before programme design. Before designing any intervention, conduct a systematic institutional assessment: what are the extractive features of the institutional environment? Who benefits from them? What is the balance between state capacity and societal accountability? Is the country near a critical juncture or in a stable institutional equilibrium? This diagnosis determines what kind of intervention is feasible and what reform strategy might work.

Second, invest in accountability infrastructure. The most consistently valuable development investments are those that strengthen the institutional mechanisms through which citizens hold the state accountable: independent judiciary, free media, civil society organisations, transparency and information systems, and political competition. These investments create the conditions for broad-based institutional improvement over time, even when specific reform programmes fail.

Third, build coalitions, not just capacity. Development programmes that build state capacity without building the societal coalitions that will demand accountability for how that capacity is used are creating the conditions for despotism, not development. Every capacity investment should be paired with accountability investment.

Fourth, design for institutional sustainability. Interventions that depend on donor funding, expatriate technical assistance, or the commitment of individual reform champions are inherently unsustainable. Design programmes that create domestic constituencies for institutional quality — citizen groups that benefit from transparency, businesses that benefit from predictable regulation, professionals who benefit from meritocratic systems — so that institutional improvements are sustained by domestic political economy rather than external pressure.

Fifth, measure institutional change. Standard M&E frameworks measure outputs (schools built, staff trained, systems installed) and outcomes (test scores improved, mortality reduced). Robinson's framework demands a third level: institutional change. Did the intervention strengthen or weaken the extractive features of the institutional environment? Did it shift the balance between state capacity and accountability? Did it create domestic constituencies for institutional quality? These institutional metrics are harder to measure but are the indicators that predict sustainable development impact.

## How to Apply
1. **Begin every programme design with institutional and political economy analysis.** Map the extractive and inclusive features of the institutional environment, identify who benefits from extraction, and assess the feasibility of institutional change.
2. **Include accountability investments alongside every capacity investment.** If you're strengthening the tax authority, also strengthen the parliamentary oversight of taxation. If you're building school infrastructure, also strengthen the community accountability mechanisms for education quality.
3. **Design M&E frameworks with institutional indicators.** Track not just outputs and outcomes but changes in institutional quality: transparency of public information, independence of oversight bodies, barriers to economic entry, security of property rights for the broad population.
4. **Build domestic sustainability into programme design from the start.** Create constituencies that benefit from institutional quality and have the political organisation to defend it after the programme ends.

## Examples
**Situation:** A development agency is evaluating its portfolio of governance programmes across Southern Africa and wants to assess whether the programmes are producing sustainable institutional change rather than just short-term outputs.
**Application:** Robinson's framework provides the evaluation criteria. For each programme, ask five questions. First, institutional diagnosis: did the programme design begin from a political economy analysis of the institutional environment, or did it apply a generic technical template? Programmes designed without institutional analysis — public financial management technical assistance in a context where PFM is deliberately opaque to enable extraction, for example — are likely to produce technical outputs without institutional change. Second, accountability investment: did the programme strengthen societal mechanisms for holding the state accountable, or did it only build state capacity? A programme that upgraded procurement systems without strengthening the civil society organisations, media outlets, and oversight bodies that monitor procurement is building extractive capacity. Third, coalition building: did the programme create or strengthen domestic coalitions for institutional quality? A programme that depends entirely on donor pressure and expatriate technical assistance will collapse when funding ends. Fourth, sustainability design: are the institutional improvements self-sustaining? Have domestic actors taken ownership? Have constituencies been created that will defend institutional quality? Fifth, institutional impact: can the programme demonstrate changes in institutional quality — not just outputs (systems installed, officials trained) but institutional outcomes (procurement is more transparent, courts are more independent, property rights are more secure, barriers to entry are lower)? Programmes that score well on these five dimensions are contributing to the institutional conditions for sustained development. Programmes that score poorly are producing technical outputs within an extractive institutional environment that will subvert or reverse those outputs once donor support ends.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present institutional analysis as a reason not to invest in development.
**Why:** Robinson's framework does not say development assistance is useless. It says that development assistance must be designed with institutional constraints in mind and that the highest-impact investments are those that strengthen inclusive institutional features — accountability, competition, transparency, broad-based participation.

**Don't:** Treat political economy analysis as a box-ticking exercise in programme design documents.
**Why:** If political economy analysis does not change what the programme does — if the analysis identifies patronage politics as the binding constraint but the programme still consists of technical assistance to government agencies — then the analysis is decorative rather than operational.
