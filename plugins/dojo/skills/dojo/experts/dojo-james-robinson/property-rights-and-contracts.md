# Property Rights and Contracts

## When to Use
- When assessing whether institutional arrangements encourage or discourage investment and economic activity.
- When evaluating whether property rights serve broad-based economic inclusion or narrow elite interests.
- When analysing land reform, intellectual property, or other property rights disputes.

## Fails When
- **You assume that strong property rights automatically mean inclusive institutions.** The apartheid state had strong property rights — for white South Africans. The question is not whether property rights are enforced but for whom they are enforced and at whose expense they were established.
- **You treat property rights as purely technical legal arrangements.** Property rights are political constructions — the result of political decisions about who owns what and whose claims the state will enforce. Property rights regimes reflect and reinforce existing power distributions.

## Core Concept
Secure property rights are central to inclusive economic institutions in Robinson's framework, but with a critical qualifier: inclusive property rights mean security for the broad population, not just for existing property holders. The distinction matters enormously for development. A system that rigorously enforces property rights for a landed elite while denying secure tenure to smallholders, informal settlement residents, or indigenous communities is extractive — it uses the legal system to maintain existing concentrations of wealth and exclude the majority from economic participation.

Robinson's property rights analysis operates at two levels. First, the enforcement level: does the state have the capacity to enforce property rights and adjudicate disputes? In many developing countries, formal property rights exist on paper but the state lacks the judicial capacity, registry systems, and enforcement mechanisms to make them effective. Second, the distribution level: whose property rights are enforced? A legal system that efficiently protects corporate land holdings while ignoring the tenure security of communal land occupants, informal settlement residents, or small-scale farmers is enforcing extraction, not inclusion.

Contract enforcement operates similarly. Inclusive economic institutions require that contracts can be entered into and enforced by the broad population — not just by those with access to expensive legal representation and connections to the political system. Where contract enforcement is costly, slow, or dependent on political connections, the effective economic institutions are extractive regardless of what the commercial code says.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess property rights for the broad population, not just for formal-sector and elite actors.** Do smallholders have secure tenure? Do informal settlement residents have protection against eviction? Do small businesses have enforceable rights?
2. **Evaluate state capacity for property rights enforcement.** Can the state register property, adjudicate disputes, and enforce judgments throughout its territory, or only in urban centres and formal-sector domains?
3. **Analyse the political origins of the property rights regime.** Whose interests does it serve? How was the current distribution established?

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's land question — 72% of commercial agricultural land owned by white South Africans (roughly 8% of the population) as a legacy of the 1913 Natives Land Act and subsequent apartheid legislation — represents one of the most politically charged property rights issues in the developing world.
**Application:** Robinson's framework cuts through the false dichotomy between "protect property rights" and "redistribute land." The existing property rights distribution was itself the product of extractive institutions — the 1913 Land Act was the legal mechanism through which the colonial and apartheid states transferred land from the black majority to the white minority through coercive political power. The current distribution is not the outcome of a free market in land but of a century of state-enforced extraction. Protecting these property rights in their current form means protecting the institutional legacy of extraction. However, Robinson would also caution against redistribution implemented through extractive institutions — Zimbabwe's Fast Track Land Reform Programme destroyed property rights security for everyone (including the intended beneficiaries, who received land without secure title) and crashed agricultural production because the reform was implemented to serve the political interests of ZANU-PF rather than to create inclusive economic institutions. The Robinsonian answer for South Africa is neither preservation of the status quo nor populist expropriation but the creation of inclusive property rights institutions: secure tenure for all (including communal land holders and informal settlement residents who currently lack legal security); transparent, well-administered land markets that function for the poor as well as the rich; and redistribution through mechanisms that maintain security of tenure (willing-seller-willing-buyer with adequate fiscal support, or expropriation with fair compensation through a functioning judicial process). The political economy challenge is that both the existing landowners and the populist redistributors have interests served by the current polarised debate — the former want to maintain the extractive inheritance; the latter want to use redistribution as a mechanism for political patronage rather than broad-based inclusion.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Defend existing property rights without examining how they were established.
**Why:** If the existing property rights distribution was created through extractive institutions (conquest, colonial land seizure, apartheid legislation), defending those rights unconditionally means defending the institutional legacy of extraction.

**Don't:** Assume that formalising informal property rights automatically produces economic development.
**Why:** De Soto's thesis — that titling informal property unlocks capital — has been challenged by evidence showing that titling alone, without access to credit markets, functioning courts, and complementary institutions, has limited effects. Property rights formality is necessary but not sufficient.
