# Political Economy of Institutions

## When to Use
- When explaining why institutions that are harmful to the majority persist.
- When identifying who benefits from the institutional status quo and how they maintain it.
- When analysing the gap between de jure institutional rules and de facto institutional practice.

## Fails When
- **You treat dysfunctional institutions as accidents or mistakes.** Robinson's central point is that institutions that appear dysfunctional from a development perspective are typically functional from the perspective of those who hold power. They persist because powerful actors benefit from them and have the means to prevent change.
- **You ignore the distinction between de jure and de facto power.** Formal institutional rules (constitutions, laws) allocate de jure power. But de facto power — derived from wealth, control of violence, organisational capacity, or social networks — may produce different actual outcomes regardless of formal rules.

## Core Concept
Robinson's political economy framework begins from a simple premise: institutions are not designed by benevolent social planners but by political actors pursuing their interests. The question "why do bad institutions exist?" has a straightforward answer: because they serve the interests of those who have the power to create and maintain them. Extractive institutions persist not because no one knows better alternatives exist, but because those who benefit from extraction have the political power to block institutional change.

This framework distinguishes between de jure political power (allocated by formal political institutions — constitutions, electoral systems, legal frameworks) and de facto political power (derived from economic resources, control of armed force, collective organisation, or social networks). In well-functioning institutional environments, de jure and de facto power substantially overlap. But in many developing countries, de facto power diverges sharply from de jure power: the constitution may guarantee rights that economic elites can subvert; elections may exist but patronage networks determine outcomes; regulatory agencies may have formal authority but lack the capacity or independence to exercise it against powerful interests.

The vicious circle describes how extractive institutions reproduce themselves. Those who benefit from extraction accumulate economic resources. Economic resources translate into de facto political power through patronage, campaign finance, media ownership, and corruption. Political power is used to maintain the extractive institutional arrangements that generate economic resources. Breaking this circle requires a shift in the balance of power — which is why critical junctures (exogenous disruptions that weaken the extractive equilibrium) are often necessary for institutional change.

The framework generates a specific analytical procedure: for any institution, ask three questions. Who benefits from this arrangement? How do they use their political power to maintain it? What would it take to shift the balance of power sufficiently to change it?

## How to Apply
1. **For any institutional arrangement, identify the beneficiaries.** Who gains from the current rules? Follow the money and the power.
2. **Map the mechanisms of institutional maintenance.** How do beneficiaries use political power — formal authority, patronage, corruption, media control, coercion, collective organisation — to prevent reform?
3. **Distinguish de jure from de facto.** Identify where formal institutional rules diverge from actual practice, and trace the de facto power that explains the divergence.

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's Broad-Based Black Economic Empowerment (BEE) policy was designed to transform the racial ownership structure of the economy but has been widely criticised for creating a narrow politically-connected black elite rather than broad-based ownership transformation.
**Application:** Robinson's political economy framework asks: who benefits from BEE as currently implemented, and how do they maintain this arrangement? The beneficiaries are identifiable: a relatively small number of politically-connected individuals who receive BEE equity stakes, board positions, and procurement preferences; existing corporate incumbents who use BEE compliance as a barrier to entry (the compliance cost is trivial for large firms but prohibitive for small entrants); and political actors who use BEE patronage as a mechanism for maintaining political coalitions. The de jure framework (the BEE codes, the DTI's scorecard system) formally promotes broad-based empowerment. The de facto outcome — concentration of BEE benefits among a narrow elite — reflects the actual distribution of political and economic power. Those who benefit from narrow BEE have the resources and political connections to influence how the codes are interpreted and enforced, to ensure that empowerment requirements are satisfied through equity deals with connected individuals rather than genuine ownership dispersion, and to resist reforms that would broaden the benefits. The political economy is self-reinforcing: narrow BEE creates wealthy politically-connected individuals whose wealth depends on continued access to political patronage, creating incentives to maintain the narrow implementation rather than to reform it toward genuine broad-based ownership. Robinson's framework predicts that reforming BEE toward genuine broad-based empowerment would require a shift in political coalitions — specifically, political pressure from the broad black majority who are excluded from BEE benefits, sufficient to overcome the resistance of the narrow elite who benefit from the current arrangement.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that identifying an inefficient institution is sufficient to produce reform.
**Why:** Efficiency is irrelevant if the institution serves the interests of those who hold power. The question is not "is this institution efficient?" but "who benefits from this institution and do they have the power to maintain it?"

**Don't:** Treat corruption as an aberration rather than an institutional feature.
**Why:** Systematic corruption is an institutional arrangement — a way of allocating resources and maintaining political coalitions. It persists because it serves the interests of those who participate in it. Anti-corruption efforts that treat corruption as individual moral failure rather than institutional structure will fail because they do not address the political economy that sustains it.
