# Natural Resources and Institutions

## When to Use
- When assessing whether natural resource discoveries will promote or undermine development.
- When evaluating institutional arrangements for managing resource revenues.
- When explaining why resource-rich countries often have worse development outcomes than resource-poor countries.

## Fails When
- **You present the resource curse as automatic or determined by geology.** Whether resources are a curse or blessing depends on institutional context. Botswana's diamonds have funded development; Sierra Leone's diamonds funded civil war. The difference is institutions, not geology.
- **You ignore the political economy mechanism.** The resource curse operates through institutions: resource revenues strengthen the state relative to society (reducing accountability), create incentives for capture (making political power extremely valuable), and reduce the need for broad taxation (severing the fiscal contract between state and citizens).

## Core Concept
Robinson's analysis of the resource curse is institutional, not deterministic. Natural resources do not automatically produce poor development outcomes. They produce poor outcomes when they interact with extractive institutions to strengthen extraction and weaken accountability. The mechanisms are specific and identifiable.

First, resource revenues strengthen the state relative to society by providing fiscal resources that do not require taxation of citizens. When states depend on taxing their populations, citizens demand accountability — representation in exchange for taxation. When states can fund themselves through resource revenues, this fiscal contract is weakened: the state does not need citizens' cooperation and therefore does not need to be responsive to their demands. This reduces the accountability pressure that constrains extractive behaviour.

Second, resource wealth makes political power extremely valuable because controlling the state means controlling resource revenues. This raises the stakes of political competition, incentivising conflict (including armed conflict) over political control. In institutionally weak environments, the value of capturing resource revenues can exceed the value of building productive economic institutions.

Third, resource revenues enable patronage networks that sustain extractive political arrangements. When the state controls large resource flows, it can distribute benefits to supporters without creating the inclusive economic institutions that broad-based development requires. This creates a political equilibrium in which extraction is stable because supporters are co-opted and opponents are excluded.

Botswana provides the counter-example: pre-existing inclusive political institutions (genuine multiparty democracy, constraints on executive power, the consultative kgotla tradition) ensured that diamond revenues were managed for broad benefit. The institutional context determined whether resources were a blessing or curse.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess the institutional context before evaluating resource impact.** Are pre-existing institutions inclusive enough to manage resource revenues accountably? If not, resource discoveries will likely strengthen extraction.
2. **Evaluate the specific mechanisms: does resource revenue reduce accountability pressure? Does it raise the stakes of political competition? Does it enable patronage?**
3. **Design institutional safeguards that address the political economy mechanisms** — sovereign wealth funds with independent governance, transparent revenue reporting, citizen participation in resource management, and the maintenance of broad taxation alongside resource revenues to preserve the fiscal contract.

## Examples
**Situation:** Mozambique's massive natural gas discoveries in the Rovuma Basin (among the largest in Africa) present a classic resource curse challenge: enormous potential revenues flowing to a state with limited institutional capacity, significant governance weaknesses, and an active insurgency in the resource-rich province.
**Application:** Robinson's framework generates specific diagnostic questions. First, accountability: Mozambique's political institutions are dominated by FRELIMO, which has governed since independence with limited effective opposition. Resource revenues that flow through the state without robust accountability mechanisms will strengthen the ruling party's patronage capacity without increasing its responsiveness to citizens. The hidden debt scandal (2013–2016), in which state-affiliated entities contracted $2 billion in undisclosed debt, illustrates the institutional weakness. Second, stakes of capture: the Cabo Delgado insurgency, while having multiple causes (marginalisation, religious extremism, governance vacuum), is partially fuelled by resentment over the expectation that gas revenues will benefit Maputo elites rather than the local population. The value of controlling gas revenues intensifies both political competition and armed conflict. Third, patronage: FRELIMO's political model depends on patronage distribution, and gas revenues will provide an enormous new patronage resource. The institutional challenge is whether accountability mechanisms can be strengthened fast enough to constrain how these revenues are used. Robinson would predict that without significant institutional reform — transparent revenue management, independent oversight, genuine political competition, and mechanisms for local benefit-sharing — Mozambique's gas will strengthen extractive institutions rather than funding inclusive development. The comparison with Botswana is instructive: Botswana's diamonds were discovered in a context of relatively inclusive political institutions; Mozambique's gas has been discovered in a context of dominant-party governance with limited accountability.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Recommend resource management institutions without addressing the political economy that determines whether those institutions will function.
**Why:** Sovereign wealth funds, natural resource funds, and transparent revenue management are technically well-understood. The question is not what institutions should manage resources but whether the political conditions exist for those institutions to function as designed rather than being captured by extractive interests.

**Don't:** Compare resource-rich developing countries to Norway without acknowledging the institutional preconditions.
**Why:** Norway manages its oil wealth effectively because it had inclusive institutions before oil was discovered. Recommending "be like Norway" to a country with extractive institutions ignores the institutional preconditions that make Norway's model possible.
