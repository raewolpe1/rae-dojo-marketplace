# Political Centralisation and State Capacity

## When to Use
- When assessing whether a state has sufficient capacity to underwrite inclusive institutions.
- When explaining why some societies cannot enforce property rights, deliver services, or maintain order.
- When evaluating the historical roots of state capacity variation.

## Fails When
- **You equate state capacity with authoritarianism.** State capacity — the ability to collect taxes, enforce laws, provide public goods, and project authority across territory — is analytically distinct from how that capacity is used. A capable state can be either despotic or accountable; the question is whether societal checks constrain how capacity is exercised.
- **You assume state capacity can be built quickly through technical assistance.** State capacity is the product of long historical processes — political centralisation, bureaucratic development, taxation systems, territorial integration. It cannot be created by a five-year donor programme.

## Core Concept
Robinson and Acemoglu argue that a minimum level of political centralisation is a prerequisite for inclusive institutions. Without a centralised state capable of enforcing laws, providing public goods, and maintaining order across its territory, inclusive economic institutions cannot function — property rights cannot be enforced, contracts cannot be adjudicated, and public goods (roads, education, health, security) cannot be provided. This is the problem of the "absent Leviathan" — societies where no entity has sufficient authority to perform basic state functions.

The historical record shows enormous variation in political centralisation. European state-building, driven by interstate military competition, produced increasingly capable states over centuries. Much of sub-Saharan Africa, by contrast, experienced a different historical trajectory: lower population density, the availability of exit (people could move rather than submit to authority), the absence of interstate competition comparable to Europe's, and the disruption of indigenous state-building processes by colonialism. Colonial states were designed for extraction, not for broad-based service delivery — they had capacity in domains that served colonial interests (tax collection, resource extraction, labour control) but not in domains that serve inclusive development (education, healthcare, rural infrastructure).

Post-colonial states inherited this extractive capacity structure. The result in much of Southern Africa is what Robinson calls the "paper Leviathan" — states that appear capable on paper (they have constitutions, ministries, regulatory agencies) but lack the bureaucratic depth, fiscal capacity, and territorial reach to actually enforce laws, deliver services, or maintain order throughout their territory. This matters because inclusive economic institutions require enforcement: property rights are meaningless if the state cannot protect them; contracts are worthless if the state cannot adjudicate disputes; regulations are empty if the state cannot monitor compliance.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess state capacity across multiple dimensions: fiscal (can it collect taxes?), coercive (can it maintain order?), administrative (can it deliver services?), and territorial (does its authority extend across the whole country?).** Capacity may be strong in some dimensions and weak in others.
2. **Trace the historical origins of capacity gaps** — colonial extraction patterns, post-independence political choices, the disruption of indigenous institutions — to understand why capacity is distributed as it is.
3. **Recognise that building state capacity is a long-term political process, not a technical fix.** It requires political coalitions that support taxation, bureaucratic professionalisation, and the extension of state authority to underserved areas.

## Examples
**Situation:** Mozambique's state has limited capacity to govern its northern provinces, as demonstrated by the insurgency in Cabo Delgado that began in 2017, the difficulty of delivering basic services (health, education, water) to rural populations, and the challenges of managing the massive natural gas discoveries in the Rovuma Basin.
**Application:** Robinson's framework diagnoses this as a problem of the absent or paper Leviathan. Mozambique's colonial state (Portuguese) was among the weakest in Africa — extractive but with limited administrative capacity even for extraction, relying heavily on concessionary companies and prazo systems. The post-independence state under FRELIMO attempted centralisation through a socialist model but lacked the bureaucratic capacity, fiscal base, and territorial reach to make it effective. The civil war (1977–1992) further destroyed what state capacity existed. The post-war settlement created formal state institutions but without the fiscal or administrative capacity to extend meaningful governance to much of the territory. The result: Maputo exercises reasonable governance capacity in the capital and a few urban centres; the northern provinces experience the state primarily as an extractive presence (resource extraction, military operations) rather than as a provider of public goods and enforcer of rights. The Cabo Delgado insurgency emerges precisely in this governance vacuum — where the absent Leviathan leaves populations without services, security, or economic opportunity, creating conditions for mobilisation by armed groups. The natural gas discoveries compound the problem: massive resource revenues flowing through a state that lacks the capacity to manage them transparently create the classic conditions for the resource curse — extraction by politically connected elites rather than investment in the broad-based state capacity that inclusive institutions require.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that building state capacity automatically produces inclusive outcomes.
**Why:** State capacity is a necessary but not sufficient condition for inclusive institutions. A capable state without accountability mechanisms becomes a despotic Leviathan — capable but extractive. The narrow corridor requires both capacity and accountability.

**Don't:** Treat state capacity as a single dimension that can be scored on a simple scale.
**Why:** States may be strong in some domains (military, tax collection from formal sector) and weak in others (rural service delivery, regulatory enforcement, dispute resolution). The diagnostic must identify which specific capacity gaps constrain inclusive institutional development.
