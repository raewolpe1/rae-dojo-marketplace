# Extractive versus Inclusive Institutions

## When to Use
- When diagnosing why a country or region is poor or unequal.
- When evaluating whether institutional arrangements create broad-based opportunity or concentrate wealth.
- When distinguishing between institutional, geographic, and cultural explanations of development.

## Fails When
- **You treat extractive/inclusive as a simple binary.** Real institutions exist on a spectrum, and most countries have a mix — some domains may be more inclusive while others remain extractive. The analysis requires granularity, not just labelling.
- **You claim extractive institutions cannot generate growth.** They can — the Soviet Union industrialised rapidly, China has grown extraordinarily under authoritarian governance. The point is that extractive growth is typically unsustainable because it cannot support creative destruction and eventually the incentives for investment and innovation erode.

## Core Concept
Robinson and Acemoglu's central argument is that the wealth and poverty of nations is explained primarily by their institutions — the humanly devised rules that structure political and economic life. The critical distinction is between inclusive institutions (which distribute political power broadly, enforce property rights for the many, provide a level playing field, and permit creative destruction) and extractive institutions (which concentrate power in a narrow elite and structure economic life to channel wealth toward that elite at the expense of the broader population).

The framework operates at two levels. Political institutions determine who holds power and how that power is constrained. Economic institutions determine who can participate in economic activity and on what terms. The crucial insight is that political institutions drive economic institutions: those who hold political power shape economic rules to serve their interests. Inclusive economic institutions — competitive markets, secure property rights, freedom of contract for the broad population — require inclusive political institutions because, without political pluralism, elites will reshape economic rules to protect their position.

Extractive institutions create a vicious circle: political power enables economic extraction, which generates wealth that reinforces political power. This self-reinforcing dynamic explains why extractive institutions persist even when they impoverish the majority — those who benefit from extraction have the means and motive to maintain the system. Inclusive institutions create a virtuous circle: broad political participation generates demand for inclusive economic institutions, which create wealth that supports further political participation and institutional strengthening.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose institutional quality by examining both political and economic dimensions.** Ask: who holds political power? How is it constrained? Who can participate in economic activity? Are property rights secure for the broad population? Can new firms enter markets freely?
2. **When institutions appear mixed, identify which dimension dominates.** A country may have formally inclusive political institutions (elections) but extractive economic institutions (state monopolies, insider-connected firms receiving preferential treatment, barriers to entry).
3. **Look for the vicious or virtuous circle.** Determine whether political and economic power are reinforcing extraction or reinforcing inclusion. This diagnostic shapes the reform strategy.

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's economy features formally inclusive political institutions (multiparty democracy, independent judiciary, free press) but persistently extractive economic features (extreme corporate concentration, high barriers to entry for small firms, insider-connected BEE beneficiaries, a labour market that excludes a third of the workforce).
**Application:** Robinson's framework illuminates this as a case where de jure political inclusion coexists with significant de facto extractive economic features. The post-apartheid political settlement changed the formal rules — universal suffrage, constitutional rights, anti-discrimination legislation — but the underlying economic structure retained extractive characteristics inherited from apartheid (concentrated ownership, a skills distribution that excludes the majority, spatial separation) and added new extractive features through the political economy of the ANC alliance (BEE rent-seeking, state capture, bargaining council extension that protects insiders). The critical diagnostic question is whether South Africa is in a virtuous circle (where inclusive political institutions will gradually produce more inclusive economic institutions) or whether extractive economic features are eroding political inclusion (through the translation of economic concentration into political influence, corruption of state institutions, and the weakening of accountability mechanisms). The evidence of state capture under the Zuma presidency suggests that the vicious circle dynamic was gaining strength — concentrated economic power corrupting political institutions — though the subsequent institutional response (Zondo Commission, electoral consequences for the ANC) suggests that inclusive political institutions retain significant resilience.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Label a country's institutions as simply "extractive" or "inclusive" without granular analysis.
**Why:** Real institutional environments are complex — a country may have inclusive institutions in some domains and extractive institutions in others. The analytical value comes from identifying which institutional features are extractive, who benefits from them, and how they interact with more inclusive features.

**Don't:** Assume that formal institutional reforms (new constitutions, new laws) automatically create inclusive institutions.
**Why:** De jure changes in formal rules often fail to produce de facto institutional change because those with economic and political power find ways to subvert, capture, or circumvent new formal institutions. Robinson's framework requires attention to both formal rules and the actual distribution of power.
