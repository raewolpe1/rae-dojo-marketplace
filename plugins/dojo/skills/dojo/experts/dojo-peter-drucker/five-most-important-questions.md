## When to Use

Use this framework when an organization — or a unit, department, or individual — needs to clarify purpose and direction before setting goals or plans. It is particularly valuable at inflection points: starting something new, renewing a long-established organization, navigating a major change in context, or resolving persistent strategic confusion. It is also the right tool when an organization is performing well operationally but seems to have lost its sense of why.

## Fails When

The five questions fail when treated as a planning template to be filled in and filed. They are a discipline of honest inquiry, and they require real answers — not the diplomatic language of vision statements designed to offend no one and commit the organization to nothing. They also fail when the conversations they generate are not followed by decisions and changed behavior. And they fail when applied by organizations that are not yet able or willing to be honest about who their customer actually is and what that customer values.

## Core Concept

In 1989, Peter Drucker was asked by the Girl Scouts of the USA to help them think through their future. From that engagement and others like it emerged *The Five Most Important Questions You Will Ever Ask About Your Organization* — a short, concentrated framework for organizational self-examination that Drucker considered among his most important contributions.

The five questions are:

**1. What is our mission?**

Not the mission statement, which is usually a public-relations document. The actual mission: the reason the organization exists, stated in terms of the difference it is trying to make in the world. Drucker's test for a good mission is that it should be actionable — it should tell someone in the organization what to do and what not to do. "To make a profit" fails this test because it tells you nothing about what to do to make a profit. "To give ordinary people access to the books that used to be available only to the affluent" passes, because it rules out some things (expensive books) and points toward others (cheap paperbacks, libraries, affordable formats).

**2. Who is our customer?**

This is the most disruptive of the five questions, because organizations typically resist a clear answer. They prefer "everyone" or lists of stakeholders. Drucker was insistent: there are primary customers, whose lives are changed by the organization's work, and there are supporting customers, who must be satisfied for the work to be possible. Confusing the two, or pretending there is only one, or trying to serve too many primary customers at once, produces strategic incoherence. For a school, the primary customer is the student; the supporting customers are parents, the community, funders. Designing the school primarily around the preferences of parents rather than the development of students is a common mistake with predictable consequences.

**3. What does the customer value?**

This is the question most organizations answer incorrectly. They answer it from the inside — from what they produce, what they believe they are good at, what they want to provide. The customer's actual answer is usually different, and the gap between what the organization thinks the customer values and what the customer actually values explains much of the failure to achieve results. Drucker insisted this question must be answered by asking customers directly, not by analysis or inference. And the answer is often surprising: customers value things the organization doesn't track and doesn't think of as its contribution.

**4. What are our results?**

For businesses, results are economic. For nonprofits and government agencies, results are changed lives, changed behavior, or changed conditions in the constituencies they serve. Drucker was unsparing: results are defined outside the organization, not inside it. Activity is not a result. A hospital that treats patients has produced activity. A hospital that produces healthy patients — who leave healthier than they arrived, who do not return with preventable complications — has produced results. Organizations that cannot name their results are not yet clear about their mission.

**5. What is our plan?**

Not a strategic plan in the elaborate sense, but a working plan that specifies what changes need to be made in light of the previous four answers. The plan follows from the honest answers to the first four questions. If the mission is right, the customer is correctly identified, the customer's values are understood, and the results are clearly defined, the plan specifies what must be done differently to improve performance against those results.

The five questions work as a system. Answering any one of them poorly contaminates the rest. A mission that doesn't reflect the customer's reality produces bad answers to "what do customers value?" A wrong definition of results makes the plan aim at the wrong target. The discipline is to work through all five questions honestly and in sequence, and to be willing to change what is working against results.

## How to Apply

1. **Reserve the questions for genuine leadership-level inquiry.** These are not survey questions or workshop exercises. They are questions that require the organization's most senior decision-makers to sit with uncomfortable truths and make real choices. Facilitate the process at the level where strategy decisions are actually made.

2. **Separate the mission question from the mission statement.** Ask: what difference does this organization make in the world? If we did not exist, what would be worse? If we are successful, what will have changed? The answer to these questions is the mission; the statement may need rewriting to reflect it.

3. **Define the customer with specificity.** Identify both the primary customer (whose life or behavior the organization most directly aims to change) and the supporting customers (who must be satisfied to enable the work). Recognize that designing around the wrong customer is a common strategic error.

4. **Ask customers directly what they value.** Do not rely on organizational assumptions. Conduct actual conversations with the primary customers. Ask: what do you get from us? What difference does it make? What do you wish we did differently? What do you value most? The answers will often be surprising.

5. **Name the results, not the activities.** For each significant program or initiative, specify the result — the change in the customer that constitutes success. Then evaluate honestly: are those results occurring? At what rate? Are they improving or declining? The answers drive the plan.

## Examples

**Example 1: A university extension program**

A university's continuing education division has been offering the same catalog of professional development courses for years. Enrollment is declining but the program is running smoothly. Applying the five questions: the mission is not "offer professional development courses" but something like "help working professionals build the capabilities their careers require." The customer is not "anyone who enrolls" but a specific profile of mid-career professional facing a specific set of capability gaps. What that customer values turns out to be not courses but credentials, peer networks, and skills that translate immediately to their jobs — not the lecture format the program primarily uses. The results are not enrollment numbers but career outcomes of graduates. The plan changes significantly once these answers are in place: different formats, different content, different outcome tracking.

**Example 2: A nonprofit advocacy organization**

A health advocacy nonprofit has operated for 20 years on the mission "to advance health equity." It raises money, publishes reports, and convenes conferences. When the leadership team works through the five questions, they find that their primary customer — the low-income communities whose health they aim to improve — is rarely engaged with their work. Their customers are funders, policymakers, and other nonprofits. What those customers value is different from what would change health outcomes in communities. Their results are hard to name in terms of changed health conditions; they can name activities and reach, but not impact. The five questions reveal a significant gap between the stated mission and the actual work. The plan must address this gap directly.

## Anti-Patterns

**Treating the five questions as a visioning exercise.** Some organizations work through the questions in a facilitated offsite and produce a document. The document is presented and filed. Nothing changes. The five questions are not a visioning exercise; they are a self-examination that should produce decisions and changed behavior. If the honest answers don't require anything to change, either the answers are wrong or the organization is already performing at a very high level.

**Answering "what do customers value?" from the inside.** This is the most common error. Organizations assume they know what customers value because they know what they provide. This substitutes organizational self-perception for customer reality. The discipline is to ask customers and listen to answers that may contradict internal assumptions.

**Leaving the customer definition too broad.** "Our customers are everyone in the community" or "our customers are all stakeholders" means the organization cannot make the trade-offs required to serve anyone well. The discipline of naming a primary customer is the discipline of strategic focus. It does not mean ignoring others; it means being honest about whose transformation is the primary goal.
