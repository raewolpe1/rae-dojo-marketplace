## When to Use

Use this when an organization is defining its products, services, or strategy primarily by reference to its own capabilities, preferences, or history rather than by reference to what customers actually value. It is the right frame when someone asks "what business are we in?" when an organization is confused about why customers are not responding to an excellent product, or when a strategy review has focused primarily on internal analysis.

## Fails When

This framework fails when the organization has confused what customers say they value with what they actually value — customer research is not infallible, and customers' stated preferences often differ from their revealed preferences and their actual needs. It also fails in genuinely new-to-world markets where customers cannot yet articulate value because they have no reference point — the classic product innovation challenge. And it fails when taken to the extreme of pure customer responsiveness: an organization that only does what customers ask will never create products customers did not know to ask for.

## Core Concept

In *The Practice of Management* (1954), Drucker wrote: "There is only one valid definition of business purpose: to create a customer." This was, and remains, a provocative claim. It directly contradicted the then-dominant view that the purpose of a business was to make a profit.

Drucker did not deny that profit matters. He argued that profit is the test of business performance and the cost of remaining in business. But profit is not purpose — and confusing the two produces a series of consequential errors.

**The outside-in requirement.** If the purpose of a business is to create a customer, then value must be defined from the outside. The customer defines what value is. The customer defines what a product is. The customer defines what the business is — not the manager, the engineer, or the accountant.

This seems obvious until you examine how most organizations actually function. Most organizations define their products by their specifications, their strategies by their competencies, their priorities by their internal metrics. They describe themselves in terms of what they make rather than what they do for customers. Drucker argued this is almost always wrong.

The classic example: a steel company that defines itself as being in the steel business will be surprised by aluminum, plastics, composites, and eventually any material that does what customers used to require steel for. A company that defines itself as providing structural strength, light weight, and thermal resistance for manufacturing processes has defined itself by what customers actually value — and can track the competitive landscape correctly.

**What customers actually buy.** Drucker's related insight: customers rarely buy what they appear to buy. A woman buying lipstick, he observed, is not buying colored wax — she is buying hope. A person hiring a car is not buying transportation — they are buying independence, status, or convenience, depending on which customer in which context. The functional specification of a product and the value the customer derives from it are rarely the same thing. Organizations that understand only the functional specification are selling something that the customer experiences as something different.

**Different customers value different things.** Even within a single market, different customer segments value genuinely different things. The business customer and the consumer customer often value completely different aspects of the same product. The early adopter and the mainstream customer value different things. Drucker argued that this requires explicit analysis: who are our customers, and what do each of them actually value?

**Consequences for innovation.** If value is defined by the customer, then innovation must start with the customer, not with the laboratory. Drucker consistently argued against technology-led innovation: beginning with an interesting technology and then searching for a customer need to apply it to. The disciplined approach is the reverse: identify a customer need — especially a need the customer is not currently aware of as a need — and then develop the product or service that addresses it.

## How to Apply

1. **Define your business from the outside in.** Not by what you make or what capabilities you have, but by what customers are actually trying to accomplish when they use your product or service. Ask: what job is the customer hiring us to do? What would they use instead if we did not exist?

2. **Distinguish between what customers say and what customers do.** Survey data and customer feedback capture stated preferences. Purchasing behavior and usage patterns capture revealed preferences. The two often diverge. When designing products, strategy, or investments, give more weight to what customers actually do than to what they say they want.

3. **Segment customers by what they actually value — not by demographics.** Two customers with identical demographic profiles may value completely different things when buying the same product category. Segmentation that is meaningful for strategy is segmentation by value: which customers are buying for price, which for reliability, which for status, which for service quality?

4. **Test every product or strategy decision against a simple question:** what problem does this solve for a specific customer? If you cannot answer this question precisely — naming the customer and the problem — the decision is not yet grounded in customer reality.

5. **Re-examine the definition of value periodically.** What customers value changes. Technology changes what is possible. Competition changes what is available. Customer circumstances change what is needed. The definition of value that drove your strategy three years ago may no longer be accurate.

## Examples

**Example 1: The engineering-led product failure**

A medical device company developed a highly advanced diagnostic instrument. By every engineering measure, it was superior to competing products — more accurate, faster, more versatile. Sales were disappointing. An analysis of customer decisions found that the primary buyers — hospital procurement departments and department chiefs — were not primarily deciding on accuracy and speed. They were deciding on ease of use by relatively junior staff, service contract reliability, and compatibility with existing hospital information systems. The device's superiority on engineering dimensions was real but irrelevant to the actual purchase decision. The company had defined value as their engineers defined it. Their customers defined value differently. The corrective required product modifications (simplified interface) and commercial changes (stronger service offering), not improvements to the core technology.

**Example 2: The retailer who redefined the business**

A regional hardware retailer was competing against large national chains on product selection and price — and losing. A customer analysis revealed that most of their transactions were with homeowners undertaking home repair and improvement projects who were not confident in their ability to complete the work successfully. These customers were not primarily buying hardware — they were buying project completion with confidence. The national chains sold hardware; they could not economically deliver project confidence. The retailer redefined its business: it provided expert advice, project planning assistance, and guidance specific enough that a non-expert could complete the project. This redefinition, based on what customers actually valued, produced a competitive position the national chains could not easily replicate.

## Anti-Patterns

**Market research as confirmation of existing decisions.** Organizations frequently conduct customer research after strategic decisions have been made, seeking confirmation rather than insight. The questions asked reflect what the organization wants to hear; the research is designed to validate, not discover. Drucker's framework requires genuine openness to the possibility that customers value something other than what the organization currently provides.

**Conflating customer satisfaction with customer value.** A customer can be highly satisfied with a product and still not value it highly in the sense that matters strategically: they may use it without being deeply dependent on it, substitute freely when alternatives appear, or be primarily driven by price rather than the product's specific attributes. High satisfaction scores do not necessarily indicate a strong value proposition.

**Defining the customer as the immediate buyer.** For many organizations, the immediate buyer is not the ultimate customer. A pharmaceutical company's customer is the patient, not the physician who prescribes the drug. A textbook publisher's customer is the student, not the professor who requires the book. A business software company's customer is the end user, not the IT department that purchases the license. Defining value requires understanding what the ultimate customer — not just the immediate buyer — actually values.
