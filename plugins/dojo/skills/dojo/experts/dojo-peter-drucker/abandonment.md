## When to Use

Use this when an organization is trying to free up resources for new initiatives but cannot find where the resources will come from, when a strategy review reveals that the organization is doing too many things but cannot identify what to stop, when products, programs, or activities are consuming resources without producing results, or when an organization has grown by addition but not by subtraction. It is also the right frame whenever someone presents a new initiative without addressing what will be discontinued to fund it.

## Fails When

This framework fails when applied without adequate diagnosis — when things are abandoned because they are unfamiliar or currently unprofitable rather than because they are genuinely no longer worth doing. Not everything with a declining revenue curve should be abandoned; some things are worth maintaining. It also fails in contexts where organizational commitments or relationships make abandonment genuinely costly: long-term customer relationships, communities that depend on an institution, legal or regulatory obligations. And it fails when the question of abandonment is used as a weapon in organizational politics rather than as a genuine strategic instrument.

## Core Concept

Drucker argued throughout his career that the most important management principle was one organizations consistently ignored: the principle of systematic abandonment.

**The theory.** Organizations grow by addition. When a new opportunity emerges, they start something. When the opportunity changes, they rarely stop. Over time, the organization accumulates activities, products, programs, and commitments that reflect what seemed promising in the past — not what is most important now. The result is an organization that is perpetually short of resources for genuinely important work because resources are being consumed by yesterday's commitments.

The solution is not working harder or finding more resources. It is abandonment: systematically asking, of every activity, every product, every program, and every process — "If we were not already doing this, would we start today?"

**The question itself is diagnostic.** The question is not "is this bad?" — most things that should be abandoned are not bad. They were reasonable when started. They may still be adequate. The question is: given what we know now about our mission, our customers, and our opportunities, would we choose to start this today, allocating the resources it currently consumes? If the answer is no, then continuing it is a choice to forgo something that would actually be started today.

**Categories of candidates for abandonment.** Drucker identified several types of activities that organizations carry unnecessarily:

*Products and services that used to be important but no longer are.* Every product has a life cycle. Organizations that grow by adding new products without retiring old ones accumulate a long tail of offerings that consume selling effort, inventory, service capacity, and management attention disproportionate to their contribution.

*Programs and activities that have achieved their original purpose.* The most common reason organizations continue programs beyond their useful life is that the people running them have no interest in stopping. The program becomes an end in itself rather than a means to an end.

*Activities that have become habitual without ever having been deliberate.* Much of what organizations do was never consciously chosen — it evolved over time, became routine, and is now assumed to be necessary. The question "why do we do this?" is often met with the answer "we've always done it."

*Good ideas in the wrong place.* Some activities that would be excellent elsewhere are in the wrong organization at the wrong time. The resources they consume could be deployed better.

**Abandonment and innovation.** Drucker insisted on the connection between abandonment and innovation. Innovation requires resources — people, money, management attention. In most organizations, those resources are fully committed. The only way to free resources for the genuinely new is to stop doing something old. An organization that cannot abandon cannot innovate, because it cannot free the inputs innovation requires.

He argued for establishing what he called a "systematic abandonment review" as a regular management discipline — not a one-time restructuring but an ongoing process of asking whether every major commitment still passes the test of deliberate choice.

## How to Apply

1. **Apply the abandonment question to every major activity on a regular schedule.** The question is: "If we were not already doing this, would we start today, given what we know and what we need?" This is not a rhetorical exercise — it requires an honest answer, which is harder than it sounds because people are attached to things they built.

2. **Separate the abandonment question from the performance review.** The abandonment question is not "is this performing well?" Something can be performing adequately and still not deserve the resources it is consuming relative to alternatives. The question is prospective — about the future allocation of resources — not a judgment on past performance.

3. **Require every proposal for a new initiative to include a proposed abandonment.** This is the most practical implementation of the principle. When someone proposes adding a new product, program, or activity, ask: what will we stop doing to fund this? If the answer is "nothing," the organization is implicitly asking for more resources than it has. If the answer is something specific, that trade-off becomes visible and can be evaluated.

4. **Identify the "living dead" — activities that are not failing enough to kill, not succeeding enough to grow.** These are the most dangerous items in any organization's portfolio because they consume real resources without producing sufficient results or growth, and they rarely fail visibly. A regular portfolio review should explicitly surface these and make a deliberate decision about them.

5. **When abandoning, be clean and quick.** Half-abandonment — reducing investment while maintaining the activity — is usually worse than full abandonment or full continuation. It produces an organization that cannot do the abandoned thing well (too few resources) and cannot redirect the resources to something better (too many commitments). Make the decision and implement it fully.

## Examples

**Example 1: The product portfolio problem**

A consumer goods company had accumulated 340 stock-keeping units across its product lines, the result of 20 years of additions with almost no retirements. The 20 largest SKUs generated 85% of revenue. The remaining 320 generated 15% of revenue while consuming a disproportionate share of manufacturing capacity, inventory capital, and sales force attention. The top sales representatives avoided the low-volume SKUs — too complex, too little reward. But the products remained because each had an internal champion who argued that it served an important niche. A systematic abandonment review, applying the test "would we start this today?", identified 180 SKUs for retirement. The resource freed up — in manufacturing capacity, capital, and sales attention — was redirected to line extensions of the top performers. Revenue per SKU increased 60% over three years. The "important niches" that had been cited as justification for the retired products were either not actually important, were served adequately by the remaining products, or were served by competitors who were welcome to them.

**Example 2: The nonprofit's program accumulation**

A community development nonprofit had been operating for 30 years. Its original mission had been to provide housing to low-income families. Over three decades, it had added programs: financial literacy, job training, childcare, health referrals, legal services, food security, and community organizing. Each program had been added in response to a genuine need. Each was staffed by people committed to the work. The organization had also become unable to hire for its core housing mission because resources were spread too thin and management attention was fragmented across seven program areas. The abandonment review produced a painful finding: several programs, while genuinely valuable, were not distinctive — other organizations in the community could provide them as well or better. The question was not whether these programs were good but whether this organization was the right provider. Three programs were transferred to partner organizations. Two were discontinued. The resources — money, staff, management attention — were redirected to the housing mission. Within two years, the organization had doubled its housing production. It had not grown its resources; it had stopped doing things it should not have been doing.

## Anti-Patterns

**Abandoning by neglect rather than decision.** Organizations frequently allow activities to wither — reducing investment gradually until they effectively end — rather than making an explicit abandonment decision. This is costlier: the activity consumes resources throughout the process of withering, prevents clear reallocation, and fails to develop organizational discipline about making hard choices.

**Treating abandonment as punishment.** When abandonment is communicated as a judgment on the people involved — implying they failed, wasted resources, or made bad decisions — it creates an organizational environment where past activities are defended rather than honestly evaluated. The frame should be: this was appropriate at the time and is no longer the right use of resources. The people who ran it are not blamed for the organization's changed circumstances.

**Abandoning in order to avoid making the hard decision about what to start.** Some organizations become skilled at stopping things but not at redirecting the freed resources to genuinely better uses. Abandonment is only valuable if the resources released are actually invested in something with better prospects. Abandonment as a cost-reduction measure without a corresponding growth agenda produces a smaller, not a better, organization.
