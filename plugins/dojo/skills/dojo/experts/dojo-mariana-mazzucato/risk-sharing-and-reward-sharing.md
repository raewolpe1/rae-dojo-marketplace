# Risk-Sharing and Reward-Sharing

## When to Use
- When designing public investment in innovation, infrastructure, or industrial development.
- When structuring public-private partnerships.
- When debating who benefits from state-funded innovation upside.

## Fails When
- **The transaction costs of reward-capture exceed the upside captured** — for small investments, reward-sharing may not be worth the design effort.

## Core Concept

When the state bears risk through R&D investment, early-stage finance, public procurement guarantees, or other risk-bearing instruments, it should share in the upside when the investment succeeds. The current pattern — public risk, private reward — is unjust, under-funds future state risk-bearing capacity, and embeds value extraction.

The asymmetry is well-documented. Tesla received over $400 million in DOE loans during its early period; the US government received no equity. The mRNA vaccine platform was funded by decades of NIH research; the resulting pharmaceutical company profits accrued to private holders. DARPA-funded technologies built the iPhone; Apple shareholders capture the upside. The state bears the early-stage risk, the private actors commercialise and capture the reward, and the state receives only corporate income tax (often minimised through transfer pricing and tax planning).

Reward-sharing mechanisms include:

**Equity stakes.** When the state funds risky early-stage development, take an equity position. Norway's sovereign wealth fund holds equity globally. Singapore's Temasek holds strategic equity. South Africa's IDC and PIC hold equity. The mechanism is established; the question is scale and design.

**Conditional grants and loans.** State funding that converts to equity or royalty obligations if the firm succeeds. The structure aligns the public return with the firm's success.

**Royalty arrangements.** State-funded research produces IP that the commercialising firm uses under royalty agreement. The state captures a share of commercial success.

**Public ownership shares.** Some innovations should produce shared public assets — e.g. public ownership of climate-critical infrastructure, public stakes in foundational technologies.

**Outcome-based contracts.** Public procurement that pays for outcomes (e.g. vaccine doses with effectiveness guarantees) rather than inputs aligns provider incentives.

For developing countries: structure state innovation and infrastructure investment with reward-sharing built in. The institutional architecture (IDC, PIC, sector-specific funds) should be authorised and equipped to take equity positions and structure conditional finance.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify state risk-bearing** in proposed investments.
2. **Specify reward-sharing mechanisms** matched to the investment type.
3. **Build the institutional capacity** for state to hold equity, royalty, and conditional positions.
4. **Make the reward-sharing public** to defend the principle politically.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's renewable energy R&D investments and the IDC's industrial finance.

**Application:** Where the IDC funds high-risk early-stage renewable energy or battery technology firms, take equity stakes proportionate to the risk borne. Where the state funds research that produces commercialisable IP, structure royalty arrangements. Where REIPPPP rounds support solar PV manufacturing with public procurement guarantees, condition the support on local content and IP-sharing.

**Result:** Public risk matched with public reward capture, building future state risk-bearing capacity.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat reward-sharing as anti-business.
**Why:** It is anti-asymmetry. Genuine private value creation generates legitimate returns; the share between public and private should reflect the actual contributions.

**Don't:** Use reward-sharing to politicise specific investment decisions.
**Why:** The mechanism should be structural, not case-by-case political negotiation.
