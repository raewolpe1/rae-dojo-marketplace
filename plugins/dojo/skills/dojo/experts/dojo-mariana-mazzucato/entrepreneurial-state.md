# The Entrepreneurial State

## When to Use
- When the framing positions the private sector as the source of innovation.
- When justifying public investment in transformative technology.
- When designing the institutional architecture for state-led innovation.

## Fails When
- **The historical examples are misapplied** to contexts where they don't translate.

## Core Concept

The entrepreneurial state thesis (Mazzucato, 2013) is the empirically grounded proposition that the state has been, and remains, the primary risk-bearing entrepreneurial actor in transformative innovation. The orthodox framing — private actors innovate, the state corrects market failures — is empirically false for most transformative technologies.

The evidence is dense. The internet was built by DARPA. GPS was a US military system. Touch screen technology came out of NSF and DOD-funded research. Voice recognition (Siri) emerged from DARPA-funded SRI research. The human genome project was a public initiative. The mRNA vaccine platform was built on decades of NIH and German federal funding. The Tesla story is funded by US DOE loans. The Solyndra failure is part of the same portfolio. Across pharmaceuticals, electronics, biotechnology, clean energy, and aerospace, the state has been the actor that bore early-stage uncertainty.

The implications:

**Public investment is not a substitute for or a complement to private innovation; it is the originator** of the high-uncertainty research that private actors later commercialise.

**The state has innovation capabilities** when properly resourced — DARPA, NASA, NIH, the Apollo programme, the Manhattan Project, the wartime industrial mobilisation. The "government can't innovate" claim is not supported by the historical record.

**The risk-and-reward structure should reflect the state's risk-bearing role.** Currently the state bears risk and private actors capture reward. Equity stakes, conditional grants, royalty arrangements, public ownership shares would correct this.

For developing countries, the entrepreneurial state framing legitimises state-led investment in transformative areas — renewable energy, biotechnology, advanced manufacturing — and frames the institutional design question as how to build state innovation capability, not whether to.

## How to Apply

1. **Recognise the state's existing innovation contribution** in the country's actual technological base.
2. **Build state innovation institutions** with the resources, talent, and political insulation to act entrepreneurially.
3. **Position state investment vehicles** to bear early-stage risk that private capital will not.
4. **Capture upside** when state-funded innovation succeeds commercially.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's R&D investment in renewable energy and battery storage technology.

**Application:** The state should be positioned as the primary investor in early-stage research and the major customer for early commercial deployments through public procurement. CSIR, universities, IDC, and dedicated state vehicles should be funded for sustained patient investment. Equity stakes in commercial spin-offs capture upside.

**Result:** A state-led innovation investment in a strategic transition area, with upside-capture mechanisms.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the entrepreneurial state as an excuse for poorly-designed state intervention.
**Why:** The historical cases (DARPA, NIH, NASA) had specific design features. Replicating the function requires replicating the design.

**Don't:** Use the framing to dismiss the private sector's commercialisation role.
**Why:** Both matter. The argument is about recognition of state contribution, not about displacement of private contribution.
