# The Big Con (Consulting Industry)

## When to Use
- When evaluating proposed engagement with major consulting firms.
- When debating outsourcing of public-sector strategy or implementation work.
- When assessing organisational capability impacts of consulting reliance.

## Fails When
- **Specific consulting work is genuinely necessary** — short-term specialised expertise. The critique is of the structural pattern, not all consulting.

## Core Concept

*The Big Con* (Mazzucato and Collington, 2023) documents the consulting industry's role in extracting value from organisations while undermining their internal capabilities and democratic accountability. The pattern is structural — not the result of bad individual consultants but of the industry's business model and the conditions of contemporary public and private organisations.

The pattern includes:

**Strategy extraction.** Consultants are hired to develop strategies that organisations should be developing internally. The strategic capability migrates out of the organisation; subsequent decisions require renewed consulting engagement.

**Capability erosion.** When strategy and major implementation are outsourced, internal staff are denied the experience of doing the work. Over time, the organisation cannot do the work without external help.

**Lock-in.** Organisations that have outsourced strategy and implementation cannot easily reverse the dependence. Consultants design subsequent engagements, embed proprietary methods, and create switching costs.

**Conflict of interest.** Major firms advise both regulators and the firms they regulate; both governments and the corporations they engage; competing players in the same market. The conflicts are managed through internal Chinese walls of limited credibility.

**Privatisation of public knowledge.** Public-sector knowledge developed during consulting engagements becomes consultant IP, available to subsequent commercial clients but not freely to other public entities.

**Democratic accountability erosion.** Major decisions developed in consulting engagements are removed from democratic deliberation; the consultant outputs are presented to elected officials as technical and beyond political contest.

The major consulting firms (McKinsey, BCG, Bain, Deloitte, Accenture, PwC, EY, KPMG, and others) have built businesses substantially on this pattern, with combined revenues in the hundreds of billions globally. The pattern is most damaging in developing-country governments where public-sector capability is most fragile.

The corrective:

**Reduce consulting dependence.** Build internal capability rather than outsourcing repeatedly to consultants.

**Restrict consultant access to strategic decisions.** Consultants for specific specialised tasks, not for core strategy.

**Address conflicts of interest** through structural separation of the firms across client types.

**Transparency** — disclose consulting contracts, scopes, deliverables, costs.

**Public ownership of consulting outputs** — knowledge generated in public-sector engagements should be available across public-sector entities.

For South Africa specifically: state capture and post-capture rebuilding has involved substantial consulting engagement. Some of it was complicit in capture; some has been part of recovery. The forward path requires reducing consulting dependence while rebuilding internal public-sector capability.

## How to Apply

1. **Audit consulting dependence** in the organisation or government area.
2. **Identify the capability that should be internal** rather than outsourced.
3. **Plan capability building** — recruitment, training, retention, organisational design.
4. **Reduce consulting scope** progressively as internal capability grows.
5. **Address conflicts of interest** in remaining consulting engagement.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African government department relies heavily on major consulting firms for both strategy and major implementation.

**Application:** The dependence has eroded internal capability over time. Reform programme: identify the strategic capabilities that should be internal; recruit and retain technical talent; reduce consulting scope to specialised short-term tasks; build institutional knowledge management; address conflicts of interest in remaining engagements.

**Result:** A department building capability rather than rebuilding dependence.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat all consulting as illegitimate.
**Why:** Some specialised short-term consulting is valuable. The critique is structural, not categorical.

**Don't:** Imagine capability building is quick.
**Why:** It requires sustained investment over years. The dependence took years to build; reversal takes years.
