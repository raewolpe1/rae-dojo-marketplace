# Mission-Oriented Innovation

## When to Use
- When designing innovation or industrial policy that should drive transformation.
- When orthogonal sectoral policies are dispersing effort.
- When responding to climate, social, or developmental challenges that require concentrated effort.

## Fails When
- **The mission is too vague to coordinate effort.** Bad mission design wastes the framework.
- **The political coalition for the mission collapses** before delivery.

## Core Concept

Mission-oriented innovation policy organises industrial and innovation effort around specific, ambitious, societal missions — climate transition, healthcare transformation, sustainable cities, food sovereignty — rather than around generic sector targeting or horizontal innovation support.

Five design features:

**Bold and inspirational.** "Decarbonise our energy supply by 2050" mobilises effort across sectors; "support clean technology" does not.

**Measurable and time-bound.** Specific milestones at specific dates enable accountability and progress tracking.

**Cross-sectoral.** Missions cut across industries, sectors, and government departments. Climate transition affects electricity, transport, buildings, industry, agriculture, finance, and labour simultaneously.

**Bottom-up experimentation.** Many actors, many approaches, learning across them. The mission sets the direction; the actors innovate paths.

**Mission-oriented procurement.** State purchasing power directed toward suppliers advancing the mission, with performance conditions on procurement.

The Apollo programme is the paradigm — get a person to the moon and back by the end of the decade. The mission was bold, measurable, time-bound, cross-sectoral (rocketry, electronics, materials, software, life support), enabled experimentation, and used procurement (NASA contracts) to drive supplier capability development. The technology spillovers (electronics, materials, software) reshaped the broader economy.

Contemporary missions could include: carbon neutrality by mid-century; universal access to clean energy; halving global cancer mortality; clean oceans; sustainable food systems for growing urban populations.

For South Africa, mission candidates include: green energy transition with domestic manufacturing capability; comprehensive social transition (employment, education quality, basic services); regional development for spatial transformation; agricultural productivity for food security and African market integration.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify 2–4 missions** matched to country challenges and capabilities.
2. **Specify them boldly, measurably, time-bound.**
3. **Coordinate cross-sectoral action** toward each mission.
4. **Use procurement as a mission instrument.**
5. **Enable experimentation** under the mission umbrella.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's Just Energy Transition.

**Application:** Frame as a mission — "decarbonise electricity by 2050 while building domestic renewable manufacturing and providing a just transition for workers and communities." Cross-sectoral coordination: electricity sector restructuring + renewable manufacturing industrial policy + skill development + regional development + finance + procurement. Bottom-up experimentation across technology pathways. Procurement conditions on local content and capability development.

**Result:** A mission-coherent JET strategy rather than fragmented energy and industrial policies.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Substitute slogans for missions.
**Why:** "Innovation-led growth" is not a mission. Missions are specific deliverables.

**Don't:** Try to run too many missions simultaneously.
**Why:** Concentration is the point. Three missions delivered are better than ten missions launched.
