# Market-Shaping vs Market-Fixing

## When to Use
- When the policy debate is framed as whether to "intervene" in markets.
- When climate or innovation policy is being designed.
- When the state is being positioned as a residual market-failure-corrector.

## Fails When
- **The market is functioning well for the purpose in question** — over-intervention is real and the framework does not deny it.

## Core Concept

The orthodox economic framework positions the state as a market-failure-corrector. Markets are presumed efficient absent specific failures (externalities, public goods, information asymmetries, monopoly); the state's role is to identify and correct these failures, then step back. This is the **market-fixing** framing.

The market-shaping framing positions the state as an active director of market activity toward public-purpose goals. The state does not wait for market failures; it sets strategic directions (climate transition, healthcare transformation, sustainable cities), creates demand for innovation in those directions, bears risk that private capital will not, and builds capabilities required for the direction. Markets respond to state direction rather than the state responding to market failures.

The distinction matters because the market-fixing framing is inadequate to challenges like climate transition. Carbon pricing is the orthodox climate policy — fix the externality and let markets work. The empirical experience is that carbon pricing alone has been too slow, too contested, and too partial to drive the transition at required pace. The market-shaping alternative — set decarbonisation missions, organise public investment, use procurement to create demand for renewables and clean technology, build state-led patient capital, coordinate cross-sectoral capability development — drives transition more rapidly.

The market-shaping framing also reframes innovation policy. Market-fixing innovation policy supports R&D as an externality correction (firms underinvest because they cannot capture all returns). Market-shaping innovation policy directs innovation toward missions through procurement, patient capital, and demand creation.

For developing countries, market-shaping is particularly important because market-fixing assumes a robust market structure that exists in advanced economies but is partial in developing ones. Many developing-country sectors are not market-failure-correctable problems; they are market-creation problems requiring active state shaping.

## How to Apply

1. **Specify the direction the state should shape markets toward** — mission, sector, capability.
2. **Use procurement, patient capital, demand creation, and coordination** as shaping instruments.
3. **Build state capability** to direct and engage, not just to regulate.
4. **Combine shaping with appropriate market mechanisms** — the framework is additive, not exclusive.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's energy transition policy is being designed primarily through carbon pricing and renewable energy auctions.

**Application:** Market-fixing alone is inadequate. The market-shaping addition: green industrial policy creating demand for domestic renewable manufacturing; state investment in transmission and battery storage capability; mission-oriented procurement requiring local content; patient capital for high-uncertainty technologies (green hydrogen, advanced batteries); state direction-setting on the pace and pathway of the transition.

**Result:** A market-shaping climate policy that complements market-fixing instruments rather than relying on them alone.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use market-shaping to bypass market accountability mechanisms.
**Why:** State direction-setting still requires discipline — performance conditions, sunset, public accountability.

**Don't:** Position market-shaping as opposed to market mechanisms.
**Why:** It works through market mechanisms (procurement, finance, demand) toward state-set directions. The framework is additive.
