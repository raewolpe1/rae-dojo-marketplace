# Praise and Feedback

## When to Use
- When someone asks how to praise children, students, employees, or colleagues effectively.
- When feedback practices are reinforcing fixed mindset without the giver being aware.
- When the mechanism by which praise shapes mindset needs to be explained.

## Core Concept
Dweck's research on praise produced one of her most striking and counter-intuitive findings. In a series of studies with Claudia Mueller, children who were praised for intelligence ("You must be smart at this") after succeeding on a moderately difficult task subsequently chose easier tasks, enjoyed the work less, performed worse on harder tasks, and were more likely to lie about their scores — compared with children who were praised for effort ("You must have worked really hard"). The praise was a single sentence. The effects were dramatic.

The mechanism is direct. When you tell a child "you're so smart," you are providing an explanation for their success — and the explanation is a fixed trait. The child learns: my success comes from being smart. This creates an immediate vulnerability: if I fail, does that mean I'm not smart? The child's rational response is to avoid failure — choose easier tasks, don't risk the identity. When you tell a child "you worked hard on that," you provide a different explanation: my success comes from my effort. This creates resilience: if I fail, the explanation is that I need to work harder or try a different strategy — both of which are within my control.

Person praise ties identity to performance. "You're so smart," "you're a natural," "you're talented" — all of these explain success through fixed traits and create fragility. Process praise ties behaviour to outcomes. "You tried different strategies until you found one that worked," "I noticed you went back and checked your reasoning," "you really stuck with that even when it got difficult" — these explain success through controllable actions and create agency.

The principle extends beyond children. Managers who tell employees "you're a star" create the same fragility as parents who tell children "you're so smart." The employee begins to protect star status rather than take developmental risks. Leaders who say "I really valued how you approached that problem — you identified the gap in the data, brought in the right expertise, and adapted when your first model didn't work" reinforce the process that produced the result rather than creating an identity that needs defending.

Feedback follows the same logic. Fixed-mindset feedback evaluates the person: "This is excellent work" or "this isn't up to standard." Growth-mindset feedback evaluates the work with developmental direction: "The analysis here is strong — the methodology section needs more rigour. Here's what I'd suggest."

## How to Apply
1. **Praise the process, not the person.** What did they do — what strategy, effort, persistence, adaptation, help-seeking — that produced the result?
2. **Give feedback that is specific and developmental.** Not "this is good" or "this needs work" but "this specific element works because... and this specific element could be strengthened by..."
3. **Avoid talent explanations for success.** "You're a natural" feels wonderful and creates fragility. "You clearly put in the preparation" feels less dramatic and creates resilience.
4. **Match praise to reality.** Don't praise effort that wasn't effective. Don't praise process that didn't produce learning. Praise should be honest, not formulaic.

## Examples
**Situation:** A team leader wants to recognise a colleague who delivered an excellent evaluation report.
**Application:** The instinct is to say "You're such a strong writer — this report is brilliant." Dweck would advise a different approach: "Notice the difference between 'you're a brilliant writer' and 'this report works because you structured the findings around the three questions the commissioner actually needs answered, you used the field data to challenge the assumptions in the logframe, and you made the recommendations specific enough to act on.' The first version feels great but creates pressure to be brilliant next time. The second version tells the person exactly what they did right — so they can do it again, and so the rest of the team can learn from the example. The best praise is praise that teaches."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Ban all acknowledgment of talent or strength.
**Why:** Dweck's point is not that you should never recognise ability. It is that you should not make ability the primary explanation for success. Saying "you have a real talent for this — and I can see how much work you've put into developing it" is fine. Saying "you're a natural" without acknowledging the effort is the problem.

**Don't:** Make process praise mechanical or insincere.
**Why:** If people feel they're receiving a formula rather than genuine recognition, the praise loses its power. Process praise must be specific, honest, and observant.
