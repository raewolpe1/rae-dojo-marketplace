# Mindset in Organisations

## When to Use
- When someone is trying to build a learning culture in their team or organisation.
- When organisational practices are inadvertently reinforcing fixed mindset.
- When the distinction between a "genius culture" and a "development culture" needs to be drawn.

## Core Concept
Dweck's research with Mary Murphy showed that organisations, like individuals, develop mindset cultures. In a fixed-mindset organisation — what Dweck and Murphy call a "culture of genius" — the implicit belief is that talent is the primary currency. The organisation hires for innate brilliance, rewards star performers, and treats struggle as evidence that someone was a bad hire. In a growth-mindset organisation — a "culture of development" — the implicit belief is that people can grow. The organisation invests in development, rewards improvement and learning, and treats mistakes as opportunities for systemic improvement.

These cultures produce measurably different outcomes. Murphy's research found that employees in fixed-mindset organisations reported less trust, less collaboration, more anxiety about evaluation, more cutting of corners, and more gaming of metrics. Employees in growth-mindset organisations reported greater trust, more willingness to take risks, more collaboration, more honest reporting of problems, and more genuine innovation. The fixed-mindset culture optimises for the appearance of competence. The growth-mindset culture optimises for actual development.

The critical insight is that organisational mindset is set by systems, not by slogans. You cannot create a growth-mindset culture by putting "We believe in growth" on the wall while maintaining a performance management system that punishes risk-taking, a hiring process that worships credentials, a leadership style that hides mistakes, and a feedback culture where honest criticism is career suicide. The systems tell people what the organisation actually believes, regardless of what it says it believes.

Four systems are particularly diagnostic. First, how does the organisation respond to mistakes? If mistakes are hidden, punished, or blamed, the culture is fixed-mindset regardless of official messaging. If mistakes are analysed, learned from, and shared as improvement opportunities, the culture is growth-mindset. Second, how does the organisation hire? If hiring optimises for current brilliance ("we only hire the best"), the culture signals fixed mindset. If hiring also values learning potential, curiosity, and resilience, the culture signals growth. Third, how does feedback flow? If feedback is a judgment (performance ratings, forced rankings), fixed mindset. If feedback is a development tool (regular coaching, specific behavioural input), growth mindset. Fourth, what does leadership model? If leaders perform infallibility, fixed mindset. If leaders share their own learning journeys and acknowledge their mistakes, growth mindset.

## How to Apply
1. **Audit the systems.** Look at hiring, feedback, error response, and promotion criteria. These reveal the actual mindset culture more accurately than official values.
2. **Start with leadership behaviour.** Leaders who model vulnerability about their own learning set the tone for the entire organisation.
3. **Reward learning, not just outcomes.** Create mechanisms to recognise when teams learn from failure, try new approaches, or develop new capabilities.
4. **Make mistakes safe to report.** If people fear career consequences for honest reporting of problems, no amount of growth-mindset messaging will change the culture.

## Examples
**Situation:** An NGO director says "We say we value innovation, but people keep doing things the same way."
**Application:** This is almost certainly a systems problem, not a people problem. Dweck would say: "Your people are rational. They look at what actually gets rewarded and what actually gets punished, and they behave accordingly. If you say you value innovation but promote the people who deliver predictable results, if you say you want experimentation but punish projects that fail, if you say you want new ideas but evaluate people on whether they hit pre-set targets — then you have a fixed-mindset culture that uses growth-mindset language. The fix is not to tell people to be more innovative. The fix is to change what you reward. Dedicate a portion of evaluation to what people learned and tried, not just what they delivered. Celebrate a failed experiment that produced useful knowledge. Promote someone who took a smart risk that didn't pay off. Your people will innovate when innovating is actually safe."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Implement growth-mindset culture through workshops alone.
**Why:** A workshop that tells people to embrace growth mindset while the performance management system punishes risk is worse than doing nothing — it creates cynicism.

**Don't:** Assume growth-mindset culture means no accountability.
**Why:** Growth-mindset organisations still hold high standards. The difference is that they combine high standards with high support — they expect excellent work and provide the development to get there.
