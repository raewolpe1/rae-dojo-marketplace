# Fixed Mindset

## When to Use
- When someone is displaying fixed-mindset patterns and would benefit from recognising them.
- When the internal logic and consequences of entity theory need to be explained.
- When the protective function of the fixed mindset needs to be honoured before it can be addressed.

## Core Concept
The fixed mindset — entity theory — is the belief that intelligence, talent, and ability are fixed traits. You have a certain amount, and your job is to demonstrate that you have enough. This belief creates a specific motivational architecture. If ability is fixed, then performance becomes a measure of your permanent worth. Every test, every project, every evaluation becomes a referendum on who you are. Success confirms your adequacy. Failure reveals your inadequacy. The stakes of every performance are existential.

This architecture produces characteristic behaviours. People operating from a fixed mindset tend to avoid challenges they might fail at, because failure would expose a lack of ability. They give up quickly when things get difficult, because struggle is evidence of inadequacy — if they were truly talented, it would come easily. They see effort as a sign that they don't have enough natural ability. They feel threatened by the success of others, because in a fixed-ability world, someone else's success diminishes their own standing. They ignore or become defensive about critical feedback, because feedback that identifies weaknesses feels like an attack on their identity.

Dweck emphasises that the fixed mindset is not stupidity or laziness. It is a coherent belief system that produces internally logical behaviour. If you genuinely believe ability is fixed, then avoiding challenge is rational — why risk exposing a permanent deficiency? If you believe effort signals low ability, then not trying hard is self-protective — you can always tell yourself you would have succeeded if you had really tried. The fixed mindset is a strategy for protecting self-worth in a world where worth is defined by innate talent.

The fixed mindset often develops in environments where performance is heavily evaluated, talent is praised, and failure is punished or shamed. Children who are repeatedly told "you're so smart" learn that their identity is their intelligence — and they become unwilling to risk that identity by attempting anything they might fail at. The very praise intended to build confidence creates fragility.

## How to Apply
1. **Recognise the pattern without labelling the person.** "That sounds like a fixed-mindset response" is more useful than "You have a fixed mindset."
2. **Honour the protective function.** The fixed mindset developed for a reason. Ask: what is this belief protecting the person from?
3. **Identify the specific domain.** Fixed mindset is rarely global. Someone may have a growth mindset about their cooking and a fixed mindset about their public speaking.
4. **Look for the underlying fear.** The fixed mindset is almost always driven by fear — of exposure, of being judged, of being found inadequate.

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme manager refuses to take on a new type of project, saying "That's not my area — I'd just make a mess of it."
**Application:** The refusal is internally logical if ability is fixed. Taking on unfamiliar work creates a high risk of visible struggle, and struggle would expose inadequacy. The safe strategy is to stay within proven competence. Dweck would say: "Notice what you're doing. You're defining 'your area' as the things you're already good at, and you're treating the boundary as permanent. But how did your current area become your area? You learned it. There was a time when programme management was unfamiliar too. The question is not whether this new project type is your area right now — of course it isn't. The question is whether you're willing to go through the learning process again. And if the answer is no, I'd want to understand what you're protecting yourself from."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Shame people for having a fixed mindset.
**Why:** Shaming someone for a fixed-mindset response is itself a fixed-mindset move — you're treating their current orientation as a permanent character flaw rather than a belief that can change.

**Don't:** Suggest that recognising the fixed mindset immediately resolves it.
**Why:** Awareness is necessary but not sufficient. The fixed mindset is an entrenched pattern that requires ongoing work, not a one-time insight.
