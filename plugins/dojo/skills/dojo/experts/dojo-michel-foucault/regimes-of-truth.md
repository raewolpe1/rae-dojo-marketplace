# Regimes of Truth

## When to Use
- When analysing the institutional rules that determine what counts as evidence, what counts as knowledge, and who is authorised to produce truth.
- When examining why certain methods, frameworks, and findings carry authority while others do not.
- When the question is about the politics of evidence — not whether evidence is true but what system determines what counts as true.

## Core Concept
A regime of truth is Foucault's term for the ensemble of rules, procedures, and mechanisms through which a society or institution distinguishes true statements from false ones and attaches specific mechanisms of power to truth production. Every society has its regime of truth — its general politics of truth: the types of discourse it accepts as true, the mechanisms through which truth is produced, the status of those who are charged with saying what counts as true.

This is not relativism. Foucault does not argue that truth does not exist or that all claims are equally valid. He argues that truth is produced — through specific institutional mechanisms, by specific authorised producers, according to specific procedural rules — and that the production of truth is always entangled with power. The peer review process, the clinical trial protocol, the evaluation methodology — all are mechanisms through which truth is produced. They are not neutral filters that separate true from false. They are productive apparatuses that determine what kinds of truth can be produced, by whom, in what form.

For M&E practice, this analysis is central. The field has its own regime of truth. What counts as a valid evaluation finding? One produced by a qualified evaluator, using an accepted methodology, presented in an accepted format, validated by an accepted review process. These rules are not arbitrary — they produce genuinely useful knowledge. But they are also not neutral — they privilege certain kinds of knowledge producers (credentialed evaluators over community members), certain kinds of methods (quantitative over narrative), certain kinds of presentation (the executive summary over the community story), and certain kinds of validation (peer review over community verification).

The practical question is not "should we abandon our regime of truth?" but "what does our regime of truth make visible and what does it hide?" If the evidence standards systematically exclude community knowledge, local knowledge, and experiential knowledge, the regime of truth is not merely incomplete — it is exercising power by determining whose experience counts as evidence and whose does not.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the regime.** "What are the rules that determine what counts as evidence in this context? Who is authorised to produce it? What methods are accepted? What format must it take?"
2. **Ask what is excluded.** "What kinds of knowledge do these rules exclude? Community testimony? Qualitative narrative? Indigenous knowledge? Embodied experience? The excluded is not merely absent — it is actively ruled out."
3. **Trace the authority.** "Who is authorised to say what is true? The evaluator? The academic? The government statistician? What gives them this authority? And who is denied it?"
4. **Examine the procedures.** "Through what procedures is truth validated? Peer review? Statistical significance? Management approval? Each procedure is a power mechanism that shapes what truth can look like."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme evaluation finds strong qualitative evidence of community-level change — social cohesion, collective action, shifts in gender norms — but limited quantitative evidence of change in the pre-specified indicators. The donor requests a "more rigorous" evaluation.
**Application:** "The donor's request reveals the regime of truth operating in this institutional context. 'Rigour' here does not mean 'accuracy' — the qualitative evidence may be entirely accurate. 'Rigour' means 'produced by the methods our regime of truth recognises as valid.' The pre-specified quantitative indicators are truth within this regime. The qualitative evidence of community-level change is not — or is truth of a lesser order, relegated to an annex or a 'lessons learned' section. The evaluator faces a choice that is simultaneously methodological and political: either translate the community-level change into quantifiable terms (which will distort it) or argue for the validity of qualitative evidence within an institutional regime that ranks it below quantitative evidence (which may not succeed). Neither option is satisfactory. The deeper question is: why does this regime of truth rank numbers above narratives? Whose interests does that ranking serve? It serves the interests of aggregation (numbers aggregate, stories do not), comparison (numbers can be compared across programmes, stories cannot), and accountability (numbers fit into performance frameworks, stories do not). These are legitimate institutional needs — but they are institutional needs, not epistemic truths."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use regimes of truth to argue that science is just politics.
**Why:** Foucault's argument is more precise. Science produces genuine knowledge — but it does so through institutional mechanisms that are entangled with power. The knowledge is real. The conditions of its production are political. Both statements are true simultaneously.

**Don't:** Suggest that abolishing evidence standards would be liberation.
**Why:** Evidence standards serve real functions — they prevent charlatanism, ensure minimally competent methodology, and enable institutional learning. The task is to examine what the standards produce and exclude, not to abolish them.
