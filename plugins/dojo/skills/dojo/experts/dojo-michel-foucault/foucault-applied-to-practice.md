# Foucault Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When someone wants to move from reading Foucault to applying his analysis in their professional work.
- When the question is "how does this help me do better development work?" rather than "how does this help me critique development work?"
- When navigating the tension between critical analysis and practical engagement.

## Core Concept
The applied Foucault is not a Foucault who prescribes. He is a Foucault who diagnoses — and diagnosis, done well, changes practice. The practitioner who understands that their indicator governs rather than merely measures will design indicators differently. The evaluator who understands that their evaluation is a power practice will conduct evaluations differently. The consultant who understands that their report produces reality rather than merely describing it will write reports differently. The change is not in the technique — it is in the practitioner's relationship to the technique.

Five practical applications are particularly relevant for development and M&E consulting.

First, reflexive indicator design. Every indicator exercises power — it makes something visible and something invisible. The applied Foucauldian does not abandon indicators but designs them with awareness: what does this indicator govern? What does it make invisible? Can additional indicators illuminate what this one hides? Can qualitative data capture what quantitative indicators exclude?

Second, power-aware evaluation design. The evaluation is a power practice — it determines what counts as success, whose knowledge counts as evidence, and what narrative reaches the decision-maker. The applied Foucauldian designs evaluations that make these power dynamics visible rather than hiding them: who defined the evaluation questions? Whose methods are used? Whose voice dominates the findings? Can the evaluation include a reflexive analysis of its own power effects?

Third, institutional honesty. The consultant who understands institutional power does not pretend that the reporting template is neutral. They name the governance: this template requires us to report in this way, which makes visible these aspects and hides those aspects. This naming does not abolish the power — but it makes it contestable.

Fourth, listening for counter-conduct. What the programme calls "non-compliance" or "low uptake" may be counter-conduct — the governed refusing to be governed in the prescribed way. The applied Foucauldian listens to resistance as information about the programme's governmentality rather than as evidence of beneficiary deficiency.

Fifth, ethical self-examination. Late Foucault's care of the self is not narcissism — it is the ongoing practice of examining one's own position within power relations. The consultant who regularly asks "what power am I exercising? whose interests does my analysis serve? what am I failing to see?" is practising Foucauldian ethics.

## How to Apply
1. **Add a power audit to evaluation design.** "Before designing the evaluation, ask: who is authorised to define the questions? Whose methods are privileged? Whose voice will dominate the findings? Document these power dynamics alongside the methodology."
2. **Design indicators in pairs.** "For every quantitative indicator, consider what qualitative counterpart would capture what the number hides. Not as decoration but as a genuine epistemic complement."
3. **Name the governance.** "In reports, name what the reporting format requires you to do: 'This template requires linear causality. The programme's actual effects are more complex.' This honesty does not weaken the report — it strengthens it."
4. **Listen to non-compliance.** "When beneficiaries do not comply, before diagnosing deficiency, ask: what are they telling us about our programme's governmentality? What governance are they resisting?"
5. **Practise evening review.** "At the end of each working day, ask: what power did I exercise today? Whose knowledge did I privilege? What did I fail to see? This is not guilt — it is craft."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant is designing a mid-term review for a governance programme funded by a bilateral donor. The donor has provided terms of reference focused on output delivery and value for money. The programme team wants the review to capture more nuanced governance changes.
**Application:** "You are navigating between two governmentalities. The donor's terms of reference embody a governmental rationality of accountability — resources in, measurable outputs out, value demonstrated. The programme team's desire for nuance reflects a different rationality — one that recognises governance change as relational, contextual, and resistant to quantification. Your practical task is not to choose between them but to serve both while being honest about what each requires. Design the review to answer the donor's questions — output delivery, value for money — because that is the institutional reality. But add a section — not an annex, a section — that analyses what the output indicators cannot capture: shifts in relationships, changes in how decisions are made, alterations in who speaks and who is heard. Call it 'governance dynamics' or 'process outcomes.' Present it with the same analytical rigour as the output data. And include a brief reflexive note: 'This review is structured by the terms of reference provided by the donor. The framework makes visible output delivery and financial efficiency. It makes less visible relational change, emergent outcomes, and effects that were not pre-specified.' This is not rebellion. It is professional honesty. And it models the Foucauldian insight: every analytical framework is a governance practice, and the honest practitioner makes visible what their framework governs."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Produce a "Foucauldian checklist" for programme design.
**Why:** Reducing Foucault to a checklist domesticates the analysis. The power of Foucault's thought is in the quality of attention it demands, not in a set of boxes to tick. A checklist would become another technology of governance — one that produces "power-aware" programmes as a new normalised category.

**Don't:** Use Foucault to justify inaction.
**Why:** The analysis that "everything is power" does not justify doing nothing. It justifies doing things with awareness, honesty, and reflexivity. The consultant who understands power and acts anyway — with open eyes — is practising applied Foucault. The consultant who understands power and retreats is practising academic tourism.
