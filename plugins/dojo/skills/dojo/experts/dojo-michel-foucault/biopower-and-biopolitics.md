# Biopower and Biopolitics

## When to Use
- When analysing how development programmes govern populations through demographic data, health statistics, and population-level interventions.
- When examining the politics of who is counted, how they are counted, and what the counting produces.
- When the question concerns the management of life itself — reproductive health, nutrition, mortality, morbidity — as a political practice.

## Core Concept
Biopower is Foucault's term for the form of power that takes life itself — birth rates, mortality rates, health, reproduction, longevity — as its object. Where sovereign power operated through the right to take life or let live, biopower operates through the right to make live and let die. It emerged in the eighteenth century alongside statistics, demography, and public health, and it governs not through individual discipline but through population-level management — the regulation of births, deaths, diseases, and productive capacity across entire populations.

Biopolitics is the political dimension of biopower — the way in which the management of life becomes a political strategy. The census, the epidemiological survey, the nutritional assessment, the demographic health survey — these are not merely data collection exercises. They are biopolitical technologies that render populations legible to governance. The population that is counted can be managed. The population that is categorised — by age, sex, income, health status, food security level — can be targeted. The statistical aggregate replaces the individual as the object of governance.

For development practice, biopower is everywhere and rarely named. The Millennium Development Goals and Sustainable Development Goals are biopolitical frameworks — they govern through population-level indicators (under-five mortality, maternal mortality ratio, stunting rates, poverty headcount) that make populations visible to international governance. The demographic and health survey is a biopolitical technology par excellence — it renders the reproductive, nutritional, and health behaviours of entire populations visible to planners who will never meet the individuals surveyed. The nutrition programme that weighs children against growth curves is exercising biopower — it measures individual bodies against a population norm and intervenes when deviation is detected.

The critical point is not that this governance is malicious. Reducing child mortality is an unambiguous good. But the techniques through which it is pursued — the surveys, the indicators, the targeting criteria, the growth curves — are biopolitical technologies that constitute their populations as objects of governance. The child who is "stunted" is not merely described — they are positioned within a biopolitical framework that determines what interventions they receive, what resources flow to their community, and what kind of life is considered adequate.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the population.** "What population is being governed? Who constructed it as a population? Through what data practices was it made visible? The 'food insecure population' exists only because a survey created it."
2. **Trace the statistics.** "What statistics are being produced? What do they aggregate? What individual variation do they erase? The statistic governs by reducing lives to categories."
3. **Ask who is let die.** "Biopower makes live and lets die. In resource allocation, targeting, and prioritisation — who is made to live (receives the intervention) and who is let die (is excluded by the criteria)? The targeting formula is a biopolitical decision."
4. **Connect the individual to the population.** "How does this individual-level intervention connect to population-level governance? The child weighed at the clinic produces a data point that feeds a district aggregate that feeds a national statistic that justifies international resource allocation."

## Examples
**Situation:** A government introduces a national nutrition surveillance system that requires monthly weighing of all children under five at community health posts, with data aggregated at district, provincial, and national levels. The system is celebrated as evidence-based governance.
**Application:** "This is a classical biopolitical technology. It renders visible the nutritional status of an entire population through a standardised measurement applied to individual bodies. Each child weighed against the WHO growth curve becomes a data point in a population aggregate. The aggregate makes the population legible to governance — districts can be compared, trends tracked, resources allocated. But observe what the technology produces. It produces 'stunted children' and 'wasted children' as categories — categories defined by deviation from a growth standard developed in specific populations under specific conditions. It produces a hierarchy of districts ranked by nutritional status — a normalising judgment at the population level. It produces a surveillance apparatus in which community health workers become the front line of biopolitical governance, monitoring bodies and reporting data upward. None of this makes the system wrong — nutritional surveillance saves lives. But it is biopower, and it deserves recognition as such. The question is: who governs through this data? What decisions does it enable? What does it make invisible — household food distribution, care practices, the gendered economics of feeding — that the weight alone cannot capture?"

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce biopower to a critique of public health.
**Why:** Foucault is not arguing against vaccinating children or treating disease. He is analysing the form of power through which these goods are delivered — and insisting that the form matters, has effects, and deserves examination.

**Don't:** Ignore the racial and colonial dimensions.
**Why:** Biopower has always operated through racial categories — deciding which populations are made to live and which are let die. In development contexts, the colonial genealogy of population management is inescapable and must be addressed.
