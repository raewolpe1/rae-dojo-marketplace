# Governmentality

## When to Use
- When analysing the rationalities behind programme design — the assumptions about how populations should be governed.
- When examining the gap between what a programme says it does and what it actually does as a governance practice.
- When the question is about how interventions function as techniques of managing populations.

## Core Concept
Governmentality is Foucault's term for the "conduct of conduct" — the art of governing populations through techniques that shape behaviour without primarily relying on coercion. It encompasses the mentalities (ways of thinking about governance), the techniques (the specific instruments through which governance operates), and the subjects (the kinds of people governance produces). The concept emerged from Foucault's 1977–1979 lectures at the Collège de France, where he traced the shift from sovereign power (the king's right to take life) to governmental power (the state's interest in managing the life and productivity of populations).

For development practice, governmentality is the most penetrating available concept. A development programme is a governmentality — it embodies a rationality about how populations should be managed, deploys specific techniques (logframes, indicators, baseline surveys, participatory workshops), and produces specific subjects (beneficiaries, empowered women, resilient communities). The programme does not merely intervene in a pre-existing situation. It constitutes the situation through its categories, its measurements, and its definitions of success.

The critical move is to ask not "does this programme work?" but "what kind of governance does this programme exercise?" Who defined the problem? Through what techniques is the population being managed? What kind of subjects is the programme producing? What assumptions about human behaviour, social change, and institutional function are embedded in the design — and where did those assumptions come from? These questions do not invalidate the programme. They make visible what the programme does beyond its stated objectives.

Three dimensions matter. First, the rationality: the theory of change is not merely a planning tool but a governmental rationality — it specifies how change happens, what causes it, and who must change. Second, the techniques: the logframe, the indicator, the monitoring visit, the evaluation report — each is a technology of governance that makes certain things visible and others invisible. Third, the subjects: the programme produces "beneficiaries" who must demonstrate specific behaviours to be counted as successful. The beneficiary who does not conform to the programme's categories is not merely unsuccessful — they are rendered unintelligible.

## How to Apply
1. **Map the rationality.** "Read the theory of change not as a planning document but as a statement of governmental rationality. What assumptions about human behaviour does it contain? Where did those assumptions originate?"
2. **Trace the techniques.** "Identify every specific instrument through which the programme operates — every form, indicator, survey, workshop protocol, reporting template. Each is a governance technique. What does each one make visible? What does it hide?"
3. **Identify the produced subjects.** "What kind of person must the beneficiary become to be counted as a programme success? What behaviours, attitudes, and identities does the programme reward, and which does it penalise or ignore?"
4. **Ask where the rationality came from.** "The theory of change was not discovered — it was produced. By whom? In what institutional context? Serving whose interests? Reflecting whose experience?"

## Examples
**Situation:** A livelihoods programme requires beneficiaries to develop individual business plans, open bank accounts, and attend financial literacy training before receiving a grant. The programme reports high compliance but limited sustainable enterprise development.
**Application:** The governmentality analysis would begin: "This programme embodies a specific governmental rationality — that poverty is addressed through individual entrepreneurship, financial inclusion, and human capital development. These assumptions are not self-evident. They were produced in specific institutional contexts — development economics, microfinance theory, World Bank policy frameworks — and they carry those contexts with them. The techniques — business plan templates, bank account requirements, financial literacy curricula — are technologies of governance that produce a specific kind of subject: the rational economic individual who plans, saves, and invests. The beneficiary who shares resources communally, who operates through informal credit networks, who defines success in relational rather than financial terms, is not merely excluded — they are rendered unintelligible by the programme's categories. The high compliance rate does not demonstrate that the programme works. It demonstrates that the governance is effective — people are conforming to the programme's requirements. The limited enterprise sustainability suggests that the governmental rationality may not match the economic reality it governs."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Equate governmentality with conspiracy or intentional manipulation.
**Why:** Governmentality operates through rationalities that their practitioners genuinely believe in. The programme designer who requires business plans is not manipulating beneficiaries — they are applying a theory of change they consider sound. The analysis is structural, not motivational.

**Don't:** Use governmentality only to critique — use it also to reveal alternatives.
**Why:** If every programme is a governmentality, the question becomes which governmentality, designed how, governed by whom, and accountable to what. The concept opens design questions, not just critical ones.
