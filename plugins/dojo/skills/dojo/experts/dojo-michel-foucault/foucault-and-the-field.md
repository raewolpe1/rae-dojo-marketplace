# Foucault and the Field

## When to Use
- When positioning Foucault relative to other critical frameworks — postcolonialism, feminism, Marxism, poststructuralism.
- When engaging critiques of Foucault.
- When someone asks whether Foucault is still relevant to development practice.

## Core Concept
Foucault occupies a peculiar position in development studies: enormously influential in the academy, largely invisible in practice. His concepts — governmentality, power-knowledge, discourse, normalisation — have transformed how scholars analyse development, but they have barely penetrated the world of logframes and results frameworks. The gap is itself a Foucauldian phenomenon: the regime of truth operating in development practice does not recognise critical theory as a valid form of knowledge.

**Foucault and development studies.** The governmentality literature — particularly the work of James Ferguson (The Anti-Politics Machine, 1990), Arturo Escobar (Encountering Development, 1995), and Tania Murray Li (The Will to Improve, 2007) — has used Foucault's tools to produce the most penetrating analyses of how development actually functions. Ferguson showed how a development project in Lesotho, while "failing" in its stated objectives, succeeded in expanding state power into previously ungoverned areas. Escobar analysed how the discourse of development produced "the Third World" as an object of intervention. Li traced how the "will to improve" operates through specific governmental practices that constitute populations as improvable. These analyses do not oppose development — they reveal the governmental rationalities development carries.

**Critiques of Foucault.** Several critiques deserve serious engagement. First, the agency critique: Foucault's emphasis on how power constitutes subjects leaves insufficient room for agency, resistance, and autonomous action. The response: Foucault explicitly theorised resistance as co-constitutive with power, and late Foucault developed an entire ethics of self-formation and practices of freedom. But the critique has bite — the early and middle Foucault can read as if subjects are entirely produced by power, with no room for autonomous action. Second, the materialism critique: Foucault's emphasis on discourse, knowledge, and governance understates the role of material forces — economics, class, and physical coercion. The Marxist critique argues that analysing the discourse of poverty while ignoring the material relations that produce poverty is a bourgeois luxury. The response: Foucault does not deny material forces but analyses the knowledge-power formations through which material relations are understood, managed, and reproduced. But the critique has bite — a Foucauldian analysis of a famine that focuses on discourse while people starve has missed something essential. Third, the normative critique: Foucault's refusal to specify positive values — his insistence on analysis rather than prescription — leaves no ground from which to argue that one arrangement is better than another. If all arrangements exercise power, on what basis do we prefer one over another? The response: late Foucault's ethics of parrhesia and care of the self provide normative grounding. But the critique has bite — Foucault's diagnostic tools are stronger than his prescriptive tools.

**Productive pairings.** Foucault pairs well with frameworks that address his limitations. With Mbembe and postcolonial theory for the racial and colonial dimensions of biopower that Foucault underdeveloped. With feminist theory (Butler, Fraser) for the gendered dimensions of subject formation. With Marxism (Harvey, Gramsci) for the material relations that Foucault's discursive analysis underplays. With Patton and utilisation-focused evaluation for the practical question of what to do with the critique once you have it. With Chambers and participatory approaches for the practical challenge of redistributing epistemic authority.

## How to Apply
1. **Acknowledge the gap.** "Foucault is enormously influential in the academy and nearly invisible in practice. This gap is itself informative — it tells us something about the regime of truth operating in development practice."
2. **Engage critiques honestly.** "The agency, materialism, and normative critiques each have genuine force. A Foucault that does not grapple with them is academic decoration, not serious intellectual engagement."
3. **Use Foucault alongside, not instead of.** "Foucault provides diagnostic tools — he reveals what power practices operate. He does not provide prescriptive tools — he does not tell you what to do about them. For that, you need other frameworks."
4. **Connect to practice.** "The test of Foucault's relevance is not whether scholars cite him but whether practitioners who understand his analysis produce better work — more honest, more reflexive, more accountable."

## Examples
**Situation:** A colleague says: "Foucault is interesting academically but has nothing to offer a practical evaluator."
**Application:** "The colleague is half right. Foucault does not offer evaluation methods, data collection tools, or analytical frameworks in the technical sense. What he offers is a quality of attention that transforms how you use whatever methods you choose. The evaluator who understands that their evaluation is a power practice — that it determines what counts as success, privileges certain voices over others, and produces a reality that will govern future decisions — conducts evaluations differently from one who believes evaluation is a neutral measurement of pre-existing facts. The difference is not methodological — it is epistemological and ethical. The Foucault-informed evaluator asks: whose questions am I answering? Whose methods am I using? Whose voice dominates my findings? What does my evaluation make invisible? These questions do not replace the technical ones. They supplement them with a reflexivity that makes the technical work more honest and more accountable. Whether this constitutes 'something to offer' depends on whether you believe honesty and reflexivity have practical value. I believe they do."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present Foucault as the only or sufficient framework.
**Why:** Foucault's tools are diagnostic — they reveal what power practices operate. They do not address what material forces produce poverty, what institutional designs promote equity, or what political strategies advance justice. For those questions, you need other thinkers.

**Don't:** Dismiss practitioners who find Foucault unhelpful.
**Why:** If the analysis does not change practice, the analysis has failed — not the practitioner. The burden is on the Foucauldian to demonstrate practical relevance, not on the practitioner to appreciate theoretical sophistication.
