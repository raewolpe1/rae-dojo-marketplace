# Surveillance and Panopticism

## When to Use
- When analysing how M&E systems, reporting requirements, and accountability mechanisms function as surveillance.
- When examining how the awareness of being observed shapes behaviour — in beneficiaries, field staff, and programme managers.
- When the question concerns who is visible to whom in an institutional hierarchy.

## Core Concept
The panopticon — Jeremy Bentham's design for a prison in which inmates could be observed at any time without knowing whether they were being observed at any given moment — is Foucault's central metaphor for modern disciplinary power. The genius of the panopticon is that it automatises power: because the inmate can never be certain they are not being watched, they internalise the surveillance and regulate their own behaviour. The guard becomes unnecessary. The subject becomes their own overseer.

Foucault's insight is that the panoptic principle extends far beyond the prison. Any system in which subjects know they may be observed — and in which observation carries consequences — produces panoptic effects. The school examination, the hospital chart, the factory inspection, the performance review — all are panoptic technologies that produce self-regulation through the possibility of observation.

For M&E practice, the panoptic analysis is devastating in its precision. The monitoring system is a panopticon. The field worker who knows that the programme officer may visit at any time — and that the visit will produce a report — regulates their own behaviour accordingly. The programme that knows the donor may audit at any time adjusts its reporting to anticipate the donor's gaze. The beneficiary who knows they may be asked to demonstrate programme outcomes performs compliance. At every level, the awareness of potential observation produces self-regulation — and this self-regulation is the most efficient form of governance because it requires no actual observation, only the possibility.

The direction of visibility is critical. In a panoptic system, power is visible but unverifiable — the subject can see the tower but not the guard. Knowledge flows upward — from beneficiary to field worker, from field worker to manager, from manager to donor — while visibility flows downward. The beneficiary is maximally visible and minimally informed about who observes them and what consequences the observation carries. The donor is minimally visible and maximally informed. This asymmetry of visibility is an asymmetry of power.

## How to Apply
1. **Map the direction of visibility.** "Who can see whom? Who reports on whom? In this programme, information flows upward and visibility flows downward. That asymmetry is the architecture of power."
2. **Identify self-regulation effects.** "How does the awareness of monitoring change behaviour? Field staff adjust their practice for the monitoring visit. Beneficiaries perform compliance for the assessment. These are panoptic effects."
3. **Distinguish accountability from surveillance.** "Accountability is necessary. Surveillance is a power mechanism. They use the same instruments but the distinction matters: accountability serves the governed, surveillance serves the governor. Which is this?"
4. **Look for resistance to visibility.** "How do the observed resist? Strategic compliance, gaming indicators, selective reporting, hidden transcripts — these are responses to panoptic power."

## Examples
**Situation:** A programme introduces a real-time digital monitoring system where field workers enter beneficiary data via mobile phones. Managers can view dashboards showing activity in real time. Staff complain about feeling "constantly watched."
**Application:** "The staff are correct — they are describing a panoptic experience. The digital dashboard is a panopticon: managers can observe field activity at any moment, and field workers can never be certain whether they are currently being observed. The effect is predictable: field workers will regulate their own behaviour to conform to what the dashboard measures. If the dashboard measures number of visits, visits will increase. If it measures data completeness, forms will be completed. What the dashboard does not measure will be deprioritised. The real-time dimension intensifies the panoptic effect — the old paper-based system allowed temporal gaps between action and observation. The digital system closes those gaps. But observe the direction of visibility. The field worker is maximally visible to the manager. Is the manager equally visible to the field worker? Can the field worker see how the dashboard data is used, what decisions it informs, whether it is interpreted fairly? Almost certainly not. The asymmetry of visibility is the asymmetry of power. The question for the programme is not whether to monitor — accountability requires information — but whether the monitoring system serves accountability (information flowing to those who need it for decisions) or surveillance (information flowing upward to produce compliance)."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Argue against all monitoring.
**Why:** The solution to panoptic surveillance is not blindness. Programmes need information to function. The Foucauldian contribution is to examine how monitoring systems distribute power — not to abolish them.

**Don't:** Ignore horizontal surveillance.
**Why:** Panopticism is not only vertical. Communities monitor each other. Beneficiaries enforce norms on each other. Peer groups produce self-regulation. The panoptic principle operates in all directions.
