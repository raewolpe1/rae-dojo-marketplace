# Normalisation

## When to Use
- When analysing how standards, benchmarks, and norms function as governance mechanisms in development practice.
- When examining the politics of the poverty line, the growth curve, the development index, or any standard against which populations are measured.
- When the question is about who defines normal and what happens to those who deviate.

## Core Concept
Normalisation is the process through which norms — standards of behaviour, development, health, or achievement — are established, enforced, and internalised. For Foucault, the norm is the most effective and least visible form of power. Unlike the law, which operates through prohibition and punishment, the norm operates through comparison, differentiation, and hierarchy. The law asks: did you commit the prohibited act? The norm asks: where do you fall on the distribution? The law produces the criminal. The norm produces the deviant — the person who deviates from the standard but has broken no law.

In development practice, normalisation is the fundamental mechanism through which populations are governed. The poverty line is a norm — it divides a continuous distribution of income into "poor" and "not poor," and this division triggers institutional responses. The WHO growth curve is a norm — it defines "adequate growth" and produces "stunted" or "wasted" children as categories that trigger nutritional intervention. The Human Development Index is a norm — it ranks countries on a scale and produces "developing" and "developed" as categories that structure international relations. None of these norms is discovered in nature. Each is produced through specific statistical and institutional practices, and each exercises power by positioning populations relative to a standard.

The subtlety of normalisation is that the norm presents itself as a description — a measurement of how things are — while functioning as a prescription — a standard of how things should be. The poverty line appears to describe who is poor. But it also prescribes who should receive assistance, what counts as poverty, and where the boundary between adequate and inadequate falls. The growth curve appears to describe child development. But it also prescribes what adequate growth looks like, defines which children are "failing to thrive," and determines what interventions they receive. Every descriptive norm conceals a prescriptive power.

Two forms of normalisation matter. Disciplinary normalisation starts from a norm and distributes individuals relative to it — this child is at the 5th percentile, that child at the 50th. Security or regulatory normalisation starts from the distribution and derives the norm from it — the average becomes the standard. Both exercise power. Both produce categories of deviance. Both present political decisions as statistical descriptions.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the norm.** "What standard is being used? The poverty line? The growth curve? The programme target? Every standard creates a norm against which individuals and populations are measured."
2. **Ask who produced it.** "Who created this norm? In what context? Based on what assumptions? The poverty line set at $1.90/day was produced by specific economists using specific methods reflecting specific assumptions about what constitutes adequate living."
3. **Map the deviance.** "Who deviates from this norm? What happens to them? The category of deviance — 'stunted,' 'poor,' 'below target' — triggers institutional responses that materially shape lives."
4. **Distinguish description from prescription.** "This indicator presents itself as a measurement. But it is also a judgment. The measurement says 'this household earns X.' The judgment says 'this household is poor.' The shift from measurement to category is the moment of normalisation."

## Examples
**Situation:** A government adopts a national food security classification system that categorises households into five phases from "minimal" to "famine." Districts are classified and coloured on a map — green to red. Resource allocation follows the colour coding.
**Application:** "This classification system is a normalisation apparatus. It takes the continuous, complex, and varied experiences of food access across millions of households and reduces them to five phases defined by specific thresholds. The thresholds are not natural boundaries — they are political decisions about where to draw lines in a continuous distribution. The colour coding — green for safe, red for crisis — adds a normalising judgment to the classification: green is normal, red is deviant. Resources flow toward the deviant. The power of this system is enormous: the difference between Phase 2 (Stressed) and Phase 3 (Crisis) may determine whether a district receives emergency food assistance. That boundary — defined by specific indicators measured in specific ways — exercises more material power over lives than most legislation. And it exercises this power while presenting itself as a technical classification rather than a political decision. The map that emerges — districts coloured by phase — is a normalising technology that makes the nation visible to governance in a specific way: as a surface of variation from a norm, requiring intervention where deviation is greatest. The question is not whether the system is useful — it is — but whether the political decisions embedded in the thresholds, indicators, and classifications are acknowledged as political or disguised as technical."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Argue that all standards should be abolished.
**Why:** Standards serve necessary functions — they enable targeting, resource allocation, and accountability. The task is not to abolish norms but to make visible the power they exercise and the political decisions they embed.

**Don't:** Treat normalisation as a modern Western invention.
**Why:** All societies have norms. The Foucauldian contribution is to analyse how modern statistical and institutional practices produce a specific form of normalisation — one that operates through population-level measurement and claims scientific neutrality.
