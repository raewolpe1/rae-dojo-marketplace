# Foucault and Measurement

## When to Use
- When analysing how M&E instruments exercise power — indicators, logframes, baseline surveys, evaluation reports.
- When examining what a specific indicator or framework makes visible and what it renders invisible.
- When the question is about how measurement shapes practice rather than merely describing it.

## Core Concept
Measurement is governance. This is the central Foucauldian claim about M&E practice, and it is not metaphorical. The indicator does not find a pre-existing fact and report it. It constitutes a fact by determining what will be measured, how it will be measured, and what the measurement means institutionally. The logframe does not describe a programme. It governs a programme by determining what activities are funded, what outputs are reported, what outcomes are expected, and what constitutes success. The baseline survey does not discover conditions. It produces conditions by determining what conditions are relevant — and therefore what conditions will receive resources and attention.

Five mechanisms connect measurement to governance. First, selection: the choice of what to measure is the choice of what to see. The indicator "number of women trained" makes training events visible. It does not make quality of training, relevance of content, or post-training application visible. What is not indicated does not exist for institutional purposes. Second, definition: the indicator defines its object. "Food insecurity" is not a natural category — it is defined by specific questions, administered in specific ways, producing specific classifications. The definition determines who is food insecure and who is not. Third, normalisation: the target creates a norm. A programme targeting "80% of beneficiaries demonstrating improved knowledge" produces a standard against which implementation is judged and staff are held accountable. Fourth, accountability: the indicator creates an accountability chain. What is measured is what is reported. What is reported is what is rewarded or punished. The indicator governs behaviour by determining what carries consequences. Fifth, invisibility: what the indicator does not measure becomes institutionally invisible. Effects that are real but unmeasured do not appear in reports, do not inform decisions, and do not receive resources.

The logframe deserves particular attention as a technology of governance. It presents itself as a planning matrix — a neutral tool for organising programme logic. But it exercises enormous power. It requires linear causality (inputs produce outputs produce outcomes produce impact) in a world of complex causality. It requires pre-specification of outcomes in a world of emergence. It requires quantifiable indicators in a world of qualitative change. These requirements are not neutral — they determine what kind of programme is conceivable, what kind of change is recognisable, and what kind of knowledge is producible.

## How to Apply
1. **Read the indicator as governance.** "This indicator governs by making certain things visible and others invisible. What does it see? What does it exclude? Whose experience does it capture and whose does it erase?"
2. **Examine the logframe as power.** "This logframe requires linear causality, pre-specified outcomes, and quantifiable indicators. What kinds of change does this make inconceivable? Emergent change? Relational change? Contradictory effects?"
3. **Map the accountability chain.** "Follow the indicator from data point to report to decision. What behaviour does it incentivise in the field? What reporting does it require? What does the funder do with it? The chain is a governance chain."
4. **Ask what is invisible.** "Name three important effects of this programme that are not captured by any indicator. Those effects are institutionally invisible. They will not receive resources or attention. That invisibility is an exercise of power."

## Examples
**Situation:** An education programme reports "number of learners completing the training" as its primary indicator. Completion rates are high, but a qualitative assessment reveals minimal change in teaching practice among graduates. The programme team is confused by the discrepancy.
**Application:** "There is no discrepancy — the indicator is governing as designed. 'Number of learners completing' measures attendance and compliance, not learning or application. The indicator made completion visible and made learning invisible. The programme then optimised for what was visible: it ensured people attended and completed. Everything the indicator did not measure — comprehension, relevance, application, sustained change — was institutionally invisible and therefore received no optimisation. The programme is not failing. It is succeeding at what the indicator governs: completion. The qualitative assessment reveals what the indicator hid: that completion and change are different things. The confusion arises because the programme team assumed the indicator described reality (people who complete learn and change). In fact, the indicator governed reality (people who are measured on completion complete). The solution is not better indicators — though better indicators would help — it is understanding that every indicator governs, and the question is always: what governance does this indicator exercise, and is it the governance you intend?"

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Argue against all measurement.
**Why:** The alternative to measured governance is not ungoverned freedom — it is governance by other means: charisma, patronage, ideology, inertia. Measurement is governance, but it is also accountability. The task is to measure honestly, not to stop measuring.

**Don't:** Propose "power-free" indicators.
**Why:** There is no indicator that does not exercise power. The task is not to eliminate power from measurement but to make visible the power each indicator exercises and to choose indicators whose power effects are acceptable.
