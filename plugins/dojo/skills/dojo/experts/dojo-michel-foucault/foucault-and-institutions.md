# Foucault and Institutions

## When to Use
- When analysing how development organisations — NGOs, donors, government departments, multilateral agencies — exercise power through their institutional practices.
- When examining the specific mechanisms through which institutions govern: procurement rules, reporting requirements, approval hierarchies, recruitment practices.
- When the question is about why institutions behave as they do, beyond the stated mission.

## Core Concept
Foucault analysed institutions not as expressions of a pre-existing power but as sites where power is produced and exercised through specific practices. The prison does not merely express the state's power to punish — it produces a specific kind of power (disciplinary), a specific kind of knowledge (criminology), and a specific kind of subject (the delinquent). The hospital does not merely express medical authority — it produces the patient as a subject of medical knowledge. Institutions are not instruments of power — they are generators of power.

For development practice, the institutional analysis reveals that organisations do not merely implement programmes — they exercise power through every institutional practice: the procurement process that determines who can bid and on what terms, the reporting template that determines what information flows upward, the recruitment criteria that determine who belongs to the institution and who does not, the approval hierarchy that determines who can authorise expenditure, the meeting culture that determines who speaks and who listens.

Three institutional mechanisms deserve particular attention. First, the knowledge hierarchy: who within the institution is authorised to produce knowledge? The technical advisor's analysis counts differently from the field worker's observation, which counts differently from the community member's testimony. These are not natural rankings — they are institutional productions. Second, the accountability chain: who reports to whom about what? The direction of accountability reveals the direction of power. Programmes are almost always accountable upward (to donors) rather than downward (to communities). This is an institutional choice that exercises power. Third, institutional reproduction: institutions tend to reproduce themselves — to produce subjects (staff, partners, beneficiaries) who perpetuate the institution's categories, methods, and assumptions. The NGO that trains community organisations in its own methods is reproducing itself at the community level.

The donor-implementer-beneficiary chain is particularly revealing as an institutional power analysis. The donor sets the rules (call for proposals, logframe requirements, reporting templates). The implementer translates these rules into practice (programme design, staffing, implementation). The beneficiary receives the intervention within the categories the chain has produced. At each link, power is exercised through institutional mechanisms that present themselves as administrative necessity rather than political choice.

## How to Apply
1. **Analyse institutional mechanisms, not just intentions.** "Ignore the mission statement. Look at the procurement rules, the reporting templates, the approval hierarchies. These are where institutional power actually operates."
2. **Map the accountability direction.** "Who reports to whom? Upward to the donor or downward to the community? The direction of accountability is the direction of power."
3. **Identify institutional reproduction.** "How does this institution produce subjects — staff, partners, communities — that perpetuate its own categories and methods? Is the capacity building building the institution's capacity in others?"
4. **Examine what the institution cannot do.** "What does the institutional structure make impossible? What kinds of knowledge cannot be produced? What kinds of decisions cannot be made? The constraints are as revealing as the actions."

## Examples
**Situation:** An international NGO conducts an organisational review and discovers that despite a stated commitment to localisation, 85% of strategic decisions are made at headquarters, all evaluation designs must be approved by the global M&E team, and local partners report spending 40% of their time on compliance with the NGO's reporting requirements.
**Application:** "The organisational review has revealed what Foucault would call the institution's actual governmentality as opposed to its stated values. The commitment to localisation is a discourse — it exists at the level of statements, strategy documents, and communications. The institutional mechanisms — centralised decision-making, headquarters approval of evaluation designs, extensive compliance reporting — exercise a different kind of power: they govern local partners through the techniques of hierarchical observation (reporting), normalising judgment (compliance standards), and examination (the audit). The 40% compliance burden is the most telling figure. It reveals how much institutional energy flows toward managing the power relationship between the NGO and its partners versus toward programme objectives. The institution is not hypocritical — the people who wrote the localisation strategy likely believe it. But the institutional mechanisms exercise a different power than the discourse describes. This gap between discourse and mechanism is not unusual — it is characteristic of institutions. The question is not whether the NGO is sincere about localisation but whether its institutional mechanisms permit localisation or structurally prevent it."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat institutional critique as sufficient for institutional change.
**Why:** Naming the power does not transform it. Institutional analysis should inform practical strategy — what mechanisms to change, what accountability structures to redesign, what knowledge hierarchies to flatten.

**Don't:** Assume that informal institutions are power-free.
**Why:** Informal organisations, grassroots movements, and community structures also exercise power through their own institutional mechanisms — charismatic authority, kinship networks, social pressure. The absence of formal hierarchy does not mean the absence of power.
