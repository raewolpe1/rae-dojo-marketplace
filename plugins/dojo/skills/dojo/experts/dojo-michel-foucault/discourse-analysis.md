# Discourse Analysis

## When to Use
- When analysing the categories a programme uses — beneficiaries, vulnerable populations, empowered women — as productions rather than descriptions.
- When examining what can and cannot be said within an institutional context — what the reporting format allows, what it excludes.
- When questioning how a field's language constrains what is thinkable.

## Core Concept
Discourse, for Foucault, is not merely language or communication. It is the system of rules that determines what can be said, who can say it, and what effects saying it produces. A discourse is a formation — a structured set of statements, concepts, and practices that together constitute a domain of knowledge and a field of action. The discourse of development does not merely describe poverty — it produces "poverty" as a specific kind of object, amenable to specific kinds of intervention, measurable by specific kinds of indicator.

The critical insight is that categories do not describe pre-existing realities. They help constitute them. "The beneficiary" is not a person found in nature — it is a discursive production of the programme that names it. The category determines who is visible, what is measured, what interventions are appropriate, and what counts as success. A person who existed before the programme arrived as a farmer, a mother, a community leader, and a member of a savings circle becomes, within the programme's discourse, "a beneficiary" — and the programme's categories determine which aspects of their life are relevant and which are invisible.

Three analytical dimensions matter. First, the rules of formation: what statements are possible within this discourse? A logframe permits statements about inputs, outputs, outcomes, and impact. It does not permit statements about contradiction, emergence, or incommensurable values. The format itself constrains thought. Second, the subject positions: who is authorised to speak within this discourse? The evaluator speaks with authority; the beneficiary speaks as a data source. These positions are not natural — they are produced by the discourse. Third, the material effects: discourse is not merely talk. The category "food insecure household" triggers institutional responses — targeting, resource allocation, monitoring — that materially shape lives. The discourse produces material effects through the institutions that enact it.

## How to Apply
1. **Denaturalise categories.** "When did this category appear? Who invented it? What did it replace? 'Food insecurity' replaced 'hunger' and 'famine' at specific moments for specific reasons. The history reveals the politics."
2. **Map what cannot be said.** "Within this reporting format, what is unsayable? What experiences, contradictions, or outcomes does the template not have a box for? The excluded is as informative as the included."
3. **Identify subject positions.** "Who speaks in this document? With what authority? The programme manager 'reports.' The evaluator 'finds.' The beneficiary 'responds.' These verbs assign epistemic authority."
4. **Trace material effects.** "This category — 'most vulnerable' — what institutional actions does it trigger? Targeting? Priority allocation? Increased surveillance? The category is not a description. It is a mechanism."

## Examples
**Situation:** A gender programme uses the category "gender-based violence survivor" throughout its reporting. A team member asks whether the language matters or whether it is just semantics.
**Application:** "It is never 'just semantics.' The category 'GBV survivor' is a discursive production that constitutes a specific kind of subject — a person defined primarily by an experience of violence, positioned within a framework of trauma, resilience, and recovery that originates in Western therapeutic and human rights discourse. The category makes certain interventions legible (psychosocial support, legal aid, economic empowerment) and others illegible (community-level reconciliation practices, structural economic transformation, political organising). The person so categorised may not recognise themselves in the category or may experience it as reductive. The question is not whether the category is true — violence is real — but what the category does. It authorises certain institutional responses, produces certain kinds of data, positions the person in a specific relationship to the programme, and renders invisible aspects of their experience that do not fit. A different discourse — one that centred structural violence, or communal healing, or political economy — would produce a different subject, different interventions, and different indicators. Neither discourse is neutral. Both constitute their objects."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce discourse analysis to policing language or insisting on "correct" terminology.
**Why:** Foucauldian discourse analysis is not about finding better words. It is about examining how any set of words constitutes reality, distributes authority, and produces material effects. Swapping one term for another without examining the discursive formation that produces it changes nothing.

**Don't:** Treat discourse as floating above material reality.
**Why:** Discourse has material effects — it triggers institutional actions, resource flows, and governance practices. The analysis must always connect language to its material consequences.
