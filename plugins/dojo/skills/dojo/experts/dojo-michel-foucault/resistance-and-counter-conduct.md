# Resistance and Counter-Conduct

## When to Use
- When analysing what programmes call "non-compliance," "programme failure," or "low uptake" as potential forms of resistance.
- When communities push back against interventions in ways that confuse or frustrate programme designers.
- When the question is whether beneficiary behaviour that does not conform to programme expectations should be understood as failure or as agency.

## Core Concept
Counter-conduct is Foucault's term for the specific forms of resistance that arise within and against a particular mode of governance. It is not revolution — it does not aim to overthrow the system. It is conduct that refuses to be conducted in the prescribed way. The term emerged from Foucault's analysis of pastoral power — the forms of resistance within the Christian pastorate: asceticism against the church's monopoly on salvation, mysticism against its monopoly on truth, community against its hierarchical structure. These were not rejections of Christianity but alternative ways of being Christian — counter-conducts within the pastoral relationship.

For development practice, counter-conduct is an indispensable analytical tool. What programmes call "non-compliance" is often counter-conduct — the refusal to be governed in the prescribed manner. The beneficiary who takes the grant but does not write the business plan is not failing — they may be resisting the programme's entrepreneurial governmentality while accepting its resources. The community that attends the participatory workshop but provides the answers it thinks the facilitator wants is not participating — it is managing the programme's surveillance. The field worker who adjusts the data to meet targets is not cheating — they are navigating between the programme's demands and the reality they observe.

Foucault's structural point is that resistance is not exceptional or pathological — it is co-constitutive with power. Where there is power, there is resistance. This is not optimism. It is a structural observation: the same techniques that enable governance also enable counter-conduct. The monitoring system that makes the beneficiary visible to the programme also makes the programme's expectations visible to the beneficiary — who can then perform compliance, game the indicators, or redirect the resources.

Three forms of counter-conduct matter in development practice. First, strategic compliance — performing the required behaviours without internalising the programme's objectives. The training participant who passes the test but does not change their practice. Second, appropriation — taking the programme's resources while redirecting them toward purposes the programme did not intend. The grant recipient who uses the business grant for school fees. Third, exit — withdrawing from the programme altogether. The community that stops attending workshops. Each form is a response to governance, and each carries information about the programme's governmentality that the programme is usually not designed to hear.

## How to Apply
1. **Reframe non-compliance.** "Before calling it failure, ask: is this resistance? What is the participant refusing? What governance are they resisting? What does their refusal tell you about the programme?"
2. **Listen to strategic compliance.** "When participants do what is required without changing — that is counter-conduct. It tells you the programme has produced compliance but not conviction. The question is why."
3. **Track appropriation.** "When programme resources are redirected — grants used for different purposes, skills applied to different domains — the community may be telling you something about the mismatch between the programme's theory of change and their actual priorities."
4. **Treat exit as information.** "When people stop coming, stop participating, stop responding — that is the strongest form of counter-conduct. It is also the most invisible to monitoring systems designed to count participation."

## Examples
**Situation:** A water and sanitation programme provides household latrines, but monitoring reveals that many households have reverted to previous practices within six months. Programme staff describe this as "behavioural relapse" requiring more intensive hygiene education.
**Application:** "Before diagnosing relapse, consider counter-conduct. The reversion to previous practices may be resistance to the programme's sanitation governmentality — a refusal to be governed in the prescribed manner. Ask: what was the programme governing? Not just defecation practices — it was governing the domestic space, the family's daily routines, their relationship to their own bodies. The household latrine carries a specific developmental rationality about hygiene, modernity, and proper behaviour. The community may be resisting not the latrine itself but the governmentality it represents — the external determination of how they organise their domestic life. Or the resistance may be more practical: the latrine design may not suit their needs, the maintenance burden may be unsustainable, or the previous practices may have served social functions the programme did not consider. The programme's response — 'more intensive hygiene education' — is itself revealing. It diagnoses the problem as insufficient governance and prescribes more governance. A counter-conduct analysis would diagnose the problem as a mismatch between the programme's governmentality and the community's priorities and would prescribe listening rather than intensified instruction."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Celebrate all resistance as liberation.
**Why:** Some counter-conduct is self-defeating. The community that rejects vaccination is exercising counter-conduct that may cost lives. Foucault does not say resistance is always good — he says it is always present and always informative.

**Don't:** Use counter-conduct analysis to blame the programme for everything.
**Why:** Counter-conduct is structural — it exists in all governance relationships, including excellent ones. The analysis should inform programme design, not replace it with guilt.
