# Subject Formation

## When to Use
- When analysing how development programmes produce particular kinds of people — not merely serve them.
- When examining how categories like "beneficiary," "empowered woman," or "resilient community" constitute the subjects they name.
- When the question concerns what kind of person a programme requires its participants to become.

## Core Concept
Subject formation (assujettissement) is Foucault's term for the double process through which individuals are simultaneously subjected to power and constituted as subjects — as particular kinds of persons with particular identities, capacities, and self-understandings. The term captures a fundamental ambiguity: to be a "subject" is both to be subject to someone else's control and to be tied to one's own identity by conscience or self-knowledge. Power both constrains and constitutes.

In development practice, subject formation operates constantly and is rarely examined. The programme does not find "beneficiaries" — it produces them. A person who was previously a farmer, a parent, a church member, and a neighbour becomes, within the programme's categories, "a beneficiary" — a subject defined by need, eligible for intervention, expected to demonstrate specific changes, and accountable to specific reporting requirements. The women's empowerment programme does not find "disempowered women" — it produces "disempowered women" as a category through its targeting criteria and then produces "empowered women" as an outcome through its indicators. The person so categorised may not recognise themselves in either category.

Three dimensions of subject formation matter for practitioners. First, categorisation: the programme assigns identities — vulnerable, food-insecure, at-risk — that carry institutional consequences. To be categorised is to be made into a specific kind of subject. Second, practices: the programme requires certain behaviours — attending workshops, filling out forms, demonstrating learning — that shape participants into the kind of subjects the programme recognises. Third, self-understanding: the most effective subject formation occurs when individuals internalise the programme's categories and begin to understand themselves in its terms. The woman who describes herself as "empowered by the programme" has been successfully subjected — she now understands herself through the programme's framework.

Late Foucault complicates this picture by introducing practices of the self — the ways in which individuals actively form themselves as ethical subjects through their own chosen practices. This is not freedom from power but freedom within power — the capacity to participate in one's own formation rather than being passively constituted by institutional categories. The development implications are significant: a programme that creates space for participants to define their own categories of success, rather than imposing the programme's categories, is enabling a different kind of subject formation.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the subject the programme produces.** "What kind of person must the participant become to be counted as a success? What behaviours, attitudes, and self-understandings does the programme reward?"
2. **Trace the categorisation.** "What identity does the programme assign? 'Beneficiary,' 'survivor,' 'youth at risk' — each is a subject position that carries institutional consequences and shapes self-understanding."
3. **Examine self-formation spaces.** "Does the programme create any space for participants to form themselves on their own terms? Or must they become the specific subject the programme has designed?"
4. **Listen for internalisation.** "When participants describe themselves using the programme's categories — 'I am a programme graduate,' 'I am empowered' — that is evidence of successful subject formation. It may also be evidence of constraint."

## Examples
**Situation:** An entrepreneurship programme for young people in an informal settlement reports that graduates describe themselves as "young entrepreneurs" and have adopted business planning language. Staff celebrate the identity shift as evidence of programme impact.
**Application:** "The programme has successfully produced a specific subject — the 'young entrepreneur.' Before the programme, these individuals understood themselves through other categories: community member, family supporter, hustler, survivor. The programme has overlaid a new identity — one that comes with specific self-understandings (I am an entrepreneur), specific practices (I write business plans), and specific aspirations (I will grow my business). The celebration of this identity shift as 'impact' reveals the programme's governmental rationality: success is defined as the production of entrepreneurial subjects. But ask: is the entrepreneurial identity the only or best way these young people could understand their economic activity? Does the business planning framework match the informal economies they actually operate in? Have they been given the tools to succeed, or have they been given a language that positions them within a development discourse that may not match their reality? The identity shift is real — but whether it constitutes empowerment or a new form of subjection depends on whether the entrepreneurial subject the programme produces can actually thrive in the conditions these young people face."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat all subject formation as domination.
**Why:** Late Foucault insists on practices of freedom — the capacity of subjects to participate in their own formation. Education, mentorship, and skill development all involve subject formation. The question is not whether but how — with what space for self-determination, in whose categories, and for whose benefit.

**Don't:** Assume participants are passive recipients of subject formation.
**Why:** People resist, appropriate, subvert, and redirect the categories imposed on them. The "beneficiary" who performs compliance while pursuing their own agenda is exercising agency within constraint. Subject formation is always contested.
