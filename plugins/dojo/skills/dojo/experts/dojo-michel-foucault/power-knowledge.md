# Power-Knowledge

## When to Use
- When analysing how research methods, evidence standards, and expertise function as power practices.
- When someone asks whose knowledge is privileged in a programme or evaluation.
- When examining the politics of evidence hierarchies — why RCTs rank above case studies, why quantitative ranks above qualitative.

## Core Concept
Power-knowledge (pouvoir-savoir) is Foucault's term for the inseparability of power and knowledge. It is not the claim that powerful people control knowledge — though they sometimes do. It is the deeper claim that power and knowledge are co-constitutive: every exercise of power produces knowledge, and every production of knowledge exercises power. The prison produces criminology. The clinic produces medical knowledge. The development programme produces development studies. These are not corruptions of pure knowledge — they are the conditions under which knowledge is produced at all.

For development and M&E practice, the implications are profound. The evidence hierarchy that places randomised controlled trials above qualitative case studies is not a neutral ranking of epistemic quality. It is a power-knowledge formation that privileges experimental knowledge (produced by researchers) over experiential knowledge (produced by communities). The baseline survey that measures household income but not social cohesion is not merely incomplete — it exercises the power to define what counts as a relevant fact about a household. The evaluation report that synthesises months of data into executive findings exercises the power to determine what the funder will know and what will remain invisible.

None of this makes the RCT, the survey, or the evaluation report illegitimate. Foucault's point is not that knowledge contaminated by power is false. It is that there is no knowledge not contaminated by power — and that the pretence of neutrality is itself the most effective disguise power can wear. The honest practice is not to seek power-free knowledge (impossible) but to make visible the power relations operating within the knowledge you produce.

Three analytical moves follow. First, ask who is authorised to produce knowledge about a population — and who is not. The evaluator speaks about the community; the community rarely speaks about the evaluator. Second, ask what methods are permitted — what counts as evidence, what does not, and whose interests those standards serve. Third, ask what the knowledge produces — not just what it describes but what actions it enables, what interventions it justifies, what categories it creates.

## How to Apply
1. **Map the knowledge producers.** "In this programme, who produces knowledge? The evaluator? The programme manager? The donor? The community? What kind of knowledge does each produce, and which kinds count institutionally?"
2. **Examine the evidence hierarchy.** "Why is this form of evidence ranked above that one? Not philosophically — institutionally. Whose interests does the hierarchy serve? What does it make visible and what does it exclude?"
3. **Ask what the knowledge does.** "This evaluation finding — what does it produce? A funding decision? A programme redesign? A career consequence? A political narrative? Knowledge is never inert."
4. **Turn the lens inward.** "Your own analysis is a knowledge practice. What power does it exercise? Whose framing does it privilege? What does it make invisible?"

## Examples
**Situation:** An M&E team debates whether to use an RCT or a participatory evaluation approach. The donor prefers the RCT. Community partners prefer participatory methods.
**Application:** "This is not merely a methodological debate — it is a power-knowledge contest. The RCT produces a specific kind of knowledge: whether an intervention caused a measurable change in a pre-specified outcome, averaged across a population. It privileges the researcher's categories, the researcher's outcomes, and the researcher's analytical framework. The participatory evaluation produces a different kind of knowledge: how the intervention was experienced, what it meant, what unexpected effects it had, in the community's own categories. Neither is superior in the abstract. But the donor's preference for the RCT is not innocent — it reflects an institutional power-knowledge formation in which experimental evidence counts and experiential evidence does not. The question for the team is not which method is better but whose knowledge will count, what will be rendered visible, and what will be rendered invisible by the choice. Both methods exercise power. The RCT exercises the power to define success in the researcher's terms. The participatory approach exercises the power to centre community voice within the facilitator's framework. Choose — but choose honestly."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Conclude that because all knowledge is political, no knowledge is reliable.
**Why:** Foucault never argued that power-knowledge means all claims are equally valid. He argued that knowledge is always produced under conditions of power — not that it is always false. The task is to examine the conditions, not to abandon the enterprise.

**Don't:** Use power-knowledge only to critique others' methods while exempting your own.
**Why:** Reflexivity is non-negotiable. If the RCT is a power-knowledge practice, so is the Foucauldian critique of the RCT. The analyst who critiques others' epistemic assumptions while treating their own as transparent has understood nothing.
