# Trauma and Relationships

## When to Use
- When someone's trauma history is creating patterns in their current relationships — mistrust, withdrawal, explosive reactivity, inability to tolerate closeness.
- When a partner, friend, or family member wants to understand what is happening.
- When someone needs to understand that their relational difficulties are trauma adaptations, not character deficits.

## Core Concept
Van der Kolk emphasises that trauma is fundamentally a relational wound — most trauma occurs at the hands of other humans — and that it therefore manifests most intensely in relationships. The traumatised person's nervous system has learned that other people are dangerous, and this learning does not confine itself to the people who actually were dangerous. It generalises to intimacy itself.

The specific relational patterns depend on the trauma. Someone who was physically abused may flinch from physical contact, even affectionate contact. Someone who was emotionally betrayed may monitor their partner for signs of deception with exhausting hypervigilance. Someone who was abandoned may cling to relationships with desperate intensity or avoid attachment altogether to prevent the possibility of being left again.

These patterns are not chosen. They are the nervous system's learned responses to danger, activated automatically by the stimuli that accompany closeness: vulnerability, dependency, physical proximity, emotional exposure. The person who pulls away during intimate moments is not rejecting their partner — they are being hijacked by a survival response that equates vulnerability with danger.

For the partner of a trauma survivor, this can be confusing and painful. The withdrawal feels like rejection. The hypervigilance feels like accusation. The emotional volatility feels like instability. Understanding the trauma framework does not eliminate the impact on the partner, but it reframes the behaviour from intentional to automatic, from personal to neurological.

Recovery of relational capacity requires both individual work (processing the trauma, building regulatory capacity) and relational work (learning, through safe relational experience, that intimacy need not mean danger). The therapeutic relationship is often the laboratory for this learning — a relationship that is reliable, boundaried, attuned, and safe, providing a corrective experience that gradually updates the nervous system's expectations.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the pattern.** When relational difficulty arises, ask: is this about the present relationship or about the past one? Is my nervous system responding to my partner or to the threat my partner's behaviour resembles?
2. **Communicate the mechanism.** If you can, tell your partner: "When you raise your voice, my body responds as though I am in danger. This is not about you — it is about my nervous system's history. I need you to understand this, not to fix it."
3. **Build relational safety deliberately.** Predictability, consistency, transparency, and physical safety are the ingredients. The traumatised nervous system needs evidence — repeated, embodied evidence — that this relationship is different from the one that caused harm.
4. **Seek couples therapy with a trauma-informed therapist.** A therapist who understands both relational dynamics and trauma neurobiology can help the couple navigate the space between one person's triggers and the other person's needs.

## Examples
**Situation:** A couple is in crisis. She is a childhood sexual abuse survivor. He reports that she alternates between wanting physical closeness and suddenly pushing him away during intimacy, sometimes with what seems like disgust. He feels confused and rejected. She feels ashamed and defective.
**Application:** Her behaviour is a trauma response, not a relational message. During intimacy, her body encounters sensory cues — physical positions, touch, vulnerability — that activate the implicit memory of abuse. Her nervous system shifts from "this is my safe partner" to "this is danger" in a fraction of a second, and the push-away is the survival response (fight/flight) activating. The disgust is the body's memory of the original violation intruding into the present. She needs individual trauma work to process the sensory memories that are being activated, and the couple needs psychoeducation so that he can understand the response as neurological rather than relational. He also needs support — being the partner of a trauma survivor is its own challenge, and his feelings of rejection and confusion are valid even though they are not the result of her intention.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Expect the non-traumatised partner to have infinite patience.
**Why:** Partners of trauma survivors have their own needs, limits, and emotional responses. Framing everything as the trauma survivor's process can leave the partner invisible and resentful.

**Don't:** Use trauma as a reason to avoid accountability in relationships.
**Why:** Understanding that a reaction is trauma-driven does not exempt the person from responsibility for its impact. "My trauma made me do it" is an explanation, not an excuse.
