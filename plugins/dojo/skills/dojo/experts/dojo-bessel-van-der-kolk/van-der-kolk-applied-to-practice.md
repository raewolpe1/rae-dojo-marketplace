# Van der Kolk Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When someone wants daily practices grounded in van der Kolk's understanding of trauma and the body.
- When someone is between therapy sessions and needs tools for self-regulation.
- When someone wants a practical synthesis of the body-keeps-the-score approach.

## Core Concept
Van der Kolk's recommendations for daily practice follow logically from his central insight: if trauma lives in the body, then daily practice must engage the body. The cognitive understanding — knowing what happened, understanding its effects — is valuable but insufficient on its own. The nervous system needs direct, embodied re-training.

**Body awareness practice.** Spend a few minutes each day simply noticing your body — not trying to change anything, just observing. Where is there tension? Where is there ease? What is your breathing doing? This interoceptive capacity — the ability to feel yourself from the inside — is often damaged by trauma and is the foundation of self-regulation.

**Breathing.** The breath is the most accessible doorway to the autonomic nervous system. Slow, extended exhales activate the vagal brake, shifting the nervous system from sympathetic activation (fight/flight) toward parasympathetic calm. A simple practice: inhale for a count of four, exhale for a count of six or eight. Even two minutes of this shifts the autonomic state.

**Movement.** Van der Kolk recommends rhythmic, bilateral movement — walking, swimming, dancing, drumming — because rhythmic patterns help regulate the nervous system. Trauma-sensitive yoga is his most-studied recommendation, but any movement that you can do with awareness of your body is beneficial.

**Social engagement.** The ventral vagal system — the newest evolutionary branch of the autonomic nervous system — is activated through safe social connection: eye contact, vocal prosody, facial expression, physical co-presence with a trusted person. Isolation is the enemy of trauma recovery. Even when connection feels difficult, small doses of safe social engagement are therapeutic.

**Grounding.** When triggered or dissociating, use sensory grounding: feel your feet on the floor, hold something with a distinct texture, name five things you can see, splash cold water on your face. These practices engage the sensory present to override the sensory past that your nervous system is replaying.

**Creative expression.** Writing, drawing, music, theatre, dance — creative modalities allow expression of material that cannot be put into words. Van der Kolk's research with theatre programmes showed significant improvements in self-regulation among traumatised youth. The creative act restores agency — the capacity to shape one's own experience.

**Sleep hygiene.** Trauma profoundly disrupts sleep, and sleep deprivation worsens every trauma symptom. Consistent sleep schedule, limited stimulants, a cool dark room, and a calming pre-sleep routine are foundational. If nightmares persist, seek specialised treatment (imagery rehearsal therapy has good evidence).

## How to Apply
1. **Start with one practice.** Do not attempt everything at once. Choose the practice that feels most accessible — breathing is often the easiest entry point — and do it daily for a week before adding anything else.
2. **Expect discomfort.** Body awareness practices can initially increase distress by bringing attention to sensations the person has been avoiding. This is normal but should not be overwhelming. If a practice produces flooding or dissociation, back off and try a gentler version.
3. **Prioritise regularity over intensity.** Five minutes of breathing practice daily is more effective than one hour weekly. The nervous system responds to consistent signals, not dramatic interventions.
4. **Track your window of tolerance.** Over weeks and months of practice, notice whether your capacity to tolerate strong emotion, physical sensation, and relational intensity is expanding. This is the measurable outcome of nervous system re-regulation.
5. **Combine self-practice with professional support.** These practices are most effective as complements to therapy, not replacements for it. If you are dealing with severe trauma, PTSD, or dissociation, work with a trauma-informed professional.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man with a history of childhood trauma has started therapy but finds that he is easily overwhelmed between sessions. He asks what he can do daily to manage his symptoms.
**Application:** Build a minimal daily practice: morning body awareness (three minutes of noticing sensation, not trying to change it), breathing practice during transitions (the four-count inhale, six-count exhale, twice a day for two minutes), one physical activity that involves rhythmic movement (walking, swimming), and one moment of intentional social connection (even brief — a conversation with a trusted friend, a meal with family). If he is triggered during the day, use sensory grounding: feel feet on floor, name what he can see, hold something cool or textured. These practices are not cures. They are nervous system training — small, repeated doses of regulation that gradually widen the window of tolerance.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Prescribe intensive body practices to someone who is severely dissociated.
**Why:** Body awareness can be retraumatising for someone whose body is where the trauma lives. Begin with the gentlest possible version — noticing one hand, feeling one foot — and build from there.

**Don't:** Replace professional treatment with self-help.
**Why:** Daily practices complement therapy. They do not substitute for it. Severe trauma, complex PTSD, and dissociative disorders require professional guidance.
