# The Body Keeps the Score

## When to Use
- When someone describes physical symptoms that intensify under stress and have no clear medical explanation.
- When someone has done extensive talk therapy for trauma but the body still carries tension, pain, or reactivity.
- When someone wants to understand the foundational insight of van der Kolk's work.

## Fails When
- **You attribute every physical symptom to trauma.** Medical conditions require medical evaluation. The body-keeps-the-score principle does not replace differential diagnosis.
- **You intellectualise the insight.** Knowing that trauma lives in the body is not the same as doing the work of engaging the body in healing. The concept must lead to practice.

## Core Concept
Van der Kolk's central thesis — the organising principle of his life's work — is that traumatic experiences are not stored merely as narrative memories that can be talked through. They are encoded in the body itself: as muscular tension patterns, as autonomic nervous system dysregulation, as altered stress hormone levels, as changes in brain structure and function, and as visceral sensations that persist long after the event has ended.

The person who was physically abused as a child may carry chronic tension in the shoulders and neck — the body's perpetual brace for impact — decades later, in a life that contains no physical threat. The combat veteran may experience a racing heart and hyperventilation at a loud noise — the body's alarm system firing because it was never told that the war ended. The sexual assault survivor may feel nausea and dissociation during consensual intimacy — the body replaying the script of the original violation.

These are not psychological metaphors. They are measurable physiological states. Brain scans of traumatised individuals show that when traumatic memories are activated, Broca's area — the speech centre — goes offline, while the amygdala — the threat detector — goes into overdrive, and sensory areas of the brain light up as though the event is happening now rather than being remembered. The body does not distinguish between remembering trauma and re-experiencing it.

This has profound implications for treatment. If trauma lives in the body, then treatment must engage the body. Talk therapy — the dominant modality in Western mental health — asks the person to narrate their experience, to put words to what happened. But if the brain's speech centre shuts down during traumatic recall, then verbal narrative is precisely what the traumatised person cannot produce. The therapy is asking the impossible. This does not mean talk therapy is useless — it means it is insufficient. It must be supplemented by approaches that work with the body directly: somatic experiencing, yoga, EMDR, neurofeedback, rhythmic movement, and other modalities that engage the sensory-motor system where trauma is stored.

## How to Apply
1. **Listen to the body.** If you carry chronic physical tension, pain, or reactivity that has no clear medical cause, consider the possibility that your body is holding traumatic memory. This is not self-diagnosis — it is a hypothesis worth exploring with a professional.
2. **Notice the body's responses under stress.** When you are triggered, what does your body do? Does your chest tighten? Does your stomach clench? Do you feel frozen or agitated? These body signatures are data — they map the specific way your nervous system learned to respond to threat.
3. **Engage the body in healing.** If you have trauma that has not responded fully to talk therapy, explore body-based modalities: yoga (especially trauma-sensitive yoga), somatic experiencing, EMDR, or even simple practices like mindful movement and breath work. The body needs its own healing process.
4. **Understand that the body's responses are intelligent.** Your body is not malfunctioning when it tenses, races, or freezes. It is doing what it learned to do in an environment that was genuinely threatening. The task is not to fight the body but to help it learn that the threat has passed.

## Examples
**Situation:** A woman in her forties has been in talk therapy for five years, processing childhood emotional abuse. She can narrate her history fluently and has genuine insight into its effects on her life. But she still carries chronic neck and shoulder pain, experiences waves of nausea when she feels criticised, and cannot tolerate being touched unexpectedly. Her therapist considers her treatment largely successful.
**Application:** Van der Kolk would say this treatment is incomplete. The insight is real but it has not reached the body. Her neck and shoulder pain is the body's continued brace against the verbal assaults she endured as a child. Her nausea when criticised is the visceral component of the threat response that was never resolved somatically. Her touch reactivity is the body's alarm system still calibrated to an environment where physical contact was unpredictable. She needs body-oriented work — somatic experiencing to help her nervous system complete the defensive responses that were thwarted in childhood, trauma-sensitive yoga to restore a safe relationship with her own body, possibly EMDR to reprocess the sensory components of the memories that talk therapy addressed only narratively.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use "the body keeps the score" as a diagnostic label for everything.
**Why:** Not every physical symptom is trauma. Bodies have medical conditions too. The insight is that the body *can* hold trauma, not that it *always* holds trauma when you feel unwell.

**Don't:** Attempt to force the body to release trauma through intense cathartic methods without professional guidance.
**Why:** The body's defences are there for a reason. Overwhelming them — through aggressive bodywork, forced breathwork, or unsupported psychedelic experiences — can retraumatise rather than heal.
