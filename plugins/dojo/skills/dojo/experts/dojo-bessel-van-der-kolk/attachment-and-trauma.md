# Attachment and Trauma

## When to Use
- When someone's difficulties are fundamentally relational — they cannot sustain intimacy, they oscillate between clinging and withdrawal, or they are drawn to relationships that replicate early patterns.
- When someone's trauma occurred within the attachment relationship — when the caregiver was both the source of danger and the source of comfort.
- When someone needs to understand that their relational patterns are not character flaws but attachment adaptations.

## Fails When
- **You present attachment categories as destiny.** Attachment patterns formed in childhood are strong but not permanent. Earned secure attachment — developed through therapy, healthy relationships, and conscious work — is well-documented.
- **You treat attachment theory as the only lens.** Attachment is one dimension of trauma's impact. It interacts with neurobiology, social context, culture, and individual temperament.

## Core Concept
Van der Kolk integrates attachment theory — Bowlby, Ainsworth, Main — into his understanding of trauma, arguing that the most devastating trauma occurs within the attachment relationship itself. When the person who is supposed to provide safety is also the source of danger, the child faces an impossible dilemma: they need to approach the caregiver for comfort (attachment drive) and simultaneously flee the caregiver for safety (survival drive). This produces what Mary Main called disorganised attachment — a pattern characterised by contradictory, confused, and fragmented behaviour.

Disorganised attachment is the attachment pattern most strongly associated with later psychopathology because it leaves the child without a coherent strategy for managing distress. Securely attached children seek comfort and are soothed. Avoidantly attached children suppress distress and manage alone. Anxiously attached children amplify distress to capture attention. But disorganised children have no strategy — they approach and withdraw simultaneously, freeze in place, or dissociate. Their internal working model of relationships is: the person I need is the person who hurts me.

This pattern persists into adulthood as a template for all intimate relationships. The adult with a disorganised attachment history may desperately want closeness and simultaneously fear it, may oscillate between idealising and demonising partners, may sabotage relationships at the point when they become truly intimate, or may remain in abusive relationships because the pattern — danger embedded in closeness — feels normal.

Van der Kolk emphasises that attachment is not just a psychological phenomenon. It is a biological regulatory system. The attuned caregiver literally regulates the infant's nervous system — through touch, voice, rhythm, and facial expression. When this co-regulation is absent or disrupted, the child's capacity for self-regulation is impaired at a biological level. This is why developmental trauma produces pervasive emotional dysregulation: the regulatory system was never properly installed.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess attachment history.** Not just whether abuse occurred but the quality of the caregiving relationship. Was there consistent attunement? Was comfort available when needed? Was the caregiver predictable? Was the caregiver also the source of fear?
2. **Understand your relational template.** Your attachment pattern shapes what feels normal in relationships. If danger-in-closeness was your childhood template, then safe relationships may feel boring or wrong, and chaotic ones may feel like home. Awareness of the template is the first step in changing it.
3. **Seek earned secure attachment.** Through a consistent therapeutic relationship, a stable friendship, or a partnership with someone who can be reliably present, new attachment experiences gradually re-wire the internal working model. This is not quick, but it is possible.
4. **Use the relationship itself as the healing agent.** In therapy, the relationship between therapist and client is not just the context for healing — it is the mechanism. A reliably attuned, safe, boundaried human connection provides the experience that development failed to provide.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man notices that every time a romantic relationship becomes serious, he sabotages it — picks a fight, withdraws emotionally, or becomes unfaithful. He genuinely wants lasting partnership but feels compelled to destroy it when it arrives. His mother was loving but erratic — warmly affectionate when sober, frightening and unpredictable when drinking.
**Application:** His behaviour is disorganised attachment playing out in adult relationships. Closeness activates both his attachment drive (come closer, I need you) and his survival drive (get away, closeness is dangerous). The sabotage is the survival drive winning — destroying the relationship before it can hurt him the way his mother's unpredictability did. He is not a bad person or a commitment-phobe. He is a person whose nervous system learned, accurately, that love and danger come in the same package. Healing requires not just understanding this pattern but having a corrective relational experience — typically in therapy — where he can experience closeness that remains safe, where the attuned other does not become dangerous, where he can gradually update his internal model from "closeness = danger" to "closeness can also mean safety."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Tell someone with disorganised attachment to "just trust people."
**Why:** Their body has learned, through direct experience, that trust is dangerous. Telling them to override that learning through willpower is like telling someone with a broken leg to just walk. The capacity for trust must be rebuilt somatically and relationally, not imposed cognitively.

**Don't:** Romanticise insecure attachment as depth or intensity.
**Why:** The passion of anxious-avoidant dynamics is often mistaken for love. It is activation — the nervous system cycling between attachment panic and survival withdrawal. Stable love feels calmer, which can be misread as absence of feeling by someone whose baseline is chaos.
