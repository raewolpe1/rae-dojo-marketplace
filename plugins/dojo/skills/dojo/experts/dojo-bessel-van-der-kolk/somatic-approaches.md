# Somatic Approaches

## When to Use
- When someone is ready to engage the body in their trauma recovery rather than staying exclusively in verbal/cognitive modalities.
- When someone has experienced the limits of talk therapy and wants to understand alternatives.
- When someone is disconnected from their body and needs to rebuild a safe relationship with physical sensation.

## Fails When
- **You push body awareness on someone who is not ready.** For some trauma survivors, attending to the body is initially retraumatising because their body is where the trauma lives. The approach must be titrated — gentle, invitational, always within the window of tolerance.
- **You present a single modality as the answer.** Van der Kolk advocates a toolkit, not a dogma. Yoga is not enough for everyone. Somatic experiencing is not enough for everyone. The approach must match the person.

## Core Concept
Van der Kolk's research and clinical experience have led him to advocate for a family of body-based therapeutic approaches that share a common insight: since trauma is stored in the body, healing must involve the body.

**Trauma-sensitive yoga** is one of the modalities van der Kolk has researched most extensively. Unlike conventional yoga, trauma-sensitive yoga prioritises interoception (the capacity to feel one's own body from the inside), agency (every instruction is an invitation, never a command), and present-moment awareness (attending to what is rather than what should be). Van der Kolk's research demonstrated that yoga produced greater reductions in PTSD symptoms than any pharmaceutical intervention studied to that point — because it directly addressed the somatic core of the disorder.

**Somatic experiencing**, developed by Peter Levine, works on the principle that traumatic energy is trapped in the body and needs to be discharged. The approach involves titrated awareness of body sensation, allowing the nervous system to complete the defensive responses (fight, flight) that were thwarted during the original event. If the person froze during the trauma, somatic experiencing helps the body complete the movement that was interrupted — the running, the pushing away, the escape — in a safe, controlled way.

**Neurofeedback** uses real-time brain imaging to help the person develop more flexible brain wave patterns. Van der Kolk has found it particularly useful for people with severe developmental trauma whose brain patterns are rigidly stuck in states of hyperarousal or hypoarousal.

**Theatre and movement therapies** engage the body through creative expression — allowing traumatised people to literally embody different roles, different movements, different ways of being in their body. Van der Kolk's work with theatre groups showed that traumatised adolescents who participated in dramatic performances showed significant improvements in self-regulation and interpersonal functioning.

The common thread across all these modalities: they help the person develop a new relationship with their body — one in which the body is experienced as a source of information and pleasure rather than a repository of pain and danger.

## How to Apply
1. **Start with interoception.** Can you feel your body? Can you notice the sensation of your feet on the floor, your breath moving, your heart beating? If body awareness is difficult or frightening, begin there — not with intense body work but with gentle, supported noticing.
2. **Choose a modality that matches your readiness.** Yoga if you can tolerate being in your body in a group setting. Somatic experiencing if you need one-to-one, carefully titrated body awareness. Neurofeedback if your dysregulation is severe. Movement or dance if creative expression feels safer than clinical work.
3. **Respect the body's pace.** The body has its own timeline for releasing trauma. Pushing for faster resolution — more intense yoga, more aggressive bodywork — can overwhelm the nervous system. Slow and steady, within the window of tolerance, produces lasting change.
4. **Combine with other modalities.** Somatic approaches are most powerful when combined with relational therapy, EMDR, and cognitive work. The body and the mind need to heal together.

## Examples
**Situation:** A woman has been in weekly talk therapy for three years, processing childhood sexual abuse. She has significant insight into her history and its effects. But she still cannot tolerate being touched, experiences chronic pelvic pain with no medical cause, and dissociates during physical intimacy.
**Application:** Her insight is genuine but it has not reached her body. The trauma lives in her pelvic floor, in her skin's hypersensitivity, in the automatic dissociation that activates when her body is engaged sexually. Van der Kolk would recommend adding trauma-sensitive yoga — specifically, practices that gently rebuild her connection to her body in a safe, controlled, non-sexualised context. The yoga class is not about flexibility or fitness. It is about learning to inhabit her body again — to feel her feet, her hands, her breath — without the body becoming a source of danger. Over time, somatic experiencing could help her nervous system complete the defensive responses that were suppressed during the abuse, releasing the trapped energy that manifests as chronic pain.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Start with intense body work for someone who is severely dissociated from their body.
**Why:** The dissociation is protecting them from somatic material they cannot yet tolerate. Begin with gentle, invitational body awareness and build gradually.

**Don't:** Assume body-based approaches are soft or supplementary.
**Why:** Van der Kolk's research shows that somatic approaches can be more effective than pharmacotherapy for PTSD. They engage the mechanism where trauma is stored. They are not adjunctive — they are central.
