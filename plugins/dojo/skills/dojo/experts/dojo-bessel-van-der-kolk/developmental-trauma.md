# Developmental Trauma

## When to Use
- When someone's psychological difficulties cannot be traced to a single event but rather to a pervasive childhood environment of neglect, abuse, unpredictability, or emotional absence.
- When someone's symptoms are diffuse — problems with identity, emotional regulation, relationships, self-worth, and bodily self-care — rather than the focused re-experiencing symptoms of classic PTSD.
- When someone asks why childhood experiences have such lasting power.

## Fails When
- **You treat developmental trauma the same as single-event PTSD.** A combat veteran who develops PTSD had a formed personality that was disrupted by a traumatic event. A child who grows up in chronic adversity never had the chance to form a stable personality in the first place. The treatment implications are profoundly different.
- **You suggest that insight alone can resolve developmental trauma.** Understanding your childhood is necessary but it does not re-wire the nervous system that was shaped by it.

## Core Concept
Van der Kolk spent years campaigning for the recognition of Developmental Trauma Disorder (DTD) as a distinct diagnostic category — separate from PTSD, which was designed to describe the effects of single-event trauma on previously well-functioning adults. His argument: when trauma occurs during the developmental period — when the brain, the attachment system, and the sense of self are still forming — the effects are qualitatively different from adult-onset trauma.

The child who grows up in a violent household does not merely have frightening memories. Their entire nervous system is calibrated to threat. Their stress response system is set on high alert because that was what the environment required. Their attachment system is disorganised because the person who was supposed to provide safety was also the source of danger. Their sense of self is fragmented because they never had the stable mirroring that coherent identity requires. Their capacity for emotional regulation is impaired because they never had a co-regulating caregiver who taught them, through attunement, how to manage their own states.

The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) study, which van der Kolk frequently cites, demonstrated that these effects are dose-dependent. The more categories of childhood adversity a person experienced (physical abuse, emotional neglect, parental substance abuse, domestic violence, etc.), the higher their risk for virtually every major health problem in adulthood: heart disease, cancer, autoimmune disorders, depression, addiction, suicide. The body keeps the score across a lifetime.

Van der Kolk's frustration with the diagnostic system is that children with developmental trauma are typically given multiple diagnoses — ADHD, oppositional defiant disorder, conduct disorder, bipolar disorder — rather than a single diagnosis that captures the underlying cause. A child who cannot sit still in class because their nervous system is in perpetual hyperarousal gets labelled ADHD and medicated, when the actual problem is that their body never learned safety.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess for developmental history.** When symptoms are pervasive (emotional dysregulation, relationship difficulties, identity confusion, somatic complaints), ask about childhood: not just overt abuse but also neglect, emotional absence, unpredictability, and household dysfunction.
2. **Understand the scope of impact.** Developmental trauma affects identity, body awareness, emotional regulation, cognition, and relational capacity. Treatment must be equally comprehensive — not a single modality but a coordinated approach.
3. **Prioritise safety and regulation before processing.** The person whose nervous system was never calibrated to safety needs to build that foundation before trauma memories can be processed. Phase-based treatment: stabilisation, then processing, then integration.
4. **Build the capacities that development failed to provide.** Emotional regulation skills. Bodily awareness. Distress tolerance. Relational trust. These are not deficits to be fixed but developmental achievements that were prevented by the environment — and they can be cultivated at any age.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man in his forties reports chronic difficulty maintaining relationships, an unstable sense of identity, emotional volatility that he cannot control, and a persistent feeling that he is fundamentally flawed. He has been diagnosed with borderline personality disorder, depression, and ADHD at various points. His childhood was characterised by an alcoholic father and an emotionally absent mother.
**Application:** Van der Kolk would look at this presentation and see developmental trauma — not three separate disorders. The emotional volatility is a nervous system that never learned regulation because there was no attuned caregiver to co-regulate with. The identity instability is the absence of the consistent mirroring that coherent selfhood requires. The relationship difficulties are disorganised attachment playing out in adult relationships. The concentration problems are a hypervigilant nervous system that was trained to scan for threat, not to sustain focus. Treatment needs to address the root: building the regulatory capacities that development failed to provide (body-based work, somatic experiencing, yoga), establishing relational safety (with a therapist who can serve as a co-regulating presence), and only then processing the specific traumatic memories that the nervous system continues to replay.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce developmental trauma to a list of bad things that happened.
**Why:** The defining feature of developmental trauma is not specific events but the pervasive environment — the absence of safety, attunement, and predictability during the period when the brain and self were forming.

**Don't:** Promise that healing will restore the person to a pre-trauma baseline.
**Why:** In developmental trauma, there is no pre-trauma baseline. The person was formed in the traumatic environment. Healing means building what was never built, not restoring what was lost. This is harder but also, in a sense, more creative — they are building a self that never existed before.
