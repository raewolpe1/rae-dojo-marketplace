# Trauma and the Brain

## When to Use
- When someone wants the specific neuroscience behind trauma's effects — which brain regions, what changes, and what that means for treatment.
- When someone needs to understand why they cannot put their trauma into words or why they feel taken over by reactions.
- When the technical detail provides therapeutic value — normalising, depathologising, or guiding treatment choices.

## Core Concept
Van der Kolk's neuroimaging research identified three critical brain changes in trauma. The amygdala (the brain's smoke detector) becomes hyperactive, triggering alarm responses to non-threatening stimuli. The medial prefrontal cortex (the brain's watchtower) becomes less active, reducing the capacity for self-observation and self-regulation. And Broca's area (the brain's speech centre) shows reduced activation during traumatic recall, producing what van der Kolk calls "speechless terror" — the literal inability to put overwhelming experience into words.

Additionally, trauma affects the thalamus — the brain's cook or integrator, which normally assembles sensory input into coherent experience. During traumatic overwhelm, the thalamus breaks down sensory experience into disconnected fragments: a sound here, a smell there, a visual flash, a body sensation. These fragments are stored without the time-stamp and narrative context that normal memory receives. This is why traumatic memories intrude as sensory fragments — not as coherent stories but as sounds, images, smells, and body sensations that feel like they are happening now.

The hippocampus, which normally provides context and time-stamping for memories ("this happened at that time in that place"), shows reduced functioning during traumatic encoding. The result: traumatic memories are stored without temporal context, which is why a twenty-year-old memory can produce the same physiological response as the original event.

These findings have direct treatment implications. If Broca's area shuts down during recall, talk therapy faces a fundamental constraint. If the thalamus fragments experience, treatment must help reassemble the fragments. If the hippocampus fails to timestamp, treatment must help the brain file the memory as past rather than present. EMDR, somatic experiencing, and neurofeedback all address these specific brain-level disruptions.

## How to Apply
1. **Use the three-part model.** Smoke detector (amygdala) is too sensitive. Watchtower (prefrontal cortex) goes offline. Speech centre (Broca's) shuts down. This gives people a simple, accurate framework for understanding their experience.
2. **Normalise speechlessness.** When someone struggles to articulate their trauma, explain that their brain is functioning exactly as the research predicts. This is not resistance or avoidance — it is neurobiology.
3. **Match treatment to brain pattern.** Overactive amygdala → calming practices (yoga, breathing, neurofeedback). Offline prefrontal cortex → mindfulness and body-awareness practices that rebuild self-observation. Impaired Broca's → non-verbal modalities (EMDR, somatic experiencing, art, movement).
4. **Emphasise neuroplasticity.** The brain changed under trauma. It can change under healing. Every safe experience, every regulated breath, every moment of present-moment awareness is building new neural pathways.

## Examples
**Situation:** A trauma survivor in therapy becomes increasingly frustrated: "My therapist keeps asking me to describe what happened and I just go blank. I know the words should be there but they disappear. She probably thinks I'm holding back."
**Application:** She is experiencing the Broca's area shutdown that van der Kolk documented through neuroimaging. During traumatic recall, the brain region responsible for putting experience into words literally becomes less active. She is not holding back — she is hitting a neurological wall. Her therapist may need to shift modalities: rather than asking her to narrate, work with the body sensations, images, and emotions that are available. EMDR can process the memory without requiring the person to verbalise it in detail. The narrative will often emerge after the somatic processing — once the brain's alarm system calms down, Broca's area comes back online and the words become available.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use brain science to create a sense of permanent damage.
**Why:** Every brain change described here is reversible through appropriate therapeutic experience. The brain that learned danger can learn safety.

**Don't:** Reduce the person to their brain scans.
**Why:** The neuroscience explains mechanism. It does not capture the person's full experience, meaning-making, or humanity.
