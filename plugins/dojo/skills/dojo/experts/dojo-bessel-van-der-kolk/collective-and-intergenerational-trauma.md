# Collective and Intergenerational Trauma

## When to Use
- When trauma has a dimension larger than the individual — community, cultural, historical, or intergenerational.
- When someone recognises that their family carries patterns (hypervigilance, emotional restriction, substance abuse, distrust of authority) that seem connected to historical events.
- When a community or organisation is dealing with the aftermath of collective adversity.

## Core Concept
Van der Kolk recognises that trauma is not only an individual phenomenon. It occurs in social contexts and transmits through social processes. War, genocide, slavery, colonisation, apartheid, forced displacement, systemic racism — these produce trauma at a population level, and that trauma does not end when the event ends. It persists in families, communities, and cultures across generations.

Intergenerational transmission occurs through multiple mechanisms. Behaviourally, traumatised parents may be hypervigilant, emotionally unavailable, or explosive — shaping their children's attachment patterns and nervous system calibration. Narratively, family stories (or silences) transmit the meaning-framework through which subsequent generations interpret the world. Socially, communities shaped by collective trauma develop norms, expectations, and coping patterns (distrust, self-reliance, emotional suppression) that persist as cultural features long after the original threat has passed.

Emerging epigenetic research suggests that trauma may also transmit biologically — through changes in gene expression that alter stress response systems and are passed to offspring. Van der Kolk cites research on the children and grandchildren of Holocaust survivors and famine survivors showing altered cortisol profiles and stress reactivity, though he is careful to note that this science is still developing.

The treatment implications are significant. Individual therapy for someone carrying intergenerational trauma must acknowledge the collective dimension — the person is not merely processing their own experience but carrying the unresolved material of previous generations. Community-level interventions — truth and reconciliation processes, collective mourning rituals, restoration of cultural practices disrupted by trauma — are essential and cannot be replaced by individual treatment.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess for intergenerational patterns.** What are the family's unspoken rules? What emotions are forbidden? What stories are never told? What survival strategies seem to have been passed down? These often map to the historical traumas the family endured.
2. **Connect individual symptoms to collective context.** The hypervigilance that seems personal may be the legacy of a grandparent who survived persecution. The emotional restriction may be the inheritance of a culture that survived through stoicism. Understanding the collective dimension depersonalises the symptom without minimising it.
3. **Honour the collective dimension.** Individual healing for intergenerational trauma often involves reconnecting with the community, culture, or history that was disrupted. This is not sentimentality — it is restoring the relational and cultural resources that trauma destroyed.
4. **Advocate for social conditions that prevent collective trauma.** Van der Kolk insists that individual treatment without attention to the social conditions that produce and perpetuate trauma is insufficient. Poverty, racism, displacement, and systemic neglect are ongoing sources of collective traumatisation.

## Examples
**Situation:** A South African man in his forties — the grandchild of people who lived through forced removals and the violence of apartheid — carries chronic anxiety, a deep distrust of institutional authority, and an inability to feel safe in spaces where he is the only Black person. He has never personally experienced overt violence but his body responds as though threat is imminent.
**Application:** His symptoms are not disproportionate to his personal experience — they are proportionate to his inherited experience. His nervous system was calibrated in a family shaped by generations of state-inflicted trauma: forced removals that destroyed community, pass laws that criminalised movement, daily humiliation designed to communicate that his people's bodies and spaces were not their own. His hypervigilance in majority-white spaces is not paranoia but the survival coding transmitted through parental anxiety, family narrative, and community atmosphere. Individual therapy can help him develop a conscious relationship with these responses. But the work also has a collective dimension: engaging with community, reclaiming cultural practices disrupted by apartheid, and understanding his responses not as personal pathology but as the legitimate legacy of historical violence that continues to shape the present.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce intergenerational trauma to individual pathology.
**Why:** Telling someone whose community has been systematically traumatised to "work on their anxiety" without acknowledging the social context is a form of gaslighting. The context is constitutive, not incidental.

**Don't:** Dismiss intergenerational transmission as unscientific.
**Why:** The behavioural, narrative, and social mechanisms of transmission are well-established. The epigenetic mechanisms are still emerging but the preliminary evidence is compelling. The phenomenon is real regardless of which transmission mechanism dominates.
