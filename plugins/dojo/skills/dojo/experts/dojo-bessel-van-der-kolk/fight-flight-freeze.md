# Fight, Flight, Freeze

## When to Use
- When someone needs to understand why their body responds to perceived threat with rage, panic, shutdown, or compliance — seemingly without their consent.
- When someone judges themselves for how they responded during a traumatic event ("Why didn't I fight back?" "Why did I freeze?").
- When someone wants to understand the autonomic nervous system's role in trauma.

## Core Concept
Van der Kolk, drawing on the work of Stephen Porges (polyvagal theory) and others, describes a hierarchy of survival responses that the autonomic nervous system activates in sequence when threat is detected.

The first response is social engagement — seeking help, calling out, looking to others for co-regulation. This is the most recent evolutionary development and depends on the ventral vagal system. If social engagement fails or is unavailable (no help is coming, the threat is the caregiver), the system escalates.

The second tier is mobilisation — fight or flight. The sympathetic nervous system floods the body with adrenaline and cortisol, preparing for physical action. Heart rate increases, muscles tense, vision narrows, blood flow redirects to the limbs. This is the body preparing to attack or escape. If mobilisation succeeds, the threat is resolved and the system returns to baseline.

If neither fight nor flight is possible — the person is pinned down, the child is too small, the threat is inescapable — the system collapses to its most primitive defence: freeze and shutdown. The dorsal vagal system takes over. Heart rate drops, blood pressure falls, pain perception decreases, consciousness narrows or dissociates. This is the body's last resort — playing dead, conserving resources, numbing the pain of what cannot be escaped.

The fawn response — compulsive compliance, people-pleasing, submitting to the aggressor's demands — is another survival strategy, particularly common in children who learn that appeasing the dangerous caregiver reduces the risk of harm.

The critical therapeutic insight: none of these responses are chosen. They are activated by the autonomic nervous system faster than the cortex can deliberate. The person who froze during an assault did not choose to freeze. Their nervous system assessed that fight and flight were impossible and activated the only remaining survival option. The person who fawned — who was compliant or even seemingly cooperative during abuse — was not consenting. Their nervous system selected the strategy most likely to minimise harm.

Understanding this is often the most therapeutically significant insight a trauma survivor can receive. The shame of "I didn't fight back" or "I went along with it" dissolves when the person understands that their response was automatic, adaptive, and beyond voluntary control.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify your default survival response.** Under stress, do you tend to fight (anger, aggression, confrontation), flee (avoidance, escape, distraction), freeze (shutdown, numbness, immobility), or fawn (compliance, people-pleasing, appeasement)? Your default was shaped by which response worked best in your early environment.
2. **Stop judging your survival responses.** If you froze during trauma, you did not fail. If you fawned, you did not consent. Your nervous system activated the best available option for survival.
3. **Notice when survival responses fire in non-threatening situations.** The rage at a minor provocation (fight). The urge to leave the room when emotions arise (flight). The numbing during intimacy (freeze). The automatic compliance with unreasonable demands (fawn). These are survival responses firing in contexts where survival is not at stake.
4. **Build the capacity to choose.** Through body awareness, grounding, and nervous system regulation practices, you can gradually expand the space between trigger and response — allowing conscious choice to enter where automatic reaction previously controlled.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man is tormented by the fact that when he was mugged at knifepoint, he froze completely — could not speak, could not move, handed over his wallet mechanically, and stood motionless for minutes after the mugger left. He believes his freeze response was cowardly.
**Application:** His freeze was not cowardice. It was the dorsal vagal response — the most primitive survival circuit activating when the nervous system assessed that fight (against a weapon) and flight (at close range) were impossible. The freeze response reduced his movement (making him less of a target for further violence), numbed his pain perception (in case of injury), and dissociated his consciousness (reducing the psychological impact of the helplessness). His nervous system saved his life using the only strategy available. The shame he carries is based on a misunderstanding: he believes he chose to freeze. He did not. His body chose for him, and it chose correctly.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Rank survival responses as better or worse.
**Why:** Fight is not braver than freeze. Flight is not more adaptive than fawn. Each response is the nervous system's best available option given the circumstances. Ranking them imports moral judgment into what is a biological process.

**Don't:** Try to override survival responses through willpower.
**Why:** Survival responses are subcortical — they operate below the level of conscious control. You cannot will yourself out of a freeze response any more than you can will your heart to stop beating. Change comes through nervous system regulation, not through force of will.
