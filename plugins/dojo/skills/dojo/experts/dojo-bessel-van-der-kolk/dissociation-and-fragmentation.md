# Dissociation and Fragmentation

## When to Use
- When someone describes feeling disconnected from their body, emotions, or sense of identity.
- When someone reports memory gaps, time distortions, or the sense of watching their life from outside.
- When someone's trauma responses include numbing, shutdown, or the feeling of not being real.

## Fails When
- **You treat dissociation as a problem to be eliminated rather than a survival strategy to be honoured and gradually replaced.** Dissociation kept the person alive. It needs to be understood before it can be changed.
- **You push for integration before the person has established enough safety to tolerate the material that dissociation has been protecting them from.**

## Core Concept
Van der Kolk understands dissociation as the mind's last-resort defence against overwhelming experience. When fight is impossible and flight is impossible, the organism has one remaining option: it leaves. Not physically — psychologically. Consciousness separates from the unbearable experience. The person is there but not there. Their body is present but their mind has vacated.

This is adaptive in the moment. The child who is being abused by a caregiver cannot fight (too small) and cannot flee (too dependent). Dissociation allows the child to survive the intolerable by not being fully present for it. The combat soldier trapped under fire with no escape route dissociates — the terror, the pain, the sensory overwhelm are placed behind a wall of unreality.

The problem is that dissociation, once established as a defensive pattern, does not confine itself to traumatic situations. It generalises. The person begins to dissociate in response to any strong emotion, any interpersonal conflict, any reminder of the original trauma. They report feeling foggy, disconnected, watching their life through glass. They lose time — hours or days that they cannot account for. Their sense of self becomes fragmented — different situations seem to produce different selves, with poor continuity between them.

In severe cases, this fragmentation manifests as distinct self-states, each carrying different memories, affects, and body states. Van der Kolk recognises this continuum — from normal dissociation (daydreaming) through peritraumatic dissociation (during the event) to chronic dissociative patterns (structural dissociation) to dissociative identity disorder. The treatment approach differs along the continuum, but the underlying principle is the same: dissociation served a protective function, and healing requires building enough internal safety that the person can gradually be present for the experiences that dissociation has been shielding them from.

## How to Apply
1. **Recognise the signs.** Emotional numbing, feeling unreal, memory gaps, feeling like you are watching your life from outside, sudden shifts in behaviour or sense of self — these may indicate dissociative patterns.
2. **Honour the defence.** Dissociation kept you alive. Before trying to change it, acknowledge its function. The goal is not to eliminate dissociation but to make it unnecessary by building internal safety.
3. **Build grounding capacity.** Grounding practices — feeling your feet on the floor, engaging your senses, orienting to the present environment — help the nervous system return from dissociative states. These are not cures but tools for gradually increasing the capacity to stay present.
4. **Work with a trauma-informed professional.** Chronic dissociation, especially with fragmentation, requires specialised treatment. This is not self-help territory. Somatic experiencing, EMDR (carefully titrated), IFS, and sensorimotor psychotherapy all address dissociation, but the work must be paced to avoid overwhelming the system.

## Examples
**Situation:** A woman reports that during arguments with her partner, she "goes away" — her vision narrows, sounds become distant, and she feels as though she is watching the interaction from across the room. She cannot respond and later has only fragmentary memories of what was said. Her partner experiences this as emotional withdrawal and feels abandoned.
**Application:** This is peritraumatic-level dissociation being triggered by a present-day interpersonal situation. The argument activates the same threat circuitry as her childhood experience of parental conflict — which she survived by psychologically leaving. Her body is replaying a defensive strategy that was adaptive at age seven and is destructive at age forty. The work is dual: first, building her capacity to stay present during moderate emotional arousal (expanding her window of tolerance through body-based practices), and second, processing the original traumatic material so that present-day conflict stops activating the childhood defence. Her partner also needs psychoeducation — this is not choice or manipulation; it is the nervous system's emergency protocol firing inappropriately.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Try to "snap someone out of" a dissociative state with sudden stimuli.
**Why:** Sudden, startling interventions can push the person deeper into dissociation or trigger a fight response. Gentle sensory grounding — a calm voice, an invitation to feel their feet, a familiar scent — works with the nervous system rather than against it.

**Don't:** Interpret dissociation as resistance, avoidance, or manipulation.
**Why:** Dissociation is involuntary. The person is not choosing to check out any more than they chose the trauma that installed the pattern.
