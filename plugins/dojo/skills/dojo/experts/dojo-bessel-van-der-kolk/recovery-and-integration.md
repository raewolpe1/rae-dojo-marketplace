# Recovery and Integration

## When to Use
- When someone has done stabilisation work and is asking: what does healing actually look like?
- When someone needs to understand that recovery is possible without being promised that it is simple.
- When someone wants a roadmap for the phases of trauma recovery.

## Core Concept
Van der Kolk is careful about the language of recovery. He does not promise that trauma can be erased or that the person will return to a pre-trauma state. What he offers is integration: the traumatic experience becomes part of the person's story rather than the controlling force of their life. The memory remains but it loses its power to hijack the nervous system. The body softens. The brain updates its threat detection. The person can be present in the current moment rather than trapped in the past.

Recovery follows a phase-based structure, drawing on Judith Herman's three-phase model that van der Kolk endorses. Phase one: safety and stabilisation — establishing both external safety (a safe environment, stable relationships) and internal safety (the capacity to regulate one's own nervous system). Phase two: processing and mourning — working through the traumatic memories and grieving what was lost (childhood, innocence, trust, time, opportunities). Phase three: reconnection and integration — rebuilding a life that is not organised around trauma, developing new relationships and activities, and finding meaning.

The phases are not linear. The person may cycle through them repeatedly, returning to stabilisation when processing destabilises, returning to mourning when reconnection surfaces new grief. This is normal, not failure.

Van der Kolk adds to this model his insistence that recovery must engage the body. Integration is not only cognitive — knowing what happened and understanding its effects. It is somatic — the nervous system must learn that the threat has passed, the body must release the patterns of tension and vigilance, and the person must develop the capacity for physical pleasure, relaxation, and safe human touch.

The markers of integration: the person can recall the traumatic experience without being flooded by it. They can be present in their body without dissociating. They can tolerate intimacy without being triggered. They can experience strong emotions without being overwhelmed. They can distinguish between past danger and present safety. They have a narrative that includes the trauma without being defined by it.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess phase readiness.** Do not rush to processing before stabilisation is secure. Can the person regulate their arousal? Do they have external support? Can they tolerate moderate distress without dissociating?
2. **Expect non-linear progress.** Healing is not a straight line. Bad days, setbacks, and temporary regressions are normal. They do not mean treatment has failed — they mean the system is reorganising.
3. **Grieve what was lost.** Recovery is not only about symptom reduction. It involves mourning — the childhood that was stolen, the years spent surviving rather than living, the relationships that were damaged, the possibilities that were foreclosed.
4. **Build a life beyond trauma.** Integration means that trauma is part of the story but not the whole story. Reconnection involves developing new activities, relationships, and sources of meaning that are not organised around the traumatic experience.

## Examples
**Situation:** A woman, two years into trauma therapy, is frustrated because she still has bad days — moments of hypervigilance, occasional nightmares, and a tendency to withdraw when stressed. She wonders if therapy is working.
**Application:** It is working. The markers of progress are not the absence of all symptoms but the change in their frequency, intensity, and duration, and in the person's relationship to them. Two years ago, hypervigilance was constant; now it is occasional. Nightmares were nightly; now they are monthly. Withdrawal was automatic and lasting; now she notices it, can name it, and can re-engage within hours rather than days. The question is not "Am I cured?" but "Can I notice what is happening, regulate myself, and return to the present?" If yes, integration is progressing. Recovery is not the absence of trauma's legacy — it is the presence of choice in how she relates to it.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Define recovery as the complete elimination of symptoms.
**Why:** Some imprint of significant trauma may persist — a heightened startle response, a sensitivity to certain triggers, a depth of feeling about the experience. Integration means these no longer control the person's life, not that they have disappeared entirely.

**Don't:** Skip the mourning phase.
**Why:** The rush to "move on" and "get back to normal" bypasses essential grief work. What was lost to trauma must be mourned — not wallowed in, but genuinely honoured — before authentic reconnection is possible.
