# Clinical Economics of Development

## When to Use

- When applying a systematic diagnostic methodology to a development challenge.
- When generic prescriptions ("improve governance," "liberalise markets") are being applied without context-specific analysis.
- When the user needs a framework for moving from diagnosis to prescription.

## Fails When

- **The problem is well-diagnosed and the user needs implementation guidance.** Clinical economics is a diagnostic methodology; implementation requires different tools.
- **The constraints are primarily political rather than technical.** Clinical economics excels at identifying technical binding constraints; it is less effective at diagnosing and resolving political economy obstacles.

## Core Concept

Clinical economics is Sachs's signature methodological contribution — the argument that development practice should emulate clinical medicine. A doctor does not prescribe the same treatment for every patient; a development economist should not prescribe the same reforms for every country. The methodology requires differential diagnosis: systematic identification of the specific constraints binding a particular economy, followed by tailored treatment.

The clinical economics checklist includes: the poverty trap (is the economy below the threshold for self-sustaining growth?); the policy framework (are macroeconomic policies sound?); governance (is there sufficient state capacity and accountability?); geography (landlocked? tropical disease burden? ecological fragility?); fiscal framework (can the government finance necessary investments?); cultural barriers (are there social norms that constrain specific populations?); and geopolitics (is the country affected by conflict, sanctions, or unfavourable trade arrangements?).

The critical innovation is the insistence on specificity. The Washington Consensus prescribed liberalisation, privatisation, and stabilisation for every economy regardless of diagnosis. Sachs argues this is like prescribing aspirin for every patient: sometimes it helps, sometimes it's irrelevant, sometimes it kills. Bolivia's crisis in the 1980s required stabilisation; Poland's transition required rapid liberalisation; Africa's poverty requires scaled investment. Same economist, different diagnoses, different prescriptions — because the conditions differ.

The methodology also insists on follow-up. A doctor doesn't prescribe and walk away; clinical economics requires monitoring outcomes, adjusting treatment, and revising the diagnosis when the patient doesn't respond as expected. This iterative, evidence-responsive approach contrasts with the "one-size-fits-all" rigidity that Sachs attributes to the IMF and World Bank during the structural adjustment era.

## How to Apply

1. **Conduct differential diagnosis.** What specific constraints — health, infrastructure, geography, governance, fiscal, ecological — bind this economy? Rank them by severity.
2. **Match treatment to diagnosis.** The prescription should follow directly from the diagnosis. If the binding constraint is disease burden, lead with health investment. If it's infrastructure, lead with roads and electricity.
3. **Reject generic prescriptions.** If someone recommends "improve governance" or "liberalise markets" without diagnosing the specific governance failures or market distortions, push back. What specifically needs to change, and why?
4. **Monitor and adjust.** Set measurable indicators for whether the treatment is working. If it's not, revisit the diagnosis.

## Examples

**Situation:** An international consultant recommends that a district in Mpumalanga "improve governance and attract private investment" as the strategy for economic development.
**Application:** Clinical economics rejects this as non-diagnosis. What specific governance failures? Is it that the municipality can't manage infrastructure maintenance? That procurement is corrupt? That planning capacity is absent? Each of these has a different treatment. And "attract private investment" assumes that private investment is available and that the barriers to it are governance-related — but what if the barriers are infrastructure (no reliable electricity, no paved roads), health (workforce debilitated by disease), or geography (remote location far from markets)? The clinical approach: diagnose the specific binding constraints through data analysis and field investigation, then prescribe investments that address those specific constraints.
**Result:** Replace the generic recommendation with a specific, evidence-based diagnosis and a targeted investment plan.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use clinical economics as a metaphor without doing the actual diagnosis.
**Why:** The power of the approach is in the discipline of systematic analysis, not in the metaphor. Saying "we need a clinical approach" while skipping the diagnostic work produces the same generic prescriptions in fancier packaging.

**Don't:** Assume the diagnosis is complete after desk research.
**Why:** Clinical economics requires field investigation — talking to communities, visiting sites, understanding local conditions. Desk-based diagnosis misses the specifics that make the methodology valuable.
