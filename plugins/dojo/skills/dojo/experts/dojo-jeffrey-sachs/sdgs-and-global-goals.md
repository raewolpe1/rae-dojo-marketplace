# SDGs and Global Goals

## When to Use

- When aligning development programmes with the SDG framework.
- When the SDGs are being critiqued or defended.
- When integrated development planning needs a structuring framework.

## Fails When

- **The user needs specific SDG indicator methodologies.** This file covers the strategic framework, not measurement technicalities.
- **The SDGs are used as a compliance checklist rather than an analytical framework.** The value is in integration, not box-ticking.

## Core Concept

Sachs was centrally involved in the design of both the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs, 2000–2015) and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs, 2015–2030). He sees the SDGs as the most important policy framework for global development because they integrate what sectoral approaches fragment.

The SDGs' defining feature is integration. Seventeen goals covering poverty (SDG 1), hunger (SDG 2), health (SDG 3), education (SDG 4), gender equality (SDG 5), water (SDG 6), energy (SDG 7), economic growth (SDG 8), infrastructure (SDG 9), inequality (SDG 10), cities (SDG 11), consumption (SDG 12), climate (SDG 13), oceans (SDG 14), land ecosystems (SDG 15), peace and institutions (SDG 16), and partnerships (SDG 17). The integration reflects the reality that these dimensions are interconnected — you cannot achieve health without water, education without nutrition, economic growth without infrastructure, or any of them without climate resilience.

Sachs acknowledges the criticisms: too many goals, unmeasurable targets, no enforcement mechanism, tension between universal aspirations and country-specific conditions. His response is that imperfect integration beats perfect fragmentation. Before the MDGs, development discourse was scattered across competing agendas. The MDGs — and their successor SDGs — provided a common framework that aligned donors, governments, civil society, and the private sector around shared objectives.

For development practitioners, the SDG framework's value is analytical: it forces you to consider how your programme interacts with other development dimensions. A health programme that ignores water (SDG 6) or nutrition (SDG 2) will underperform. An infrastructure programme that ignores climate (SDG 13) will produce stranded assets. The SDGs are a reminder that development is a system, not a collection of sectors.

## How to Apply

1. **Map programme contributions to specific SDG targets.** Not just goals but targets — SDG 3.8 (universal health coverage), SDG 2.3 (agricultural productivity), SDG 13.1 (climate resilience).
2. **Identify cross-goal synergies.** Where does your programme contribute to multiple goals simultaneously? These synergies are the strategic leverage points.
3. **Identify cross-goal tensions.** Where might pursuing one goal compromise another? Economic growth (SDG 8) vs climate (SDG 13) is the classic tension.
4. **Use SDGs for gap analysis.** Which goals does your programme not address? Are those gaps acceptable, or do they represent missing complementary investments?

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial health department in Gauteng develops a strategy focused exclusively on SDG 3 (health) targets without considering other SDGs.
**Application:** Health outcomes depend on water and sanitation (SDG 6), nutrition (SDG 2), education (SDG 4, particularly health literacy), gender equality (SDG 5, women's health agency), and climate resilience (SDG 13, heat-related illness, vector-borne disease range shifts). A health strategy that ignores these interconnections will underperform because it treats symptoms without addressing drivers. The SDG framework prompts: what are the water and sanitation conditions in your target populations? What are their nutritional conditions? What are the gender barriers to health access? What climate-related health risks are emerging? Addressing these is not "mission creep" — it is recognising that health is produced by the system, not by health services alone.
**Result:** Broaden the health strategy to include cross-SDG investments that drive health outcomes.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat SDG alignment as a reporting exercise.
**Why:** Mapping your programme to SDG numbers after design is cosmetic. The value is in using the SDG framework during design to identify interconnections and gaps.

**Don't:** Try to address all 17 goals in a single programme.
**Why:** Integration means considering interconnections, not doing everything. Focus on the goals most relevant to your context while being alert to how they interact with others.
