# Public Health and Development

## When to Use

- When health is relevant as a development constraint, not just a social sector.
- When disease burden is a binding constraint on economic productivity.
- When health investment is being justified as development investment.

## Fails When

- **The health challenge requires clinical medical expertise.** This file addresses health as a development strategy, not medical treatment protocols.
- **The context has low disease burden and health is not a binding constraint.** In such contexts, other Sachs frameworks (infrastructure, geography) may be more relevant.

## Core Concept

Sachs's most consequential practical work has been in public health — specifically, the argument that health investment is not a social sector luxury but a core development strategy. Disease is not just a consequence of poverty; it is a cause of it. Malaria, HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and waterborne diseases impose enormous economic costs: reduced labour productivity, school absenteeism, healthcare expenditures that crowd out other investments, and premature death that destroys human capital.

The economic argument is direct. A worker debilitated by malaria produces less. A child with chronic intestinal parasites learns less. A farmer who dies of AIDS at 35 leaves an orphaned family that falls into deeper poverty. Treating health as a social sector — separate from economic strategy — misses these productivity linkages entirely.

Sachs's signature health interventions have been cost-effective, scalable tools: insecticide-treated bed nets for malaria, antiretroviral therapy for HIV/AIDS, oral rehydration therapy for diarrhoeal disease, vaccines, and clean water systems. These are not expensive technologies — they are cheap, proven, and available. The barrier to deployment is not technology but financing and distribution.

The Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria, which Sachs championed, is the institutional embodiment of this argument: a dedicated financing mechanism that has channelled billions into disease control in the world's poorest countries, with measurable results — malaria deaths in Africa fell by more than half between 2000 and 2015, largely due to bed net distribution and treatment funded by the Global Fund and other mechanisms.

For Southern Africa, where HIV prevalence remains the highest globally and malaria persists in northern areas, health is an inescapable development constraint. Any development strategy for the region that treats health as a separate sector is analytically incomplete.

## How to Apply

1. **Include disease burden in the development diagnosis.** What diseases are prevalent? What is their economic cost in lost productivity, school absenteeism, and healthcare expenditure?
2. **Calculate the return on health investment.** Bed nets cost a few dollars and prevent weeks of lost productivity. Antiretroviral therapy keeps productive adults alive and working. Quantify these returns.
3. **Lead with health when disease burden is high.** In high-burden contexts, health investment is the highest-return development investment available.
4. **Use health as an entry point for broader development.** Health interventions build trust, create delivery systems, and demonstrate that investment works — all of which support subsequent investments in other sectors.

## Examples

**Situation:** A development programme in northern KwaZulu-Natal focuses on agricultural productivity and infrastructure but does not address the district's HIV prevalence of over 25%.
**Application:** With a quarter of the adult population living with HIV, agricultural productivity gains will be eroded by labour force depletion unless treatment coverage is ensured. Infrastructure investments will be underutilised if the workforce is too sick to use them. The clinical diagnosis: HIV is a binding constraint that makes all other investments less effective. The prescription: integrate HIV treatment and prevention into the development programme — not as a health add-on but as a core economic strategy. Ensure antiretroviral access, reduce new infections, and address the orphan and vulnerable children burden that depletes household resources.
**Result:** Integrate health as a core component of the economic development programme, not a separate social sector intervention.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat health investment as charity rather than development strategy.
**Why:** The economic returns to health investment — in productivity, human capital, and reduced healthcare costs — are among the highest of any development investment. Framing health as charity undermines the case for adequate funding.

**Don't:** Assume health gains are automatic with economic growth.
**Why:** Economic growth can coexist with persistent health crises — South Africa has one of Africa's largest economies and one of the world's highest HIV prevalence rates. Active health investment is required; growth alone is insufficient.
