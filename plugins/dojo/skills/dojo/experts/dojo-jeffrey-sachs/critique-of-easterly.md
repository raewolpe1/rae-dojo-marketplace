# Critique of Easterly (and Aid Scepticism)

## When to Use

- When the debate about whether foreign aid works is active.
- When Sachs's response to William Easterly, Dambisa Moyo, and other aid sceptics is needed.
- When the user needs a steel-manned articulation of both positions.

## Fails When

- **The user needs a balanced academic literature review of aid effectiveness.** This file presents Sachs's position in the debate; for a balanced review, consult the broader literature.
- **The aid being discussed is humanitarian emergency relief rather than development aid.** The Sachs-Easterly debate is primarily about development aid.

## Core Concept

The Sachs-Easterly debate is one of the defining intellectual conflicts in development economics. William Easterly, in The White Man's Burden (2006) and subsequent work, argues that large-scale planned aid — "Plans" — fails because it ignores local knowledge, creates perverse incentives, strengthens dysfunctional governments, and substitutes technocratic ambition for bottom-up problem-solving. Easterly favours "Searchers" — local entrepreneurs, market mechanisms, and incremental discovery. Dambisa Moyo, in Dead Aid (2009), makes a complementary argument that aid to Africa has been actively harmful, fostering dependency, corruption, and institutional decay.

Sachs's response is multi-layered and, at its strongest, is compelling on several fronts.

First, the empirical response: aid sceptics often cite the aggregate correlation between aid flows and growth rates as evidence that aid doesn't work. Sachs argues this is analytically flawed — countries receive aid because they are poor and crisis-affected, so the correlation is confounded by selection. The relevant question is not whether aid recipients grow fast, but whether specific interventions produce measurable outcomes. And here the evidence is strong: bed nets reduce malaria deaths; antiretroviral therapy keeps HIV-positive adults alive and productive; vaccines prevent childhood diseases; oral rehydration therapy saves lives. These are aid-funded, proven interventions with measurable results at enormous scale.

Second, the structural response: Easterly's preference for market-driven solutions assumes functioning markets. But the poorest countries face market failures so severe that markets alone cannot solve them — infrastructure is absent, disease burden is overwhelming, human capital is depleted. The "big push" is not an alternative to markets; it is the precondition for markets to function. You cannot have market-driven agricultural development without roads, or market-driven health care in a country that cannot train or retain doctors.

Third, the moral response: the amount of aid under discussion is modest relative to donor capacity. Sachs's call for 0.7% of GNI in aid — the long-standing UN target — would represent a trivial fiscal commitment for rich countries. The sceptics' argument that this aid would be wasted ignores both the evidence of what targeted aid achieves and the moral imperative of the richest societies to address preventable suffering.

Fourth, the historical response: the Marshall Plan was a big push that worked. The Green Revolution was a planned intervention that transformed Asian agriculture. Smallpox eradication was a centrally planned, aid-funded programme that eliminated a disease. The claim that planned interventions always fail is contradicted by history.

Where Sachs is most vulnerable — and where Easterly's critique has force — is on institutional capacity. Aid that flows through dysfunctional governments may indeed strengthen dysfunction. The Millennium Villages Project, Sachs's signature practical initiative, produced mixed results and was criticised for weak evaluation methodology. And Sachs's confidence that the right technical inputs will produce development outcomes can underestimate the political economy constraints that prevent good technical solutions from being implemented.

The strongest synthesis acknowledges both positions: targeted, evidence-based aid for specific interventions (health, infrastructure, agriculture) works and should be scaled. But aid must be accompanied by institutional strengthening, accountability mechanisms, and attention to political economy — the conditions under which aid translates into sustained development.

## How to Apply

1. **Distinguish between types of aid.** Not all aid is the same. The debate is not about whether "aid" works but which types of aid, delivered how, in what conditions, produce which outcomes.
2. **Lead with evidence on specific interventions.** The strongest case for aid is not aggregate statistics but specific programmes: bed nets, antiretroviral therapy, vaccines, agricultural extension. Cite the evidence.
3. **Acknowledge institutional constraints honestly.** Aid delivered through dysfunctional institutions may not achieve its objectives. Institutional capacity is a real constraint, not a sceptic's invention.
4. **Make the moral and economic case together.** Aid is both a moral obligation and an economic investment. Countries that escape poverty traps become trading partners, not permanent aid recipients.

## Examples

**Situation:** A donor government is considering reducing development aid to Southern Africa, citing evidence from aid sceptics that aid has not produced growth in recipient countries.
**Application:** Sachs's response: the aggregate aid-growth correlation is misleading because it confounds cause and effect. The relevant evidence is programme-specific. In Southern Africa, aid-funded programmes have produced measurable results: PEPFAR and Global Fund antiretroviral programmes have kept millions of HIV-positive adults alive and productive; malaria control programmes have reduced malaria deaths significantly; vaccine programmes have reduced childhood mortality. These are not speculative — they are measured outcomes. Cutting aid would reverse these gains, not improve them. The question is not whether to provide aid but how to make it more effective — through better targeting, stronger accountability, and integration with institutional development.
**Result:** Defend the aid budget with programme-specific evidence while acknowledging the need for improved effectiveness.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Dismiss aid sceptics as heartless or ignorant.
**Why:** Easterly and Moyo raise legitimate concerns about institutional capacity, incentive distortion, and accountability. Dismissing these concerns weakens the case for aid by ignoring real problems.

**Don't:** Claim that more aid alone will solve development challenges.
**Why:** Sachs himself argues for aid targeted at specific binding constraints, accompanied by institutional development. "More aid" without strategy and accountability is not his position, even if it is sometimes caricatured as such.
