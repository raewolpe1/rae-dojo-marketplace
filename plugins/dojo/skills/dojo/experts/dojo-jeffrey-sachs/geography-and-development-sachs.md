# Geography and Development (Sachs)

## When to Use

- When geographic constraints — landlockedness, tropical disease burden, ecological fragility, distance from markets — are relevant to development analysis.
- When the debate between institutional and geographic explanations for poverty is active.
- When the user needs to understand why Sachs disagrees with Acemoglu-Robinson on the primacy of institutions.

## Fails When

- **The user needs a geographic determinism argument.** Sachs does not argue that geography determines outcomes — only that it matters alongside institutions.
- **The geographic constraints are not relevant to the specific context.** Not all poverty is geographically constrained.

## Core Concept

Sachs has consistently argued that geography matters for development — and that the economics profession has systematically underweighted it. His argument is not geographic determinism (geography determines outcomes) but geographic conditioning (geography shapes the constraints and opportunities that development policy must address).

The specific geographic factors Sachs emphasises include: tropical disease burden (malaria, dengue, soil-transmitted helminths impose enormous economic costs in tropical regions); landlockedness (countries without sea access face higher transport costs and reduced trade opportunities); ecological fragility (arid and semi-arid regions face agricultural constraints that temperate regions do not); distance from major markets (remote regions face structural disadvantages in trade and investment); and natural resource endowments (which can be blessings or curses depending on governance).

Sachs's most prominent debate on this topic is with Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, who argue that institutions — not geography — explain why nations succeed or fail. Sachs responds that the institutions-only explanation ignores why institutions differ in the first place (partly because of geographic conditions), fails to explain why tropical countries are disproportionately poor (an institutional explanation would need to show why tropical regions systematically produce worse institutions, which is itself a geographic argument), and provides no actionable guidance for policy (you can't change a country's geography, but you can invest to overcome geographic constraints).

For Southern Africa, geographic factors are significant: the region includes landlocked countries (Botswana, Zimbabwe, Zambia, Malawi), tropical disease burdens (malaria in northern areas), arid and semi-arid zones (Namibia, Botswana, parts of South Africa), and vast distances between production areas and ports.

## How to Apply

1. **Include geography in the diagnosis.** When assessing development constraints, systematically consider geographic factors alongside institutional ones. Is the area landlocked? What is the disease burden? What are the ecological constraints?
2. **Invest to overcome geographic constraints.** Geography is not destiny — it is a constraint that can be addressed through investment: malaria control reduces the tropical disease burden; roads and rail reduce the cost of landlockedness; irrigation and drought-resistant crops overcome arid conditions.
3. **Don't accept the false binary.** Geography vs institutions is a false dichotomy. Both matter. The clinical economics approach considers all binding constraints, including geographic ones.
4. **Use geography to explain, not excuse.** Geographic constraints explain why some regions face greater challenges, but they don't excuse inaction — they prescribe specific investments.

## Examples

**Situation:** A policy debate in Malawi asks why the country remains persistently poor despite democratic governance and macroeconomic stability.
**Application:** Sachs's geographic lens adds critical dimensions that institutional analysis alone misses. Malawi is landlocked (transport costs for exports are extremely high), tropical (malaria burden is enormous), ecologically fragile (soil degradation from intensive smallholder farming), and dependent on rain-fed agriculture in a region of increasing climate variability. Even with perfect institutions, these constraints would make development harder than in a coastal, temperate, well-watered economy. The prescription: invest specifically in the geographic constraints — malaria control, transport infrastructure linking Malawi to ports, irrigation to reduce rain-fed dependence, soil restoration.
**Result:** Complement institutional reforms with targeted investments that address geographic binding constraints.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use geography to argue that some countries are doomed.
**Why:** Sachs's argument is that geographic constraints can be overcome through investment, not that they are permanent barriers. Singapore is a tropical island; Botswana is landlocked. Both have developed.

**Don't:** Ignore geography because institutions are "more fundamental."
**Why:** Even if institutions are ultimately more important (which Sachs disputes), geographic constraints remain real and must be addressed through policy. Institutional reform alone won't eliminate malaria or build roads.
