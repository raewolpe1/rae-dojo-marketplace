# Shock Therapy Legacy

## When to Use

- When Sachs's role in economic reform in Poland and Russia is discussed.
- When the debate about rapid vs gradual market reform is relevant.
- When the user needs to understand how Sachs's views evolved from this experience.

## Fails When

- **The user needs a detailed history of post-Soviet transitions.** This file covers Sachs's role and lessons drawn, not comprehensive transition history.
- **The comparison with developing country contexts is stretched too far.** Transition economies had different starting conditions from developing countries.

## Core Concept

Sachs's earliest global prominence came from advising Poland (1989–1990) and Russia (1991–1993) on rapid market reform — "shock therapy." In Poland, the rapid stabilisation and liberalisation programme (the Balcerowicz Plan, which Sachs advised) is widely considered successful: hyperinflation was tamed, markets were opened, and Poland subsequently became one of Europe's growth success stories. In Russia, the outcome was catastrophic: the economy collapsed, inequality exploded, oligarchs captured state assets, and living standards plummeted for most of the population.

Sachs's account of the difference centres on context and Western support. Poland received substantial debt relief, EU membership prospects, and targeted financial support. Russia received advice but not resources — the West prescribed liberalisation while failing to provide the financial support needed to manage the transition. Sachs argues that he warned the Bush and Clinton administrations that Russia needed a Marshall Plan-scale assistance package and was ignored. The failure was not shock therapy per se but shock therapy without adequate financial support.

Critics argue differently: that rapid liberalisation in the absence of institutional foundations — functioning legal systems, property rights enforcement, regulatory capacity — predictably produced predation and oligarchy. Stiglitz, among others, argues that the pace of reform was the problem, not just the level of support.

The shock therapy experience shaped Sachs's subsequent thinking in important ways. It reinforced his belief that external financial support is essential for economic transformation. It led to his emphasis on clinical economics — the lesson that the same prescription produces different outcomes in different contexts. And it gave him first-hand experience of the damage done by ideological prescriptions (liberalise everything, fast) applied without adequate diagnosis.

## How to Apply

1. **Use the shock therapy experience as a cautionary tale about context.** The same reform package produced success in Poland and disaster in Russia, because the contexts — and the level of external support — differed fundamentally.
2. **Draw the clinical economics lesson.** Sachs's evolution from shock therapy adviser to clinical economics advocate reflects the central learning: prescriptions must match diagnosis.
3. **Acknowledge the controversy honestly.** Sachs's role in Russia is his most contested legacy. Present both his account and the critiques fairly.
4. **Connect to current debates.** When rapid reform is proposed for any economy, the shock therapy experience is relevant: what is the institutional capacity? What external support is available? What happens to the most vulnerable during transition?

## Examples

**Situation:** A consultant proposes rapid liberalisation of a protected agricultural sector in a Southern African country, arguing that market exposure will improve efficiency.
**Application:** The shock therapy lesson is directly relevant. Rapid liberalisation in the absence of institutional readiness — functioning markets, access to credit, transport infrastructure, information systems — may destroy existing livelihoods without creating viable alternatives. The Poland lesson: liberalisation with institutional support and external resources can succeed. The Russia lesson: liberalisation without these produces predation and collapse. The clinical economics question: what is the diagnosis? Are the markets functioning? Do farmers have the information, credit, and infrastructure to compete? If not, liberalisation is premature.
**Result:** Sequence reform based on diagnosis: build institutional foundations first, then liberalise — or liberalise gradually while building foundations.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use the Russia experience to argue that market reform is always wrong.
**Why:** Poland shows that rapid reform can work in the right conditions. The lesson is about matching prescription to context, not about avoiding reform.

**Don't:** Let Sachs off the hook entirely.
**Why:** Even if Sachs's account of the Russia failure is correct (insufficient Western support), the experience raises questions about the wisdom of advising rapid reform when the support conditions are uncertain. A clinical economist might have diagnosed the absence of support as a contraindication.
