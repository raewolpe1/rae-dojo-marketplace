# Millennium Villages Project

## When to Use

- When evaluating integrated, multi-sector community development approaches.
- When the MVP is cited as evidence for or against scaled development interventions.
- When lessons from the MVP are relevant to programme design.

## Fails When

- **The user needs a definitive verdict on whether the MVP "worked."** The evidence is mixed and contested. Present the findings honestly.
- **The context is so different from rural sub-Saharan Africa that MVP lessons don't transfer.** The MVP was designed for specific poverty trap conditions.

## Core Concept

The Millennium Villages Project (2005–2015) was Sachs's attempt to demonstrate the big push at community scale. Launched in ten sites across sub-Saharan Africa, the MVP invested approximately $120 per person per year in integrated interventions across health, education, agriculture, infrastructure, and business development — simultaneously, in a coordinated package.

The theory was direct: if poverty traps are real and the big push is the solution, then a well-designed, community-scale demonstration should show measurable improvement across all dimensions of poverty within five years, with self-sustaining dynamics taking over thereafter.

Results were mixed. The MVP sites showed significant improvements in health indicators (child mortality, malaria incidence, maternal health), agricultural productivity (through improved seeds, fertiliser, and extension), and infrastructure (clinics, schools, water points). However, the project faced several serious critiques. The evaluation design lacked rigorous comparison groups in early phases, making attribution difficult. Economic sustainability was questionable — gains depended on continued external funding. Some critics argued that comparable gains occurred in non-MVP areas due to broader trends (aid scale-up, commodity price increases, national policy changes). The cost per person was higher than initially projected. And the project's ambitious timeline — five years to self-sustaining development — proved unrealistic.

Sachs's response to these critiques is that the MVP demonstrated the potential of integrated investment, that the health and agricultural gains were real and significant, that the comparison-group criticism was addressed in later evaluation phases, and that the project's imperfections reflect implementation challenges, not flaws in the underlying theory. The lesson, for Sachs, is not that the big push doesn't work but that it needs better implementation, longer timescales, and more rigorous evaluation design.

For Southern African development practice, the MVP offers both positive lessons (integrated design, health as an entry point, community engagement) and cautionary ones (sustainability planning, evaluation design, realistic timescales).

## How to Apply

1. **Use the MVP as a design reference, not a template.** The principle of integrated, multi-sector investment is sound. The specific MVP package must be adapted to local conditions.
2. **Build evaluation into design from day one.** The MVP's evaluation weaknesses undermined its credibility. Any big push programme must have rigorous comparison groups and independent evaluation.
3. **Plan for sustainability.** The MVP's dependency on external funding was a design weakness. Build domestic financing, institutional capacity, and exit strategies from the start.
4. **Be realistic about timescales.** Five years is too short for self-sustaining transformation. Plan for ten to fifteen years with phased withdrawal of external support.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African district municipality proposes an "integrated development push" modelled on the MVP, investing across health, agriculture, infrastructure, and education simultaneously.
**Application:** The instinct is right — integrated investment is more effective than sequential, sectoral projects. But learn from the MVP's weaknesses: include a rigorous evaluation design with comparison districts from the start; plan domestic financing to replace external funding over a ten-year horizon; set realistic targets (measurable improvement, not poverty elimination, in five years); and build implementation capacity as a programme component, not an assumption.
**Result:** An integrated district development programme that incorporates MVP strengths (multi-sector coordination, health as an entry point, community engagement) while addressing MVP weaknesses (evaluation design, sustainability, realistic timescales).

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Dismiss the MVP entirely because of its evaluation weaknesses.
**Why:** The health and agricultural gains in MVP sites were real and significant. The evaluation problems mean we can't attribute all gains to the MVP, but they don't mean the gains didn't happen.

**Don't:** Cite the MVP as proof that the big push works at national scale.
**Why:** The MVP was a community-scale demonstration. Scaling from community to district to national level involves governance, coordination, and political economy challenges that the MVP did not address.
