# Debt Relief

## When to Use

- When sovereign debt burden is constraining a country's ability to invest in development.
- When the case for debt relief, cancellation, or restructuring needs to be made.
- When historical debt campaigns (HIPC, Jubilee 2000) are relevant.

## Fails When

- **The debt situation requires technical financial restructuring analysis.** This file covers the development case for debt relief, not debt restructuring mechanics.
- **The country's debt burden is sustainable and debt is not a binding constraint.** Not all debt is a development constraint.

## Core Concept

Sachs has been one of the most prominent advocates for debt relief as a development strategy, arguing since the late 1980s that unsustainable sovereign debt diverts resources from essential investments in health, education, and infrastructure. A government spending a quarter of its revenue on debt service cannot fund the investments needed to break poverty traps.

The argument has several dimensions. First, much of the debt accumulated by the poorest countries was contracted by unaccountable or illegitimate regimes, often with the active encouragement of Cold War-era lenders — the moral case for relief is strong. Second, debt service by the poorest countries transfers resources from the world's poorest people to the world's richest institutions — a perverse redistribution. Third, the economic case: debt overhang depresses investment because creditors capture any growth gains, removing the incentive to invest. Relief removes this disincentive.

Sachs was instrumental in building the intellectual and political case for the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative and the Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (MDRI), which together cancelled tens of billions of dollars in debt for the world's poorest countries. The Jubilee 2000 campaign, which Sachs supported, mobilised public pressure for debt cancellation.

The results were significant. Countries that received HIPC relief — including several in Southern Africa — were able to increase health and education spending substantially. Zambia, Mozambique, and Tanzania all expanded social spending after debt relief.

The debt issue has resurfaced as many developing countries, including in Africa, have accumulated new debt — to China, to Eurobond holders, and to multilateral institutions. Sachs argues that a new round of debt relief may be necessary, particularly as climate-related shocks increase fiscal pressure on vulnerable countries.

## How to Apply

1. **Assess debt as a development constraint.** What share of government revenue goes to debt service? Is it crowding out development investment?
2. **Make the moral and economic case together.** Debt relief is not just compassion — it is economically rational when debt overhang depresses investment and growth.
3. **Link relief to development investment.** Debt relief should be conditional on the freed resources being invested in development priorities — health, education, infrastructure.
4. **Address new debt accumulation.** Debt relief without addressing the causes of over-borrowing — commodity dependency, fiscal mismanagement, predatory lending — produces repeat crises.

## Examples

**Situation:** Zambia's debt service consumes a growing share of government revenue, reducing available funding for health and education in a country where poverty rates remain above 50%.
**Application:** This is the classic debt-development bind. Every kwacha spent on debt service is a kwacha not invested in the health, education, and infrastructure that could reduce poverty. Sachs's argument: Zambia needs debt restructuring that reduces the service burden to a level compatible with development investment. The freed resources should be channelled to specific development investments with measurable outcomes — a "debt-for-development" swap. Without relief, Zambia is trapped: too poor to invest, and unable to grow out of poverty because it can't invest.
**Result:** Advocate for debt restructuring linked to a credible development investment plan.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Present debt relief as a gift to irresponsible governments.
**Why:** Much of the debt was contracted by unaccountable regimes, encouraged by reckless lenders, and accumulated through terms that disadvantaged borrowers. Relief is a correction of a systemic injustice, not charity.

**Don't:** Support unconditional debt relief.
**Why:** Relief without commitment to development investment and fiscal reform risks repeating the cycle. Conditions should be development-focused (spend on health and education), not austerity-focused (cut spending).
