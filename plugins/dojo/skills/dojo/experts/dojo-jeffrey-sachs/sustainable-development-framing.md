# Sustainable Development Framing

## When to Use

- When development planning needs to integrate economic growth, social inclusion, and environmental sustainability.
- When sustainability is relegated to an environmental chapter rather than embedded throughout.
- When the conceptual framework for why these dimensions are inseparable needs articulation.

## Fails When

- **The user needs specific sustainability metrics or reporting frameworks.** This file covers the conceptual framing, not measurement tools.
- **The context is purely environmental with no development dimension.** Sachs's framework is specifically about the integration of development and sustainability.

## Core Concept

Sachs defines sustainable development as the integration of three systems: economic prosperity, social inclusion, and environmental sustainability. This is not a hierarchy — it is a system in which each dimension depends on the others. Economic prosperity without environmental sustainability depletes the resource base. Environmental sustainability without social inclusion ignores the needs of the poorest. Social inclusion without economic prosperity lacks the resources to deliver.

This three-pillar framing, which Sachs has articulated in The Age of Sustainable Development (2015) and subsequent work, is the conceptual foundation of the SDG framework. The SDGs operationalise sustainable development by setting goals across all three dimensions and — critically — requiring their integration.

Sachs argues that the development models of the twentieth century failed because they fragmented these dimensions. The growth-first model (focus on GDP, deal with environment and equity later) produced environmental degradation and persistent inequality. The environment-first model (protect nature, restrict development) ignored the needs of the world's poorest. The equity-first model (redistribute before growing) lacked resources. Sustainable development insists that all three must be pursued simultaneously.

The practical implication for development practitioners is that every programme should be assessed across all three dimensions. Does it contribute to economic productivity? Does it include the poorest and most marginalised? Does it operate within environmental limits? If any dimension is missing, the programme is unsustainable in the original sense of the word — it cannot be maintained over time.

## How to Apply

1. **Assess programmes across all three dimensions.** Economic return, social inclusion, environmental impact. If any is missing, the programme is incomplete.
2. **Look for synergies.** Climate-smart agriculture simultaneously improves productivity (economic), feeds the poorest (social), and reduces environmental degradation. These triple-win investments are strategic priorities.
3. **Identify trade-offs honestly.** Not all trade-offs can be avoided. Mining creates economic value but environmental damage. The sustainable development framework doesn't eliminate trade-offs; it requires that they be made explicit and managed.
4. **Embed sustainability in design, not in reporting.** If sustainability considerations enter only at the evaluation or reporting stage, they're cosmetic.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African municipality's Integrated Development Plan treats economic development, social services, and environmental management as separate chapters with separate budgets and separate implementation plans.
**Application:** The sustainable development framing reveals the fragmentation. Economic development that ignores water scarcity (environmental) will fail when the water runs out. Social services that ignore economic productivity will face fiscal unsustainability. Environmental management that ignores poverty will face non-compliance from communities that depend on natural resources for survival. The IDP should be restructured around integrated programmes that address all three dimensions simultaneously — for example, a water programme that ensures supply (environmental), creates economic opportunity (productive use), and serves the poorest households (social inclusion).
**Result:** Restructure the IDP around integrated programmes rather than sectoral silos.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat "sustainable" as a synonym for "environmental."
**Why:** Sustainable development explicitly integrates economic and social dimensions. Using "sustainable" to mean only "green" loses two-thirds of the concept.

**Don't:** Claim that all three dimensions can always be optimised simultaneously.
**Why:** Trade-offs are real. The sustainable development framework requires that trade-offs be explicit and managed, not that they be denied.
