# Africa Development Agenda

## When to Use

- When Africa-specific development constraints and strategies are under discussion.
- When Sachs's diagnosis of why Africa has lagged — and what can be done — is needed.
- When common narratives about African underdevelopment (corruption, bad governance) need to be complicated with structural analysis.

## Fails When

- **The context requires country-specific political economy analysis.** Sachs's Africa agenda is continental in scope; country-level detail requires additional sources.
- **The user needs a critique of Sachs's Africa engagement.** For critiques, see critique-of-easterly.md and millennium-villages-project.md.

## Core Concept

Sachs has devoted more sustained intellectual and practical effort to African development than perhaps any other economist of his generation. His central argument is that Africa's persistent poverty is not primarily a governance failure — it is a structural trap produced by the interaction of geography, disease burden, colonial legacy, and inadequate external investment.

The diagnosis has several components. First, geography: much of Sub-Saharan Africa is tropical (high disease burden), landlocked (high transport costs), and ecologically fragile (variable rainfall, poor soils in many areas). These geographic constraints impose costs that temperate, coastal economies do not face. Second, disease: malaria, HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and other diseases impose enormous economic costs — reduced productivity, premature death, overwhelmed health systems. Third, colonial legacy: colonial boundaries created artificial states, extractive institutions, and economies oriented toward commodity export rather than internal development. Fourth, the post-independence debt trap: many African countries accumulated unsustainable debt, often contracted by unaccountable regimes and encouraged by Cold War-era lenders.

Sachs's prescription follows from the diagnosis: a "big push" of targeted investment to overcome structural constraints. This means health investment (bed nets, antiretroviral therapy, clean water), infrastructure investment (roads, electricity, broadband), agricultural investment (improved seeds, fertiliser, irrigation), and education investment — financed substantially by external aid because the countries are too poor to finance the push themselves. The Millennium Villages Project was an attempt to demonstrate this approach at village scale.

For Southern Africa specifically, Sachs's framework highlights the HIV/AIDS burden as the single largest development constraint, the landlocked status of several countries (Botswana, Zimbabwe, Zambia, Malawi, Lesotho, Eswatini), the colonial legacy of extractive institutions (particularly in South Africa, Namibia, and Zimbabwe), and the need for regional infrastructure to reduce the costs of landlockedness.

## How to Apply

1. **Start with structural diagnosis, not governance critique.** Before attributing poverty to governance failures, assess geographic constraints, disease burden, infrastructure gaps, and historical legacies.
2. **Quantify the structural constraints.** What does landlockedness cost in transport premiums? What does malaria cost in lost productivity? What does HIV cost in human capital? These are measurable.
3. **Design investment packages that address multiple constraints simultaneously.** Health, infrastructure, agriculture, and education are complementary — investing in one without the others limits returns.
4. **Make the case for external financing.** Countries spending a quarter of revenue on debt service and a fifth on health crises cannot self-finance the investment needed to escape poverty traps.

## Examples

**Situation:** A policy debate in Zambia asks why the country remains poor despite copper wealth, democratic governance, and macroeconomic stability.
**Application:** Sachs's structural diagnosis adds dimensions that governance-focused explanations miss. Zambia is landlocked (transport costs for non-copper exports are prohibitive), has a significant malaria burden (particularly in northern and eastern provinces), faces HIV prevalence of around 11% (depleting human capital), and depends on a single commodity (copper) whose price volatility transmits external shocks. Even with good governance, these structural constraints make development harder. The prescription: invest in malaria control (highest-return health investment), diversify the economy through agricultural modernisation (Zambia has significant agricultural potential), invest in regional transport infrastructure (reduce the cost of landlockedness), and restructure debt to free fiscal resources for development investment.
**Result:** Complement governance reforms with a targeted investment programme addressing Zambia's specific structural constraints.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Attribute African poverty primarily to corruption and bad governance.
**Why:** This narrative ignores structural constraints that would make development difficult even with perfect governance. It also blames the victims — many governance failures are themselves consequences of structural constraints (resource dependence, colonial institutional legacies).

**Don't:** Treat Africa as a single country.
**Why:** The continent contains 54 countries with vastly different conditions. Botswana, Rwanda, and Ethiopia have different development trajectories from DRC, South Sudan, and Somalia. Sachs's framework is diagnostic — it requires country-specific application.
