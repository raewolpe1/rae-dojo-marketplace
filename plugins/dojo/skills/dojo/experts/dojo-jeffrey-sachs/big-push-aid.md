# Big Push Aid

## When to Use

- When designing or evaluating a large-scale, multi-sector development intervention.
- When the argument for scaled investment over incremental approaches needs articulating.
- When responding to the claim that "aid doesn't work."

## Fails When

- **Governance is so poor that scaled investment will be captured.** The big push assumes a minimum level of implementation capacity. Where this is absent, institutional strengthening must come first — or the push must build implementation capacity as a component.
- **The binding constraint is political, not financial.** If the problem is active predation by elites, more money exacerbates the problem.

## Core Concept

The big push theory — rooted in the work of Rosenstein-Rodan, Nurkse, and Murphy-Shleifer-Vishny, and updated by Sachs — argues that escaping a poverty trap requires a large, coordinated investment across multiple sectors simultaneously, above a critical threshold. Below the threshold, investment is dissipated by the remaining constraints; above it, self-sustaining growth dynamics take over.

The logic is direct. If a community's poverty is driven by interlocking constraints — disease, infrastructure deficit, low agricultural productivity, inadequate education — then addressing any one constraint while leaving the others in place produces limited results. Build a clinic, but if there's no road to reach it and no food to feed recovering patients, health gains are marginal. Build a road, but if there's nothing to transport because farms produce at subsistence, the road is underused. The big push addresses all binding constraints simultaneously, creating the conditions for each investment to reinforce the others.

Sachs argues that the international community has the resources for a big push. The 0.7% GNP commitment — repeatedly pledged since 1970 and never met by most donor countries — would fund the necessary investments. The failure is not one of affordability but of political will.

The big push is not a blank cheque. It requires clinical diagnosis of the specific constraints, careful design of the investment package, measurable targets, and rigorous monitoring. The Millennium Villages Project was Sachs's attempt to demonstrate the big push at community scale — with mixed but instructive results.

Critics argue that big pushes are wasteful, that they overwhelm absorptive capacity, and that they ignore the institutional constraints that prevent effective use of resources. Sachs responds that these critiques confuse badly designed aid with the concept of scaled investment, and that the alternative — doing nothing or doing too little — is worse.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose the specific constraints that require simultaneous investment.** The big push is not "spend more on everything" but "invest simultaneously in the specific sectors whose constraints reinforce each other."
2. **Calculate the investment threshold.** Below what level of investment will the trap not be broken?
3. **Design for complementarity.** Each sector investment should reinforce the others — health improvements increase agricultural productivity, infrastructure enables market access, education builds human capital for all sectors.
4. **Build in monitoring from day one.** The big push requires rapid feedback on what's working and what's not.

## Examples

**Situation:** A district in the Eastern Cape has seen multiple donor-funded projects over 15 years — water points, school renovations, clinic upgrades — with minimal impact on poverty rates.
**Application:** The pattern of sequential, small-scale projects is the opposite of a big push. Each project addressed one sector in isolation, at insufficient scale, without coordination with other sectors. The big push alternative: a five-year integrated investment in the district covering health (clinics staffed and supplied, community health workers, clean water), education (schools functional with materials and qualified teachers), infrastructure (roads connecting the district to markets, electricity), and agriculture (extension services, improved inputs, irrigation where feasible). The investment must be large enough to break the trap — not another incremental project but a district-level transformation.
**Result:** Replace the portfolio of disconnected projects with a single, coordinated, multi-sector district development programme at sufficient scale.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Equate the big push with throwing money at problems.
**Why:** The big push is a specific, diagnosable, designable intervention — not an argument for more spending without design. Sachs is explicit that clinical diagnosis, targeted design, and monitoring are essential.

**Don't:** Assume the big push works regardless of context.
**Why:** The big push requires minimum governance capacity, community engagement, and implementation systems. In contexts where these are absent, building them is part of the push.
