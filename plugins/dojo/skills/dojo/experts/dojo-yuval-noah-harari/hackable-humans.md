# Hackable Humans — AI Knowing Us Better Than We Know Ourselves

## When to Use
- When assessing the implications of data collection, biometric surveillance, or behavioural analytics for individual autonomy and collective self-governance.
- When explaining why "privacy" is not just about hiding embarrassing information but about maintaining the conditions for autonomous decision-making.
- When diagnosing why micro-targeted advertising, algorithmic feeds, and personalised nudging represent a qualitatively new form of influence.

## Core Concept

The liberal tradition — the intellectual foundation of democratic societies, human rights, market economies, and individual freedoms — rests on a specific assumption: that the individual is the best judge of their own interests. Voters know whom to elect. Consumers know what to buy. Believers know what to worship. This assumption was never perfectly true, but for most of history it was approximately true enough to serve as a workable foundation for political and economic organisation. No external system could reliably outperform individual self-knowledge at scale.

Harari argues that this assumption is being undermined by the convergence of three forces: biological science (which increasingly understands the biochemical mechanisms of human emotion, desire, and decision-making), computing power (which can process vast quantities of data about individual behaviour), and data (which is being collected on an unprecedented scale through smartphones, wearables, social media, and digital transactions). The convergence of these three forces makes it possible, in principle, to "hack" a human being — to acquire enough knowledge about a person's biological and psychological makeup to predict and manipulate their choices more effectively than the person can manage on their own.

"Hacking a human" does not mean breaking into their body or brain. It means building a model of their decision-making that is more accurate than their own self-model. If an algorithm that has analysed your search history, purchasing patterns, biometric data, and social media activity can predict what you will want, feel, and choose more accurately than you can — then in what sense are you still making free choices? The algorithm does not need to control you directly. It needs only to know you well enough to present the right stimulus at the right moment. This is the logic of micro-targeted advertising, personalised news feeds, and algorithmic recommendation systems — all of which work by modelling individual preferences more accurately than the individual can articulate them.

The political implications are profound. Democracy assumes that citizens can form independent preferences and express them through votes. If citizens' preferences can be predicted and manipulated by whoever controls the data and the algorithms, then the democratic process becomes a contest not between citizens' authentic preferences but between the manipulative capacities of competing algorithmic systems. Elections become a question of who has the better behavioural model, not who has the better policies.

Harari is careful to note that the hackability thesis is a trajectory, not a completed fact. Algorithms cannot yet perfectly predict individual behaviour. But the trajectory is clear, and the gap between algorithmic prediction and human self-knowledge is narrowing. The question is not whether humans will become fully hackable — that may never happen. The question is whether the degree of hackability already achieved, and the degree that will be achieved in the coming decades, is sufficient to undermine the functioning assumptions of liberal democratic societies.

## How to Apply
1. **Apply the three-ingredient test.** For any data system or technology under evaluation, ask: does it combine biological or psychological knowledge, computing power, and personal data in ways that enable prediction or manipulation of individual behaviour? If all three ingredients are present, the hackability risk is real.
2. **Assess the asymmetry.** Who understands whom? If a system understands its users better than its users understand it — or themselves — then the power relationship is fundamentally asymmetric, regardless of what the terms of service say.
3. **Distinguish prediction from manipulation.** Predicting what someone will choose is not the same as manipulating their choice. But the step from prediction to manipulation is short: once you can predict a preference, you can reinforce it, redirect it, or suppress it by controlling the information environment.
4. **Ask who benefits from hackability.** Hackability is not a natural disaster — it is produced by specific actors who profit from it. Data brokers, advertising platforms, political campaigns, and state surveillance systems all benefit from the ability to model and influence individual behaviour. Following the incentives reveals the governance challenge.

## Examples
**Situation:** A development agency is deploying a mobile-phone-based social protection programme that collects beneficiary data — location, spending patterns, communication networks, health-seeking behaviour — to improve targeting and monitor outcomes.

**Application:** Through the hackable-humans lens, this programme is not merely collecting data to improve service delivery — it is assembling the ingredients for hackability. The data (individual-level behavioural, financial, health, and social network information), combined with computing power (cloud processing, machine learning models), and increasingly with biological knowledge (health data correlated with behaviour), creates the capacity to model beneficiary behaviour with high precision. The immediate intent is benign — better targeting, better outcomes. But the data infrastructure persists beyond the programme. A future government, a commercial data buyer, or a political campaign could use the same data to predict, profile, and manipulate the same populations. In a context where democratic institutions are fragile and data protection regulation is weak, the programme is creating a hackability infrastructure that outlives its development purpose. The governance question is: what constraints ensure that the data can be used for service delivery but not for surveillance, manipulation, or political targeting? If the answer is "trust" or "good intentions," the protection is insufficient — because the hackability risk is structural, not intentional.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Claim that humans are already fully hackable — this overstates the current state of the technology and discredits the argument.
**Why:** Algorithmic prediction of individual behaviour is improving but remains imperfect. The hackability thesis is about a trajectory and a threshold, not a completed fact. Precision matters: the argument is strongest when it identifies the direction and speed of the trend, not when it asserts that we have already arrived.

**Don't:** Use "hackable humans" to argue against all data collection.
**Why:** Data collection enables genuine public goods — disease surveillance, economic monitoring, evidence-based policy. The issue is not data collection per se but the convergence of data, computing power, and biological knowledge in the absence of governance frameworks that protect individuals and populations. The hackability framework is a tool for governance design, not for technophobic refusal.
