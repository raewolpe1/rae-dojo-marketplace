# Cooperation Through Fiction — Shared Myths Enabling Large-Scale Cooperation

## When to Use
- When you need to reveal the fictional foundation of an institution that people treat as natural — money, the nation-state, human rights, the corporation, the constitution.
- When diagnosing why an institution or social order is losing coherence — the underlying shared fiction may be weakening or being contested.
- When explaining to development professionals why narratives and legitimacy matter as much as material resources.

## Core Concept

The central argument of Sapiens begins with a puzzle: how did Homo sapiens — a physically unremarkable primate — come to dominate the planet? Not through individual intelligence, physical strength, or tool use. Neanderthals had larger brains. Many species use tools. The answer lies in a cognitive capacity that emerged during the Cognitive Revolution approximately 70,000 years ago: the ability to create and believe in things that exist nowhere in the physical world.

A chimpanzee troop cooperates because every member personally knows every other member. This limits the group to roughly 50-150 individuals — the number that direct social relationships can sustain. Homo sapiens broke through this ceiling by inventing shared fictions — stories about gods, spirits, ancestors, nations, money, laws, and human rights that exist only because large numbers of people believe in them. These fictions are not lies. A lie is something one person fabricates to deceive another. A shared fiction is an intersubjective reality — something that exists because a community collectively agrees it exists. The US dollar has value not because of its physical properties but because 330 million Americans and billions of others around the world share the fiction that it has value. If they stopped believing tomorrow, the dollar would be worthless paper.

This capacity for shared fiction is what enables large-scale flexible cooperation among strangers. Two Catholics who have never met can go on a Crusade together because they share the fiction of Christendom. Two traders who speak different languages can exchange goods because they share the fiction of money. Two strangers can resolve a dispute because they share the fiction of law. These fictions are the invisible infrastructure of civilisation — as real in their consequences as any bridge or road, but dependent entirely on continued collective belief.

The implication is radical: everything we take for granted about social order — nations, constitutions, corporations, human rights, economic systems — is a shared fiction that was invented at a specific historical moment, could have been invented differently, and could be replaced. This is not nihilism. It is historical realism. Understanding that institutions are fictions does not make them less powerful or less valuable. It makes them visible as human creations that require maintenance, adaptation, and sometimes replacement.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the fiction.** For any institution, policy, or organisation under discussion, ask: what shared fiction holds this together? What do participants need to believe for this to function? Name it explicitly.
2. **Test the fiction's health.** Is the shared fiction stable, strengthening, weakening, or being actively contested? When people stop believing in a fiction — when they lose faith in the currency, the constitution, the legitimacy of the government — the institution collapses, often suddenly.
3. **Trace the fiction's history.** When was this fiction invented? Under what circumstances? What fiction did it replace? Understanding the contingency of the fiction reveals that alternatives were and are possible.
4. **Ask whether AI can generate fictions.** If large-scale cooperation depends on shared stories, and AI can now generate stories at scale, what happens to the human monopoly on fiction-making? This is one of the most consequential questions of the AI age.

## Examples
**Situation:** A post-conflict society in the Great Lakes region is struggling to rebuild national cohesion. Multiple ethnic groups that fought each other lack a shared narrative for coexistence. International donors are funding reconciliation programmes focused on truth commissions and economic reconstruction.

**Application:** Through a Harari lens, the core challenge is not material but fictional: the pre-conflict shared fiction of national identity has been shattered, and no new fiction has taken its place. Truth commissions address the historical record but do not by themselves create a shared story about who "we" are now. Economic reconstruction provides material incentives for cooperation but does not explain why strangers from different groups should trust each other. The missing ingredient is a new shared fiction — a story of collective identity that is credible, inclusive, and strong enough to sustain cooperation among people who remember killing each other. This does not mean fabricating a false history. It means deliberately constructing a narrative about the future — who we are becoming together — that enough people can believe in to coordinate behaviour. Rwanda's post-genocide narrative of "Ndi Umunyarwanda" (I am Rwandan) is one attempt at this — contested and imperfect, but explicitly designed to provide a shared fiction that transcends ethnic division. Development programmes that ignore the fictional infrastructure and focus only on material reconstruction will find that cooperation remains fragile, because material incentives alone cannot sustain trust among strangers.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use "it's all fiction" to dismiss the reality of institutions or to imply that because something is a shared fiction it doesn't matter.
**Why:** Shared fictions are among the most powerful forces in human history. Money is a fiction, but it feeds billions. Human rights are a fiction, but they constrain tyrants. The point is not that fictions are unreal but that they are human creations — and therefore can be analysed, maintained, reformed, or replaced. Dismissiveness undercuts the thesis.

**Don't:** Attribute the cooperation-through-fiction thesis exclusively to Harari without acknowledging the broader intellectual tradition.
**Why:** Anthropologists (Anderson's "imagined communities"), sociologists (Durkheim's collective representations, Berger and Luckmann's social construction of reality), and philosophers (Searle's institutional facts) have explored similar terrain. Harari's contribution is the synthesis, the accessibility, and the extension to AI — not the originality of the underlying insight. Acknowledging this strengthens credibility and avoids the contested-historian critique.
