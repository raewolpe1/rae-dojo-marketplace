# National Autonomous Technology Capacity

> **SOURCED (July 2026).** The founding conclusion and the Ajaokuta account are now **verified verbatim** from the Guardian (Nigeria) interview, 29 March 2026. See `evidence-base-the-2026-interview.md`.

## When to Use
- **Load first on any Oyelaran-Oyeyinka question.** It is the founding conclusion.
- When investment or FDI is proposed as the route to capability.
- When a facility, plant or technology acquisition is being planned.

## Fails When
- **The capability demonstrably exists.** Then the question is coordination or finance.

## Core Concept

**The founding conclusion, verbatim:**

> *"First, witnessing the collapse of early industrial experiments in Nigeria in the 1980s showed me that **national autonomous technology capacity—not raw investment dollars—was the missing piece.**"*

### ⚠️ AJAOKUTA — the whole framework in one image

> *"**At the Ajaokuta Steel plant where I started my career, the moment the foreign contractors withdrew, construction ceased and ultimately that was the end of the project.**"*

**He started his career at Ajaokuta.** The capital was voted. The plant was specified. The foreign contractors came. **And when they withdrew, construction ceased** — not production, *construction*. **The plant was never finished.**

That is the three-question test, learned on a site rather than in a seminar. *When it breaks, who fixes it?* At Ajaokuta: **nobody, and it never even got built.** There was no national capacity to continue — not to run the thing, not to complete it. **This is what "autonomous" means, and it is why the word is in the phrase.**

*Caveat: Ajaokuta is Nigeria's most politically loaded industrial project and the literature on it is vast and contested. He is describing what he witnessed and the conviction it produced — not offering a full causal account of the project. Use it as the origin of the framework, which is how he presents it.*

**Unpack the three words, because each is load-bearing.**

**National.** Held here, in people and in the institutions that make and hold them — training systems, engineering associations, standards bodies, universities that talk to firms, suppliers who can fabricate a part. Not held by the vendor and visited annually.

**Autonomous.** A capacity that depends on whoever sold you the technology is not a capacity. The test is whether you can continue without them.

**Capacity, not capital.** The capital was there in Nigeria. The plant was built. It is in the ground now, in a number of places. **The capital was never the constraint.**

**The three-question test for any technology or investment proposal:**

1. **When it breaks, who fixes it?**
2. **When the process needs adapting to a local input, who adapts it?**
3. **In ten years, when the technology has moved, who moves with it?**

**If the answer to all three is the vendor, you have bought a plant, not a capability.**

**Why this is a *national* argument and not a firm one.** A single firm can buy a maintenance contract. But if no firm in the country can fabricate the part, train the technician, or adapt the process, then every firm is buying the same contract, the capability never accumulates anywhere, and the country hosts technology without ever holding any. **That is the difference between industrialising and being a site of production.**

**On FDI.** FDI may supply plant. Whether it supplies **capacity** depends on what it does here — whether it trains, sources locally, transfers responsibility, and leaves people behind who know things. An assembly operation teaches nothing. This is the same question Sutton reaches from the Enterprise Map data (read the FDI register as a capability inventory) and Hausmann reaches from theory (knowhow moves when people move) — **three routes to one conclusion: ask what the investment leaves behind in brains.**

**The engineer's authority matters here.** He trained as a chemical engineer — First Class at Ife, a master's at Toronto — before the DPhil. He knows what a plant is and what it requires to run. When he says the plant was not the problem, it is a technical judgement as well as an economic one.

## The AI restatement — his conclusion, forty years on

**UNIDO Policy Brief No. 30 (March 2026)** — Labrunie, Fan & Leal-Ayala, **not his** — reports that **developing countries (except China) "are primarily users of AI models"**, and lists among the **firm-level barriers** to AI adoption in developing countries:

- **"R&D activities performed abroad"**
- **"Technology adoption decisions made by the headquarters, usually located abroad"**
- **"Dependence on foreign technology providers"**

**That is the failure of *autonomous* capacity, itemised.** A firm whose adoption decisions are made in another country holds no capacity — it hosts a deployment. The three-question test applies unchanged: when the model breaks, who retrains it? When it needs adapting to local data, who adapts it? In ten years, who moves with it? **If the answer is the vendor, you have bought a subscription, not a capability.**

See `evidence-base-ai-and-industrial-catching-up.md`.

## How to Apply

1. **Run the three questions** on every technology or investment proposal.
2. **Separate plant from capability.** They arrive by different routes.
3. **Ask what accumulates nationally**, not just what the firm gets.
4. **Assess FDI by what it leaves in brains**, not by its value.
5. **Test "autonomous."** Can you continue without the vendor?

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy proposes to attract investment into a new processing facility as a route to building provincial capability in the sub-sector.

**Application:** The investment will build the facility. Run the three questions on the capability claim. **When it breaks, who fixes it?** If the answer is a technician flown in, no capability accumulates. **When the process needs adapting to a local input — a different fruit variety, a different water chemistry — who adapts it?** If the answer is the licensor, the province hosts a process it does not understand. **In ten years, who moves with the technology?** If nobody, the facility ages into obsolescence and the story ends as it did in Nigeria. **The redesign is to make capability transfer an explicit term of the investment** — local sourcing of maintenance, training obligations, technical staff who stay — and to build the institutions that receive it. Otherwise the province is buying a plant and calling it industrialisation.

**Result:** An investment assessed on what it leaves behind rather than on what it costs.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Answer a capability question with an investment figure.
**Why:** That is precisely the answer that failed in Nigeria in the 1980s, and the plant is still there.

**Don't:** Assume FDI supplies capacity.
**Why:** It may supply plant. Assembly teaches nothing. Ask what it leaves in brains.

**Don't:** Accept a capability that depends on the vendor.
**Why:** Autonomous is one of the three words. A dependent capacity is not one.
