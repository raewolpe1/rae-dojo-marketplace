# Evidence Base — AI and Industrial Catching-Up

> **ATTRIBUTION — READ FIRST. Oyelaran-Oyeyinka did NOT write this and is not a co-author.**
>
> **Source:** **Labrunie, M., Fan, Z. & Leal-Ayala, D. (March 2026), "AI and the future of industry: Challenges and opportunities for developing countries", UNIDO Policy Brief Series: Insights on Industrial Development, Issue No. 30.** The authors are at **Cambridge Industrial Innovation Policy, IfM Engage, University of Cambridge**. Produced by UNIDO's **Division of Industrial Policy Research and Statistics (IPS)** as **preparatory work for the UNIDO Industrial Development Report 2026**. UNIDO's own disclaimer: the views are the authors' and "do not necessarily reflect the views of UNIDO."
>
> **Verified by machine search of the full document: the strings "Oyeyinka", "Oyelaran" and "Banji" appear zero times.** He is not an author, not acknowledged, and not cited. **Never attribute any of it to him.**
>
> **Why it is in his dojo:** because it **tests his AI caution** — now **VERIFIED VERBATIM** (Guardian (Nigeria), 29 March 2026): *"A future shaped by Artificial Intelligence (AI) is going to make industrial catching up very difficult."* See `evidence-base-the-2026-interview.md` — and because its analytical structure is, without naming him, **his framework applied to AI**: capacity versus purchase, institutions as the binding layer, and the collective rather than the firm as the unit. The verdict is **half-confirmed, half-qualified**, and the qualification is the useful part.
>
> **It post-dates my training cutoff.** Every figure below is *as reported by the document*. None is independently verified.

## When to Use
- **Whenever an AI, 4IR or digitalisation pillar is being designed.**
- When his AI caution is invoked.
- When someone proposes copying **Brasil Mais Produtivo** or a similar MSME digitalisation programme. **Read the warning below first.**

## Headline — does his AI caution survive?

| His claim (VERIFIED, Guardian 29 Mar 2026) | The brief | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| AI makes industrial catching up **very difficult** | AI market "becoming more concentrated and capital-intensive, **making it difficult for newcomers to enter**"; **top 5 countries = 85% of notable AI models**; frontier models cost **>USD 100m**; chip access geopolitically controlled | **CONFIRMED — for catching up via AI *development*** |
| — | **"For developing countries, the main industrial opportunity offered by AI lies in using it to boost the productivity and efficiency of existing industries"**; adoption "still in the early stages"; "benefits for early adopters" | **QUALIFIED — catching up via AI *adoption* is open, and there is a window** |
| National autonomous technology capacity is the missing piece | Developing countries "are **primarily users** of AI models"; firm-level barriers include **"R&D activities performed abroad"** and **"technology adoption decisions made by the headquarters, usually located abroad"** | **His founding conclusion, restated in AI** |
| The cluster holds the capability, not the firm | **"While SMEs might not individually produce enough data to train AI models, if companies pool their data, together they can develop AI models tailored to their needs"** | **His collective unit, in data form** |

**⚠️ AND NOTE THE DATES — this is the strongest convergence in the collection.** He said *"The Fourth Industrial Revolution does not replace the old economy; it reprograms it"* in the Guardian on **29 March 2026**. The UNIDO brief said *"the main industrial opportunity offered by AI lies in using it to boost the productivity and efficiency of existing industries"* in **March 2026**. **Same month. Same claim. Neither cites the other, and he is not an author of the brief.** Two independent arrivals at the same conclusion is the strongest form of evidence this collection contains.

**The synthesis, which is genuinely his even though he did not write it:** catching up by *building* AI is very difficult and getting harder. Catching up by *using* AI in industries you already have is open — **and it requires exactly what he has said industrialisation always required: national autonomous technology capacity, institutions that carry technical change, and capability held collectively rather than firm by firm.**

## THE EVIDENCE

### The concentration — and the absence that matters most
- **Reported (Figure 3, sourced to Stanford University 2025):** total notable AI models by geographic area, 2003–24. **United States 558. China 102. United Kingdom 72. Canada 61. Germany 28. Top 5 = 85% of the total.** "developing countries (except China) are **primarily users of AI models**, with only a few having notable models."
- **The finding the brief does not draw out, and you should.** The chart lists **31 geographies**: the US, China, UK, Canada, Germany, France, Republic of Korea, Switzerland, Israel, Hong Kong SAR, Singapore, Japan, Australia, Netherlands, Czechia, Finland, Poland, Taiwan, UAE, Austria, Belgium, India, Ireland, Saudi Arabia, Argentina, Iran, Italy, Norway, Russian Federation, Spain, Sweden. **Not one African country appears. Not South Africa. Not anywhere.** Argentina and Iran are on the list; the entire continent is not.
- **Caveat:** "Notable" is Stanford's construct — models "particularly influential within the AI/machine learning ecosystem" — and a threshold measure will always under-count the tail. The absence is not a claim that no AI model has been built in Africa. **It is a claim that none has been influential enough to register**, which for a capability argument is the more damaging version.

### The capital intensity
- **Reported (Figure 4, sourced to Stanford University 2025):** estimated training cost of selected generative AI, 2019–24, inflation-adjusted. **"the latest generative AI models have cost over USD 100 million, highlighting that entry into the AI market is becoming expensive."** Models charted include Transformer (2017), GPT-3 175B (2020), Megatron-Turing NLG 530B (2021), LaMDA and PaLM 540B (2022), GPT-4, PaLM 2, Llama 2-70B, Falcon-180B and Gemini 1.0 Ultra (2023), Mistral Large, Llama 3.1-405B and Grok-2 (2024).
- **Caveat — DO NOT QUOTE PER-MODEL FIGURES.** They are bar-chart estimates in a chart I am reading, not tabulated values, and they are Stanford's estimates of costs that no lab publishes. **The safe claim is the text's own: over USD 100 million for the latest models.** That is sufficient for the argument.

### The chip geopolitics — and a warning about volatility
- **Reported:** "the supply of specialized hardware (i.e., AI chips) is controlled by companies in a few countries, and access to these products can be geopolitically challenging. For example, **in January 2025, the United States government issued a *Framework for Artificial Intelligence Diffusion*, introducing export restrictions on specialized AI chips for 120 countries. The framework was rescinded before it took effect and replaced by guidance on export controls specifically for China.** These policies highlight the strategic importance of geopolitics and its impact on AI-related hardware."
- **Caveat — READ THIS BEFORE PUTTING ANY OF IT IN A DOCUMENT.** The brief itself records a policy **issued and rescinded within months**. This is the most volatile material in the entire roster. A strategy running to 2035 that names a specific export-control regime will be wrong before it is gazetted. **Make the structural point — chip access is geopolitically contingent and not a thing a province or a country can assume — and name no regime.**

### The adoption opportunity — the qualification that matters
- **Reported:** **"For developing countries, the main industrial opportunity offered by AI lies in using it to boost the productivity and efficiency of existing industries. In practice, AI in industry is still in the early stages of implementation, and its quick integration can generate benefits for early adopters."** And, sourced to a **WIPO (2024)** patent landscape report: **"the manufacturing sector accounts for only a small fraction of the generative AI patents published to date, with the majority of the sector's patents focusing on software applications. This shows that AI in manufacturing is still in its early stages."**
- **Caveat:** This is the half of the picture that qualifies the AI caution, and it is doing a lot of work. Note the shape: **the frontier has not yet taken the industrial ground.** Whether "early stages" constitutes a genuine window for latecomers, or merely a lag before the same concentration arrives in manufacturing, the brief does not establish. It asserts the optimistic reading. **Perez would call this a window; Keun Lee would ask whether the technology cycle is short enough for incumbents' advantage to decay. Neither question is answered here.**
- **But the direction is clear and it is the practically important claim: the opportunity is in adoption within existing industries, not in model development.** That is an adjacency argument (Hausmann) and a capability argument (his) and it is the opposite of what most 4IR chapters propose.

### The barrier taxonomy — his institutional argument, in a table
- **Reported (Figure 5, sourced to Cambridge Industrial Innovation Policy 2022 and The Commonwealth 2023):** barriers to adopting AI and digital technologies in industry in developing countries, split firm-level and contextual.

**Firm-level:**
- **Technical:** technical challenges integrating new technologies into existing systems; **dependence on foreign technology providers**; limited understanding of the skills needed; limited capacity to attract talent; safety and cybersecurity risks; a lack of access, use and protection of data.
- **Economic:** a lack of awareness of technologies and their potential benefits (the business case); short-term profit-seeking; financial constraints; the perception that technologies are too expensive.
- **Organizational:** **R&D activities performed abroad**; **technology adoption decisions made by the headquarters, usually located abroad**; organizational and individual resistance to change; unclear implementation strategy; **top-down implementation approaches with little involvement of workers**; lack of cooperation among business functions and departments.

**Contextual:**
- **Skills:** mismatch between graduates' skills and the skills demanded by industry; shortages in ICT, specialised manufacturing, technology adaptation and integration, data analysis, managerial skills, fluency in foreign languages.
- **Entrepreneurial and innovation systems:** **limited collaboration between industry and research organizations**; **absent or weak technology-transfer systems**; limited access to specialised expertise and technology facilities; limited training services; limited access to finance for SMEs.
- **Institutional framework:** limited cyber-security, data protection and data privacy regulation; **lack of reference architecture and standards**; **little focus on manufacturing in digital policy frameworks**; **lack of coordination across ministries**; complex and lengthy processes for importing technologies.
- **Infrastructure:** limited physical infrastructure; **costly and unstable electricity supply**; limited and costly internet service and data-centres.

- **Caveat — three of these are his, verbatim, without attribution.** **"R&D activities performed abroad"** and **"technology adoption decisions made by the headquarters, usually located abroad"** are *precisely* the failure of **autonomous** capacity: a firm whose adoption decisions are made elsewhere holds no capacity, whatever equipment sits on its floor. **"Absent or weak technology-transfer systems"** and **"limited collaboration between industry and research organizations"** are the firm–university and firm–firm linkages of his UNU-INTECH survey work — see `inter-organisational-linkages.md`. And **"top-down implementation approaches with little involvement of workers"** is Lundvall's work organisation variable, arriving in a UNIDO brief as a barrier to AI adoption.
- **The infrastructure row is where the South African argument gets uncomfortable:** "costly and unstable electricity supply." Note the collision with **Kaplan's 2024 claim** that start-ups are "largely independent of the constraints that significantly curtail growth in other areas of the South African economy — electricity and transport." **AI-enabled manufacturing is not.** Whatever exemption the start-up sector enjoys does not extend to the data centres and the shop floors.

### The data-pooling argument — his collective unit, in data form
- **Reported:** "One of the primary resources required for developing new AI solutions is high-quality data. **Developing countries could leverage data from their own industries to develop localized AI solutions.** However, manufacturing data is often proprietary and sensitive, making companies reluctant to share it. **This creates a chance for governments to build secure platforms for industry collaboration to develop AI applications using local data.**" And: **"While small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) might not individually produce enough data to train AI models, if companies pool their data, together they can develop AI models tailored to their needs."**
- **Caveat — this is the sharpest convergence in the file.** His cluster finding is that **no single micro or small firm holds the capability; the cluster holds it, distributed.** Here the same structure appears in data: **no single SME holds enough data; pooled, they do.** Same claim, new substrate. **The unit of analysis is collective, and the state's role is to build the thing that permits pooling.** He has been making this argument since the 1980s about knowledge; the brief makes it about data without noticing it is the same argument.

### Box 1 — China's Industrial Brain, and the fact that it is provincial
- **Reported:** the **Industrial Brain programme in Shandong** "aims to unlock the potential of industrial data resources. To advance this initiative, **the province built a single unified system to manage industrial data** and support a comprehensive map to support innovative data applications." **"By the end of 2024, Shandong launched 80 pilot projects for the Industrial Brain construction, 12 of which were recognized as provincial demonstration cases."** Similar programmes are promoted in **Zhejiang and Shanghai**, "where authorities are working to digitalize the manufacturing sector using common databases, standards and equipment. This coordinated approach provides Chinese manufacturers with access to large datasets and broader influence across the supply chain."
- The functional structure (Figure 2) runs: a **service portal** (industry development, government-side, enterprise-side); a **service platform** covering **industry governance** — *industry landscape mapping, industry monitoring, industry competition mapping, investment attraction* — plus **industry collaboration and innovation** (digital centralised purchasing and selling, digital finance, synchronized manufacturing, **manufacturing capacity sharing**), **enterprise digital transformation** (AR factory, smart production, smart procurement, smart warehousing) and **comprehensive enterprise services** (policy services, regulation guidance, standards compliance, IP, skills services); an **algorithm development platform**; a **data aggregation platform** (trusted access and authentication, shared data circulation, data classification and grading, data governance, data security and privacy; basic / industry / scenario data pools); and a **computer power scheduling platform**.
- **Caveat — READ THIS TWICE. Shandong is a province. Zhejiang is a province. This is a provincial-scale instrument, built by a provincial government.** Everything else in this roster that a province cannot do — exchange control, immigration, the science base — this is not that. **A sub-national government built a shared industrial data platform and ran 80 pilots.**
- **And note what sits in its service platform: "industry landscape mapping" and "industry monitoring."** That is **Sutton's Enterprise Map as a live government data service** rather than a book. The thing this roster has been telling you to commission, China's provinces built as software.
- **The honest limits.** This is **China** — state capacity, firm compliance with data-sharing mandates, and fiscal depth that a South African province does not have, and the brief reports no evaluation, no cost, and no outcome beyond pilot counts. **"80 pilot projects, 12 recognized as provincial demonstration cases" is an activity metric.** Nobody has shown it works. Kaplan would ask which institution delivers it here and whether it can.

### Box 2 — Brasil Mais Produtivo, and a number you must not repeat
- **Reported:** launched **2016**, Brazil's flagship initiative to boost productivity, competitiveness and digital maturity of MSMEs across industry, commerce and services. Coordinated by **MDIC** (Ministry of Development, Industry, Trade and Services), implemented through **SEBRAE** (micro and small enterprise support), **SENAI** (industrial learning), **ABDI**, **EMBRAPII**, **BNDES** and **FINEP**.
  - **The design, which is genuinely interesting:** "low-cost, high-impact consultancy and training services" plus a national digital platform; **tailored diagnostics and technical assistance** in lean manufacturing, energy efficiency, innovation management and digitalization. **Segmented delivery** — industrial MSMEs get hands-on consultancy from **SENAI**; commerce and service firms are served through **SEBRAE's network of Local Innovation Agents**. **Sliding subsidy** — micro and small industrial firms receive **fully subsidised** consultancy; medium-sized firms up to **70 per cent** coverage; services-sector firms get process guidance and **reimbursements up to USD 400 for digital tools**.
  - **Scale:** the revamped programme aims to reach **200,000 firms** and deliver **over 90,000 consultancy engagements by 2027**. In **São Paulo** alone, a version called *Jornada de Transformação Digital* served around **18,000 firms**, supported by over **700 industrial consultants**. It is a central component of Brazil's **Nova Indústria Brasil** industrial strategy (2024–2033), **Mission 4** on digital transformation.
  - **The claim:** **"The programme's pilot (2016–2018) was successful and achieved average productivity gains of over 50 per cent among 3,000 industrial MSMEs."**
- **⚠️ CAVEAT — THE 50% FIGURE SHOULD NOT ENTER A DEDAT DOCUMENT AND PROBABLY SHOULD NOT BE BELIEVED.**
  - **No control group is mentioned.** No counterfactual. No identification strategy. "Average productivity gains of over 50 per cent" among firms that **selected into** a subsidised consultancy programme is a before-and-after number on a self-selected sample.
  - **Compare it to the best-identified estimate in existence.** **Bloom, Eifert, Mahajan, McKenzie & Roberts (2013), QJE** — months of **free intensive consulting** from an international firm, delivered to **large multi-plant** Indian textile firms, **randomised**, with control plants — produced **17%**. This brief reports **over 50%**, from a pilot, for **MSMEs**, with no control. **A programme cannot beat the randomised frontier by 3x on a weaker population with a lighter touch.** Something else is in the number: selection, regression to the mean, Hawthorne effects, a productivity measure of convenience, or all four.
  - **Route this to McKenzie and Bloom before it goes anywhere.** Bloom's `dose-and-population.md` is the file. This is precisely the artefact that guardrail exists for — and it is about to be attractive, because B+P is exactly the programme a provincial MSME strategy would want to copy.
  - **The design is still worth stealing. The effect claim is not.** Segmented delivery, a sliding subsidy that fully covers micro firms and tapers to 70% at medium, and a small digital voucher (USD 400) are real, cheap, legible design features. **Take those. Commission your own evaluation. Do not inherit the 50%.**

### AI for industrial policymaking — and the tools that already exist
- **Reported (Figure 6):** AI applications across the policy cycle.
  - **Formulation:** synthesising public consultation information; detecting supply chain gaps and vulnerabilities; mapping existing policies to identify synergies and overlaps; identifying potential export markets. Named instances: the UK's **Incubator for Artificial Intelligence (i.AI)** developing **Consult**, "an AI tool that identifies themes and presents them via dashboards", plus the open-source package **ThemeFinder**; the **Netherlands Enterprise Agency** applying **topic modelling** to over **1,000 EU-funded projects** to map R&D themes and identify synergies; and the **Supply Chain AI Lab at the Institute for Manufacturing, University of Cambridge**, applying complexity science and agent-based modelling to reveal vulnerabilities, risks and procurement strategies.
  - **Implementation:** accelerating eligibility requirement checks; identifying target firms and population; mapping relationships between agents involved in policy delivery. China's Industrial Brain tracks industrial dynamics in real time; the **Centre for European Economic Research** uses semantic analysis of patents and funding data to identify potential university–firm partnerships.
  - **Evaluation:** scenario analysis and simulation modelling; detecting fraud and misuse of benefits; predictions of business interruption and bankruptcy risk. Russia's Federal Service for Veterinary and Phytosanitary Supervision traces counterfeit food products; machine learning is used to simulate trade and tax policy effects; AI models assess the financial health of firms applying for policy support.
- **Caveat — the honest one, and it is close to home.** **"Synthesising public consultation information"** is the first item on the list, and it is a thing you are doing by hand right now across Saldanha, George and the DEDAT workshops. The UK is building **Consult** and **ThemeFinder** is open source. **That is a real, checkable, near-term efficiency and it is the only item in this figure a provincial department could act on this year.** Everything else assumes national data holdings.
- **And the caution the brief does not give:** none of these tools is evaluated in the brief. They are described as existing, not as working.

### The five recommendations — and who they are for
- **Reported:** "The recommendations below are **more relevant for middle-income countries**, but developing countries can also benefit."
  1. **Monitor and share advances in industrial AI** — case study repositories, workshops, policy dialogues, "particularly to SMEs and resource-constrained stakeholders."
  2. **Address barriers to adopting AI and digital technology** — "regularly conduct diagnostic assessments through surveys, focus groups, and stakeholder consultations"; use targeted funding, technical assistance, **public procurement**, standards development and innovation incentives.
  3. **Create local data ecosystems to support local AI innovation** — "facilitate the formation of an industrial data-sharing consortia or community of practice that unites firms, research institutions and other stakeholders willing to securely share data... **reduce the dependency on external platforms and foreign technologies.**"
  4. **Leverage AI for smarter industrial policymaking** — "particularly in **data-scarce and capacity-constrained environments**."
  5. **Promote the adoption of responsible and inclusive AI** — safety, fairness, transparency and accountability in national AI strategies; "prioritising applications that benefit a range of firms and workers."
- **Caveat:** **South Africa is middle-income — these are aimed at exactly its tier.** Note that #3 is the one with a named working precedent (Industrial Brain), the one that is **provincial in that precedent**, and the one that is **his collective-capability argument**. If the dojo has a single recommendation to lift from this brief, it is #3.
- Also note **Table 2** lists **"attraction of foreign qualified workers"** as the policy tool for **low absorptive capacity** — Hausmann's people-as-transmission, arriving in a UNIDO instrument menu.

## How to Apply

1. **Never attribute this brief to him.** Labrunie, Fan and Leal-Ayala, Cambridge / UNIDO, March 2026.
2. **Split his AI caution in two.** Catching up by *building* AI: confirmed very difficult, and hardening. Catching up by *adopting* AI in existing industries: open, with a claimed window. **The second is where a provincial strategy lives.**
3. **Use the concentration finding, and name the African absence.** Top 5 = 85%; 31 geographies listed; no African country among them.
4. **Do not name a chip export-control regime.** The brief records one issued and rescinded within months. Make the structural point only.
5. **Take recommendation #3 seriously and note it is provincial in its precedent.** Shandong — a province — built a shared industrial data platform. Data pooling is his collective unit in a new substrate.
6. **Steal Brasil Mais Produtivo's design; refuse its number.** Segmented delivery, sliding subsidy, small vouchers — yes. **The >50% productivity claim — no. Route to Bloom's `dose-and-population.md` and McKenzie.**
7. **Note the electricity collision.** Kaplan says start-ups escape the electricity constraint. AI-enabled manufacturing does not.
8. **Look at ThemeFinder.** It is open source, it does the consultation synthesis you are doing by hand, and it is the one item here a province could use this year.

## Examples

**Situation:** A provincial strategy proposes a 4IR / AI pillar aimed at positioning the province as an AI hub.

**Application:** The brief refutes the hub framing and supplies a better one. **Positioning as a developer of AI is a contest against a distribution where five countries hold 85% of notable models, frontier training runs cost over USD 100 million, chip access is geopolitically contingent, and no African country appears in the 31 geographies charted at all.** That is the AI caution, confirmed. **But the brief's own recommendation for middle-income countries is different and reachable: "the main industrial opportunity offered by AI lies in using it to boost the productivity and efficiency of existing industries"** — which for the Western Cape means agri-processing, logistics, and the existing manufacturing base, not a hub. And the enabling instrument is **local data ecosystems**: pooling data across firms too small to train anything alone, which is his collective-capability argument and which **a Chinese province has actually built**. The pillar rewrites from *become an AI hub* to *pool the data of the industries we already have, and adopt into them* — which is cheaper, provincial in scale, has a working precedent, and is the only version the evidence supports.

**Result:** A 4IR pillar that is about adoption in the existing base rather than a hub that will not happen.
