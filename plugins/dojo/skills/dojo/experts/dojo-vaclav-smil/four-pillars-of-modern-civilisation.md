# The Four Pillars of Modern Civilisation

## When to Use
- When the conversation turns to decarbonisation of "industry" or "hard-to-abate sectors" without specifying what.
- When a strategy assumes industrial transformation by 2050 without engaging the materials base.
- When degrowth advocates claim we can simply consume less of everything.
- When a development plan ignores what urbanisation actually requires materially.

## Fails When
- **The question is not about physical resources.** The four pillars are about industry and infrastructure; they do not illuminate questions about software, services, finance, or culture.
- **You treat the list as complete.** Aluminium, copper, paper, glass, sand, lithium, cobalt, and others also matter. The four are the largest and most foundational, not the only material constraints.
- **You let the analysis become moral.** The four pillars are a physical fact. Whether we should consume more or less is a separate question.

## Core Concept
Modern civilisation rests on four material pillars produced in the billions of tonnes per year, all overwhelmingly from fossil fuels, none with a proven low-carbon alternative at current global demand scale.

**Ammonia (NH₃).** About 180 million tonnes per year globally. Made by the Haber-Bosch process from atmospheric nitrogen and natural gas (CH₄ supplies the hydrogen and most of the process heat). The base of synthetic nitrogen fertiliser, on which roughly half the human population depends for its food calories. Without Haber-Bosch ammonia, perhaps four billion people would not be alive. The process is the largest single industrial consumer of natural gas in the world.

**Plastics.** About 400 million tonnes per year globally, made overwhelmingly from natural-gas liquids and naphtha (oil fraction). The material of medicine (IV bags, syringes, sterile packaging, prosthetics), food preservation (without which urban food systems would collapse), insulation (the largest single contribution to building energy efficiency), clothing, electronics, pipes, seals, and the components of essentially every industrial product. A genuinely plastic-free modern hospital is not possible.

**Steel.** About 1.9 billion tonnes per year. Produced mainly via the blast-furnace / basic-oxygen-furnace route using coking coal (about 1.4 tonnes of coal per tonne of steel) at temperatures around 1500°C. The structural metal of every building over a few storeys, every bridge, every car, every train, every ship, every pipeline, every wind turbine. Alternative routes exist (electric arc furnace using scrap, direct reduced iron using hydrogen) but the green-steel deployment globally is in single-percent territory.

**Cement.** About 4 billion tonnes per year, of which more than half is produced in China. Made by heating limestone (CaCO₃) and clay in a kiln at about 1450°C, which releases CO₂ both from the fuel and from the limestone itself (the chemistry of calcination). The matrix of every concrete structure. China has produced more cement in three years than the United States has in its entire history. Decarbonising cement requires solving two distinct problems: process heat and process chemistry.

**Why this matters.** Strategies that claim to decarbonise civilisation without addressing the four pillars are not credible. The four pillars together account for a substantial share of global industrial CO₂ emissions. They have no scaled alternative. The technologies being proposed (green hydrogen for steel and ammonia, carbon capture and storage for cement, mechanical recycling for plastics) are real but their deployment is at single-digit percentages of current production.

For a development context: any plan to urbanise the African population at middle-income densities by 2050 requires cement, steel, and ammonia at volumes that current "green development" frameworks do not engineer pathways for. The four pillars are not optional. The question is whether the materials base of development is being made explicit or assumed away.

## How to Apply
1. **When industrial decarbonisation is mentioned, ask which pillar.** Steel decarbonisation, cement decarbonisation, ammonia decarbonisation, and plastics decarbonisation are very different problems with very different difficulties.
2. **Demand volumes.** Any pillar-decarbonisation plan must specify what fraction of current global production it covers, by what date, at what cost.
3. **Distinguish process heat from process chemistry.** Steel and cement both face the temperature problem; cement also faces the CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ chemistry problem, which is harder.
4. **Check the alternative-route maturity.** Green hydrogen DRI steel exists at pilot scale; commercial deployment is at single percentages. Cement CCS is at demonstration scale. Plastics from biomass / recycling is significant but not at scale.
5. **Project the materials forward for the development case.** Africa's urbanisation pipeline implies cement, steel, ammonia demand that any serious climate-plus-development strategy has to engineer.

## Examples
**Situation:** A national climate strategy includes a 2050 net-zero target with an "industrial decarbonisation" pillar but does not specify the steel and cement pathways.
**Application:** This is the typical pattern. The high-level target is set; the four pillars are not addressed at sector level. Demand a sector-by-sector pathway with volumes, technologies, costs, and dates. Without those, the industrial pillar is decoration.
**Result:** Either the plan acquires substantive pathways or the strategy team confronts that it does not yet have one.

**Situation:** A degrowth-influenced consultancy proposes that the global economy halve its material throughput by 2050.
**Application:** Pose the specific question: by what factor must global ammonia production fall, and what fraction of the human population is the proposal willing to displace from current food security? By what factor cement, and which infrastructure does this constrain? The argument is morally serious but physically must engage the numbers.
**Result:** The strong degrowth claim either acquires a specific materials engineering — which it usually cannot — or moderates to a less ambitious efficiency-and-substitution programme.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat "industry" as a single decarbonisation problem.
**Why:** Steel, cement, ammonia, and plastics have different feedstock chemistries and different binding constraints. Treating them as one problem produces unhelpful strategy.

**Don't:** Assume pilot-scale demonstrations are equivalent to deployed industries.
**Why:** Green-steel pilots and cement-CCS demonstrations exist. Their commercial-scale industries do not yet. The gap between pilot and industry is typically a decade or more.

**Don't:** Frame the four pillars as a counsel of despair.
**Why:** They are a reality to be engineered, not an argument against decarbonisation. The point is to plan for the materials base honestly rather than wish it away.
