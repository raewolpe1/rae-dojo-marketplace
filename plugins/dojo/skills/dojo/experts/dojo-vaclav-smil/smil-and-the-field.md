# Smil and the Field

## When to Use
- When situating Smil's position relative to mainstream energy and climate institutions.
- When the user wants to know who disagrees with him and why.
- When triangulating among competing voices on energy futures.
- When teaching the intellectual landscape of energy and decarbonisation debate.

## Fails When
- **The question is about one specific framework.** "Tell me about Smil's position on X" is a different question from "where does Smil sit in the field?"
- **You reduce the field to a single axis.** The energy debate has multiple axes (technology optimism, growth orientation, climate urgency, institutional trust) that do not collapse.

## Core Concept
Smil sits in a distinctive position: empirically rigorous, historically grounded, sceptical of both techno-optimism and pure degrowth, uncomfortable for almost every other camp.

**Where Smil broadly agrees with the IPCC.** On the underlying climate science. On the urgency of decarbonisation. On the need for substantial energy-system transformation. Where he differs from the dominant IPCC scenarios: he believes the rate of energy-system transformation implied by 1.5°C scenarios is physically unlikely on the basis of historical transition rates and current deployment evidence. The IPCC SR1.5 pathways assume rates of decarbonisation that have no historical precedent. He treats them as illustrative scenarios, not as plans.

**Where Smil disagrees with the IEA.** The International Energy Agency's net-zero-by-2050 scenarios are widely cited. Smil's view: the scenarios are mathematically possible but operationally unrealistic given historical deployment rates, materials demand for the transition itself, and the social and political constraints on land use, permitting, and capital allocation. The IEA scenarios serve a useful North-Star function; they are not predictions.

**Where Smil disagrees with the McKinsey / climate-tech-consulting establishment.** The genre of "transition pathway" reports produced by major consulting firms typically assumes technology adoption rates and cost curve extrapolations that are at the high end of historical experience. Smil's critique is that these reports systematically under-weight the materials demand for the transition itself, the engineering challenges of industrial-heat and feedstock decarbonisation, and the slow pace of infrastructure replacement.

**Where Smil disagrees with the Silicon Valley techno-optimist camp.** Andreessen, Altman, Hoffman, and others. Smil's critique is that the camp confuses information-technology trajectories (which were exceptional) with energy and materials trajectories (which are governed by different physics). AI cannot solve climate by itself; fusion is not 2035; direct air capture is not at scale. The optimism that worked in software does not transfer to energy.

**Where Smil disagrees with the degrowth camp.** Kallis, Hickel, and others. Smil's critique: pure degrowth fails the materialist test on ammonia (and therefore food), cement (and therefore housing), steel (and therefore infrastructure). The strong universal degrowth claim cannot be operationalised without consequences for human welfare its proponents have not engaged.

**Where Smil agrees with post-growth and doughnut economics.** Tim Jackson (already in your library) and Kate Raworth. Their positions are more careful — they argue for ecological boundaries and social floors, not pure contraction. Smil is broadly sympathetic to these frames, while emphasising that the materials base of meeting the social floor at global scale is itself substantial.

**Where Smil agrees with Bill Gates.** Both share an empirical, engineering-oriented approach to climate. Gates has called Smil his favourite author. The agreement is on the breadth and difficulty of the decarbonisation challenge, especially industrial-heat, transport, and feedstocks. They differ on specific bets: Gates is more willing to fund speculative technologies (small modular nuclear, novel cement, novel batteries) as portfolio bets; Smil is more sceptical that any single technology bet will deliver at the timescale required.

**Where Smil is alone.** Few other commentators combine the breadth (energy, materials, food, transport, demography), the historical depth (centuries of transitions and innovations catalogued), and the discipline of writing in absolute numbers. The closest analogues are Robert Bryce (energy journalism), Mark Mills (energy commentary, more politically conservative), and parts of the academic energy-history community (Roger Fouquet, others). None is as widely read.

**Where to read Smil's actual position.** Books rather than tweets. He has been writing for forty years; the genre demands depth. *Energy and Civilization: A History* (2017) is the definitive long-form work. *How the World Really Works* (2022) is the bestseller, accessible to non-specialists. *Growth* (2019), *Numbers Don't Lie* (2020), *Invention and Innovation* (2023) extend the body of work. The earlier books on energy in nature and society, on Japan, on China, on power density, on the four pillars are technical and detailed.

## How to Apply
1. **Use the field map to calibrate users' expectations.** If they have been reading McKinsey, Smil will sound sceptical. If they have been reading degrowth, he will sound materialist. If they have been reading Andreessen, he will sound conservative. The disorientation is the point.
2. **For triangulation, position Smil between the major camps.** He is broadly aligned with IPCC science and Gates-style engineering empiricism; he diverges from both the consulting-firm transition-pathway genre and the Silicon Valley techno-optimist genre.
3. **Direct readers to the actual books.** Smil's full position is in the books, not in summaries. He requires reading.
4. **Engage the empirical disagreements honestly.** Some IPCC pathways may turn out closer to true than Smil expects; some Silicon Valley bets may pay off faster than he expects. The discipline is to engage the specific evidence, not to take Smil's authority as a substitute for the analysis.

## Examples
**Situation:** A municipality is being given conflicting advice — McKinsey says 2050 net zero is achievable, an academic critic says it is not, the municipal officials don't know who to trust.
**Application:** The two voices are answering different questions. McKinsey is producing a target-aligned pathway report; the academic is producing a probability-of-realisation estimate. Both can be useful. The municipality's question — what should we actually do in the next five years — is best answered by the work that fits in the gap: identify the no-regret interventions, the conditional interventions, the long-horizon options. Smil's perspective sharpens this gap-work.
**Result:** Municipality stops trying to choose between voices and instead uses both for different purposes.

**Situation:** A donor wants Smil's view on "what should African countries do about climate?"
**Application:** Smil would refuse the framing. The first question is what each country can physically and economically do; the second is how that interacts with global emissions and finance flows. The frame "what should Africa do about climate" presumes a level of agency over climate trajectories that Africa does not have (Africa contributes about 4% of global emissions; what Africa does is more about adaptation and energy access than about mitigation of global emissions). Reframe.
**Result:** The conversation moves to the more useful questions: energy access, adaptation, just-transition for coal-dependent African economies.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce the field to optimist vs pessimist.
**Why:** The substantive disagreements are about specific physical and historical claims, not about psychological orientation.

**Don't:** Use Smil as authority for refusing to act.
**Why:** Smil is not against decarbonisation; he is for honest planning. The two are different.

**Don't:** Use Smil as a stick against renewables.
**Why:** He is broadly supportive of renewables deployment. His critique is of the timelines and the under-weighted materials demand, not of the technology direction.
