# Material Throughput

## When to Use
- When a "decoupling" or "dematerialisation" claim is being made for an economy or sector.
- When circular economy is being proposed as a near-term solution at scale.
- When a development plan treats infrastructure as a fixed cost rather than an ongoing materials commitment.
- When teaching how to read an economy by its mass.

## Fails When
- **The economic activity is genuinely intangible.** Software, insurance, financial services have small direct material footprints. Their indirect footprints (data centres, offices, the materials that allow customers to consume their services) are not small.
- **You treat all materials equally.** A tonne of cement has different emissions implications from a tonne of timber. Material throughput must be weighted by criticality and embodied energy.
- **You use it as a moral cudgel.** Material throughput is a descriptive metric. Whether throughput should be lower is a separate normative question.

## Core Concept
Global material throughput — the mass of resources extracted, processed, used, and disposed of by the economy each year — was approximately 100 billion tonnes in 2024, up from about 27 billion in 1970. It has roughly quadrupled in five decades and is projected by the OECD's Global Material Resources Outlook to reach 167 billion tonnes by 2060 on current trajectories.

The composition matters:
- About 40% biomass (food crops, timber, fodder)
- About 25% non-metallic minerals (sand, gravel, limestone — the cement, glass, and construction inputs)
- About 20% fossil fuels
- About 10% metal ores

Per capita material consumption varies dramatically by country: roughly 30+ tonnes per person per year in rich countries, 2-5 tonnes per person per year in low-income countries. The development trajectory from low to middle income has historically been a steep increase in per-capita material consumption, particularly cement, steel, and asphalt.

**Decoupling claims need scrutiny.** The economic literature distinguishes "relative decoupling" (material throughput grows more slowly than GDP) from "absolute decoupling" (material throughput falls while GDP grows). Relative decoupling has been observed in many rich economies. Absolute decoupling at country level, sustained over decades, is rare and largely an artefact of offshoring material-intensive production to other countries. Globally, absolute decoupling between GDP and material throughput has not been observed.

**Why this matters for climate and ecology.** Material extraction, processing, and disposal account for a substantial share of global greenhouse-gas emissions (estimates run from 30% to 50% depending on methodology and boundary), nearly all global land-system change, most freshwater use, and most biodiversity loss. Climate strategy that does not address materials cannot succeed even if electricity decarbonises fully.

**For development planning.** A central question for African, South Asian, and Latin American development is whether the historical materials-intensive path to middle-income status can be modified, or whether it must be substantially traversed before efficiency and circularity become operational. Smil's position is that the materials-intensive phase is largely unavoidable: cities require cement and steel, agriculture requires synthetic nitrogen, transport requires asphalt and steel. The question is whether the materials can be sourced and used more efficiently than the historical Western pattern, not whether they can be avoided.

**The circular economy in proper proportion.** Circular economy strategies are useful at the margin and necessary in the long run, but at current rates of build-out they recover a small fraction of global throughput. Cement is not meaningfully recyclable into new cement; concrete is recyclable into aggregate. Steel recycles well (scrap is the basis of the electric-arc-furnace route, about 30% of global steel) but virgin production still dominates because demand grows faster than scrap availability in industrialising economies. Plastics recycling captures less than 10% of global plastic flow. These are technical realities, not strategic failures.

## How to Apply
1. **Distinguish per-capita from total throughput.** A country can lower per-capita while total rises due to population growth and urbanisation.
2. **Distinguish offshoring from genuine reduction.** A rich country whose material throughput per capita is falling may be displacing it to other countries.
3. **Weight materials by emissions intensity.** Cement and aluminium are emissions-intensive per tonne; biomass and recycled metals are not.
4. **For development planning, separate the unavoidable from the avoidable.** Basic infrastructure (water, sewerage, housing, roads, schools, hospitals) is largely unavoidable. Luxury consumption is more elastic.
5. **Distinguish material throughput from energy throughput.** Related but not identical. A solar economy uses materials differently than a fossil-fuel economy, not less in mass.

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor-funded "sustainable urbanisation" programme for a sub-Saharan city is being designed.
**Application:** Begin from the materials forecast for the city's population trajectory: housing, water, sewerage, transport infrastructure. Convert to volumes of cement, steel, asphalt, copper. Identify where local production, recycling, and material-efficient design can reduce throughput per capita. Resist the framing that the city can leapfrog the materials phase — it cannot. The frame should be "less materials-intensive than the Chinese / European historical pattern", not "materials-free".
**Result:** The programme acquires a realistic materials profile and the donor conversation moves from rhetoric to engineering.

**Situation:** A national climate strategy claims that GDP and emissions have decoupled over the last decade.
**Application:** Check whether the decoupling is genuine or whether material-intensive production has moved offshore (typically to China or South-East Asia) while GDP composition has shifted to services. The decoupling story usually weakens substantially under consumption-based accounting.
**Result:** The strategy gets a more honest baseline, which strengthens it.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat material throughput as monolithic.
**Why:** A tonne of biomass and a tonne of plastic have very different implications. The aggregate hides the substance.

**Don't:** Claim decoupling without consumption-based accounting.
**Why:** Production-based accounting credits the offshoring country with the emissions reduction; consumption-based accounting credits the importing country. Both are needed.

**Don't:** Treat the circular economy as a near-term substitute for primary materials.
**Why:** At realistic rates of build-out, secondary materials cannot meet a growing global economy's demand. Circularity is a necessary part of the long-run picture, not a short-run alternative to primary production.
