# Critique of Techno-Optimism

## When to Use
- When a strategy assumes AI, fusion, direct air capture, or green hydrogen will deliver decarbonisation at scale within a decade.
- When a Silicon Valley or climate-tech narrative is being treated as policy-grade.
- When teaching the gap between laboratory demonstration and deployed industry.
- When grounding investor / donor conversations in physical reality.

## Fails When
- **The technology is genuinely deployed at scale.** Solar PV, lithium-ion batteries, and electric vehicles have moved from techno-optimist territory to deployed-industry territory. The skepticism that applied at the demonstration stage does not apply now.
- **You become reflexively dismissive.** Smil is empirical, not nihilistic. Some technologies will scale. The discipline is to engage the specific evidence.
- **You confuse "not yet" with "never".** Many technologies that were techno-optimist claims fifty years ago are now deployed. The discipline is to engage timescales, not to predict permanent failure.

## Core Concept
Techno-optimism is the position that transformative technology, currently in development or early deployment, will resolve the major problems of climate, energy, food, and resources within a relatively short horizon. It is the dominant narrative in Silicon Valley and increasingly in mainstream climate-tech investment.

Smil's critique is not against technology but against the systematic miscounting that techno-optimism entails. Several specific patterns:

**The demonstration-to-deployment gap.** A working laboratory or pilot-scale demonstration is not a working industry. The gap between the two is typically a decade or more, often longer, and many technologies do not cross it. Fusion has had working demonstrations since the 1950s and has no deployed industry. Direct air capture has working pilots; global capacity in 2024 is on the order of 0.01 million tonnes CO₂ per year, against global emissions of 37,000 million tonnes. The arithmetic gap is six orders of magnitude.

**The cost-curve extrapolation error.** Cost curves bend. Early-stage technology costs fall rapidly as initial deployment scales. As the technology matures, raw-material costs, manufacturing complexity, and grid-integration costs put a floor under further reductions. Solar PV's cost curve was unusually long; most technologies have shorter ones. Extrapolating an early cost curve indefinitely is a routine technical error.

**The energy-demand-of-the-solution problem.** Several proposed solutions are themselves substantial new energy demands. Direct air capture requires ~1.5-2 MWh of electricity per tonne of CO₂ captured. Scaled to meaningful gigatonnes of CO₂ would require terawatt-hours of new clean electricity that does not yet exist. Green hydrogen at scale requires terawatts of additional renewable capacity. AI data centres are already a substantial new electricity demand. Solutions that require enormous new energy supply have to engineer that supply alongside themselves.

**The AI-as-solution miscount.** AI training and inference consumed roughly 400-500 TWh in 2024 — comparable to France's total electricity consumption — and is the steepest-growing major electricity-demand category. The narrative that AI will solve climate has so far been false: AI is contributing more to electricity demand growth than to documented decarbonisation outcomes. This may change; it has not yet.

**The "we just need political will" formulation.** This is the rhetorical move that papers over the absence of engineering pathways. Political will, in the absence of supply chains, factories, skilled labour, transmission lines, and permits, accomplishes nothing. Where political will is the binding constraint (e.g., permitting reform), name it specifically.

**What the skepticism is not.** It is not an argument that renewables will fail (they are succeeding at electricity decarbonisation), that electrification of transport is impossible (it is happening), that efficiency cannot reduce demand (it routinely does), that R&D should be cut (the opposite). It is an argument that the time horizons, the scale gaps, and the physical constraints have been routinely under-counted by the techno-optimist narrative, and that strategic planning grounded in that narrative will fail.

## How to Apply
1. **For any "transformative technology" claim, ask: deployed at what scale today?** If the answer is laboratory or pilot, the claim is at least a decade from material impact.
2. **Ask the energy-demand-of-the-solution question.** What new energy supply does this solution require, and where will it come from?
3. **Distinguish the technical claim from the deployment claim.** "It's technically possible" is much weaker than "it can be deployed at scale by year X".
4. **Compare cost-curve extrapolations to historical analogues.** Most curves bend; the indefinite extrapolation should be the exception, not the assumption.
5. **Engage AI as a real new load.** A digital-economy strategy that assumes AI is climate-neutral has missed reality.

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor proposes funding a national green-hydrogen export industry as the "primary lever" of decarbonisation.
**Application:** Walk through the physics. Green hydrogen production requires electrolysers at scale, electricity at terawatt-hour volumes, desalination, ammonia synthesis (for export shipping), and offtake markets that do not yet exist. Each step has 5-10 years of deployment work ahead. As the "primary lever" it is not credible on a 5-10 year horizon. As one element of a longer-term portfolio, it may be defensible.
**Result:** The "primary lever" framing is replaced with a portfolio-of-bets framing that engages the actual technology readiness.

**Situation:** A consulting firm is asked to assess an "AI for climate" investment thesis.
**Application:** Identify the specific AI contribution claimed (grid optimisation, materials discovery, building efficiency, demand response). Quantify the documented impact. Compare to the AI workload's own electricity demand. Many "AI for climate" claims net out negative once the data-centre load is counted; some specific applications are genuinely net positive. The discipline is to do the arithmetic for the specific claim.
**Result:** Investment thesis is sharpened from a genre claim to a specific-application bet.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reject techno-optimism as a category.
**Why:** Several technologies celebrated by techno-optimists have in fact delivered (solar PV, batteries, EVs). The discipline is to apply skepticism to the specific claim, not the genre.

**Don't:** Confuse "currently far from scale" with "permanently impossible".
**Why:** Timescales matter. Many things deployable in 2050 are not deployable in 2030. The honest claim is about when and at what scale, not "yes/no".

**Don't:** Let skepticism prevent strategic experimentation.
**Why:** Decarbonisation requires multiple parallel bets, including on uncertain technologies. The point is to be honest about which bets are at what stage of maturity.
