# Smil Applied to African Development

## When to Use
- When a sub-Saharan or pan-African development strategy is being assessed.
- When "leapfrog" or "green growth" narratives are being applied to African contexts.
- When the question is the materials and energy base of African urbanisation.
- When grounding donor-funded African programmes in physical reality.

## Fails When
- **The question is genuinely a sub-sector or political-economy question.** Smil's framing is materials-and-energy first; institutional questions belong elsewhere.
- **You homogenise the continent.** South Africa, Nigeria, Ethiopia, Mauritius, the DRC have different trajectories and constraints. Aggregate-Africa statements often mislead.
- **You let the materialist argument become anti-development.** The argument is for honest planning of the materials base, not against the development that requires them.

## Core Concept
The Smil framing applied to African development produces a set of analytical questions that mainstream "African green growth" rhetoric routinely dodges.

**Africa is the continent with the fastest projected urban population growth.** The UN projects African urban population to roughly double between 2025 and 2050, adding roughly 800 million urban dwellers. This will require housing, water and sewerage, transport, schools, hospitals, electricity, and food at unprecedented scale.

**The materials implication.** Reaching even Middle-Income-Country levels of urban density and basic infrastructure for 800 million new urban Africans implies cement, steel, asphalt, and ammonia at volumes that current "green development" narratives do not engineer pathways for. China's urbanisation between 1980 and 2020 used roughly 60% of all the cement produced in the world during that period; the African urbanisation pipeline is smaller per capita (because density patterns differ) but still vast in absolute terms.

**The electricity implication.** Africa's electricity consumption per capita averages about 600 kWh per year, compared to roughly 5,000 kWh in middle-income countries and 12,000+ kWh in rich countries. Reaching even middle-income consumption levels for the African population would require roughly an 8-10× increase in continental electricity supply. The current installed generation capacity cannot deliver that increase even on a 30-year horizon under any plausible plan. South Africa is currently load-shedding; Nigeria's grid covers less than half its population.

**The energy mix question.** Most analysis assumes African electricity growth will be renewable-dominated, on the strength of solar resources and falling costs. The physics supports this for new generation in geographically suitable areas. The political-economy challenges are substantial: transmission, financing, gas-fired baseload for reliability, and the role of new Chinese-financed coal in some countries (Zimbabwe, Tanzania).

**The "leapfrog" question.** The leapfrog narrative — that African countries can skip the materials-intensive industrial phase and go directly to a service economy — is partially true and partially misleading. African telecommunications did leapfrog (mobile phones, mobile banking, no landline phase). African digital services in many sectors have similarly leapfrogged. Domains where leapfrog is genuine: telecommunications, digital banking, some forms of distributed energy. Domains where it is not: cement and steel for buildings, asphalt for roads, water reticulation, sewerage, ports, rail, electricity grid infrastructure. The four pillars are not leapfrog-able.

**The transport question.** African passenger-car ownership is rising rapidly from a low base. Electric passenger vehicles can absorb much of the growth if charging infrastructure is built; the technology and economics are increasingly favourable. African long-distance freight is dominated by diesel trucking on poor road infrastructure; the diesel will persist for the medium term.

**The food question.** African staple-crop yields per hectare are well below global averages, partly because synthetic nitrogen application rates are very low. The agronomy of doubling African cereal yields is well-established (the agricultural intensification programme of the African Green Revolution direction) and depends substantially on increasing fertiliser access. Smil would press: this is the highest-leverage agricultural intervention available for African food security.

**The geographically-specific transitions.** Smil's framework supports several specific African plays:
- Geographically advantaged renewables (Northern Cape solar, Moroccan and Sahara solar/wind, East African rift geothermal) — these are physically feasible.
- Domestic green industries built on locally-produced renewable electricity (green steel in places with cheap renewable electricity, green ammonia for fertiliser and export).
- Mobile-financial and digital-service leapfrogs.
- Urban renewable build-out where the grid can absorb it.

**The geographically-implausible plays:**
- National-scale energy transitions on European timelines.
- Heavy industry decarbonisation at speeds that exceed industrialised-country precedent.
- Universal substitution of synthetic nitrogen with organic methods.
- Net-zero national targets without engineering pathways.

## How to Apply
1. **For any African development plan, force the materials forecast.** Cement, steel, ammonia, electricity over the planning horizon.
2. **Distinguish leapfrog-able from non-leapfrog-able.** Apply the leapfrog narrative only where it actually applies.
3. **Engage Chinese supply-chain and financing roles directly.** They are dominant in African infrastructure.
4. **Use country-specific analysis, not continental averages.** South Africa, Nigeria, Kenya, Ethiopia, DRC have different trajectories.
5. **Frame transitions on multi-decade horizons.** African energy transitions on 5-10-year timelines are aspirational, not planning.

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor proposes a "green industrial policy" for an East African country, focused on green hydrogen and electric mobility, with a 2035 horizon.
**Application:** Green hydrogen requires terawatt-hours of dedicated renewable electricity that don't yet exist in the country. Electric mobility for passenger cars is feasible in major cities with charging investment; for inter-city freight it is not on the 2035 horizon. The "green industrial policy" should be re-scoped to: distributed solar deployment, urban EV charging infrastructure, agricultural fertiliser-access programme, mineral-processing value addition where geological resources support it. Not green hydrogen at scale.
**Result:** Programme has more credible deliverables and avoids the green-hydrogen overhype.

**Situation:** A South African municipality is developing a 2050 net-zero plan with consultants who are using a European template.
**Application:** Adapt the template to South African physical realities. Coal-dominated grid means municipal electricity emissions trajectory depends on Eskom's transition trajectory, which is structurally constrained. Urban renewable deployment is the local lever. Building-energy efficiency, transport modal shift, and waste management are within municipal control. Heavy industry in the metro is largely outside municipal control. Frame the plan to address what the municipality controls, not what the national grid does.
**Result:** Plan is implementable and honest about the institutional limits.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Apply Western net-zero timelines to African contexts.
**Why:** The starting point, the rates, the institutional capacity, and the materials demand all differ. The timelines need national-specific calibration.

**Don't:** Treat all African countries as one strategic context.
**Why:** Per-capita energy, urbanisation pace, geological endowment, institutional capacity vary by an order of magnitude across the continent.

**Don't:** Overstate the leapfrog story.
**Why:** Telecoms leapfrogged. Steel, cement, asphalt, sewerage, and electricity grid do not.
