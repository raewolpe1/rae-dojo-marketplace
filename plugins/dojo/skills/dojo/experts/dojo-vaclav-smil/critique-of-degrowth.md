# Critique of Degrowth

## When to Use
- When a strong degrowth narrative is being presented as a policy framework.
- When a development plan invokes degrowth as the basis for low-material-throughput growth.
- When teaching the materialist limits of pure post-growth positions.
- When balancing the techno-optimist critique with the degrowth critique.

## Fails When
- **The audience wants ideology, not physics.** Smil's critique is from physical reality, not from a growth-as-good position. It cuts both directions.
- **You collapse "degrowth", "post-growth", and "doughnut economics" into one position.** They are distinct, with different operational implications.
- **You over-state the critique.** Many degrowth proposals are sensible (less luxury consumption, less throughput in over-consuming economies). The critique applies to the strong universal claim, not the targeted one.

## Core Concept
Degrowth, in its strong form, argues that to remain within planetary boundaries the global economy must substantially shrink in material and energy throughput, even if that means contracting GDP. The position has academic respectability (Kallis, Hickel, D'Alisa, Demaria) and growing influence. It is distinct from "post-growth" (Tim Jackson, in your library) which is more agnostic on contraction and from "doughnut economics" (Kate Raworth) which is about staying between social and ecological boundaries rather than contraction per se.

The materialist critique applies to the strong degrowth claim:

**Ammonia.** Roughly half the human population is alive because of synthetic nitrogen fertiliser from the Haber-Bosch process. A meaningful contraction in global ammonia production would mean a contraction in global food output. The strong degrowth advocate must answer: by what fraction must global ammonia production fall, and what is the implied food consequence?

**Cement.** Modern cities require concrete for housing, water and sewerage, schools, hospitals, transport. A meaningful contraction in global cement production would mean stopping or substantially slowing urban construction. In the African and South Asian context, where urbanisation is in progress for hundreds of millions of people, a degrowth-driven cement contraction implies either no urbanisation at modern density, or no infrastructure to support the urbanisation that occurs. The strong degrowth advocate must answer: what is the implied housing, water, and infrastructure consequence in low- and middle-income countries?

**Steel.** Wind turbines, transmission lines, electric vehicles, rail, water pipes, agricultural machinery all require steel in large volumes. A meaningful contraction in steel production constrains the renewable energy build-out itself, as well as the basic infrastructure of low-income development.

**Plastics.** Modern medicine cannot function without plastics. A meaningful contraction in plastic production has direct health consequences, especially in resource-constrained health systems.

**Energy.** Global per-capita primary energy consumption ranges from about 200 GJ/year in rich countries to about 5-15 GJ/year in many low-income countries. A degrowth that universally reduces energy throughput collides with the legitimate aspiration of billions to higher (not lower) energy use. A degrowth that targets only rich-country consumption is more defensible but has different politics.

**The reasonable position.** Smil's critique is not that material throughput must rise indefinitely — that is the other error. It is that:

1. Rich countries can probably reduce their per-capita material throughput substantially through efficiency, substitution, and reduced luxury consumption, without compromising welfare. The doughnut economics direction.
2. Low- and middle-income countries will almost certainly need to increase their per-capita material throughput as they urbanise, electrify, and industrialise. This is morally legitimate and physically necessary.
3. The aggregate global picture is therefore one of relative decoupling (material throughput growing more slowly than GDP) and shifted composition (less luxury, more infrastructure), not of absolute contraction.
4. The four pillars (ammonia, plastics, steel, cement) cannot meaningfully contract without compromising food security, basic shelter, basic health, and basic infrastructure for billions of people.

This is a position that respects what degrowth gets right (over-consumption is real; throughput cannot grow forever; rich-country growth has been carbon-intensive) while refusing the over-claim that civilisation can simply contract.

## How to Apply
1. **Distinguish strong from weak degrowth.** Strong (universal contraction) faces the materialist objections; weak (selective reduction in rich-country luxury) does not.
2. **Identify whose material throughput is being asked to fall.** The politics differs sharply between rich-country reduction and global-South constraint.
3. **Engage the four pillars specifically.** Any degrowth proposal that does not address ammonia, plastics, steel, and cement specifically has not engaged the binding constraints.
4. **Acknowledge what is right.** Over-consumption in rich countries is real. Throughput cannot grow indefinitely. Some sectors should contract. Hold these alongside the materialist critique.
5. **Look for the convergent path.** Doughnut economics and efficient-materials-use development are more defensible than either pure growth or pure degrowth.

## Examples
**Situation:** An African government is being advised by an international NGO that the country should adopt a degrowth-aligned development path.
**Application:** Ask the advising NGO to specify the implied trajectories for cement, steel, ammonia, electricity, and per-capita material throughput in a country with rapid urbanisation and a young population. The conversation typically reveals that the degrowth framing is being applied where it was not designed — to a country that has not yet had the growth it would in principle de-grow from.
**Result:** The advice is reframed. The development pathway aims for efficiency and material discipline, not contraction.

**Situation:** A consulting firm is asked to compare a "doughnut economics" municipal plan with a conventional growth-led plan.
**Application:** Doughnut economics is more defensible than strong degrowth because it frames the goal as staying between social floor and ecological ceiling rather than universal contraction. For a Cape Town or Amsterdam, it can be operationalised. For a rapidly-urbanising sub-Saharan municipality, it requires careful adaptation because the social floor demands substantial material throughput growth.
**Result:** The municipal plan adopts a doughnut frame with explicit recognition that the social floor implies material throughput trajectories not currently met.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Collapse "degrowth", "post-growth", "doughnut economics", and "circular economy" into one critique.
**Why:** They are distinct positions with different physical implications. The critique applies most sharply to strong universal degrowth.

**Don't:** Mistake the materialist critique for a defence of unlimited growth.
**Why:** Smil is sharply critical of techno-optimism too. The position is empirical, not pro-growth.

**Don't:** Apply rich-country degrowth logic to low-income countries.
**Why:** The materialist case for substantially increasing low-income per-capita material throughput is strong. Universal degrowth is a category error in those contexts.
