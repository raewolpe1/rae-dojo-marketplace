# China's Energy Transition

## When to Use
- When global energy and emissions trajectories are being discussed.
- When a national or regional analysis needs to be situated in the dominant global context.
- When the question is whether renewables build-out is "working" globally.
- When teaching the central case in modern energy history.

## Fails When
- **The topic is genuinely sub-national or country-level outside China.** Smil's China framing is most useful for the global picture.
- **You treat China as monolithic.** Eastern coastal provinces, central provinces, and Western provinces have very different energy profiles.
- **You let the analysis become geopolitical rather than physical.** Smil's interest is the engineering and material reality, not the strategic competition.

## Core Concept
China is the centre of gravity of every serious story about global energy and materials in the 21st century. Any analysis that does not start with the Chinese numbers is decoration.

**The dominant facts:**

China is the world's largest energy consumer (about 30% of global primary energy). It is the world's largest coal producer and consumer (about 50% of global coal use). It is the world's largest CO₂ emitter (about 30% of global energy-related emissions). It is the world's largest renewable-energy installer (about half of global new solar and wind capacity in 2024). It is the world's largest manufacturer of solar PV, wind turbines, batteries, and electric vehicles. It is the world's largest steel producer (more than half of global output) and cement producer (more than half of global output). It built more cement in 2011-2013 than the United States built in the entire 20th century.

**The pattern of growth.** China's primary energy consumption grew roughly five-fold between 1990 and 2024, driven by the largest single industrialisation and urbanisation in human history. About 800 million people were lifted from extreme poverty in roughly four decades, with corresponding rises in per-capita energy and material consumption. The vast majority of this energy was supplied by coal, which remains the dominant primary energy source in China (about 55% of primary energy in 2024, down from 70% in 2010).

**The renewable build-out.** China deployed about 280 GW of new solar PV in 2024 alone — more than the rest of the world combined. Total Chinese installed solar capacity is approaching 900 GW. Wind installed capacity is over 500 GW. China dominates the supply chain for solar PV (more than 80% of global polysilicon and modules), batteries (over 70% of global cell production), and wind turbines (about half of global installations). This is the build-out that is making the rest of the world's energy transitions affordable.

**The coal paradox.** Despite the unprecedented renewable build-out, China's coal use has continued to grow in absolute terms through 2024. China approved more new coal-fired capacity in 2023 than in the previous seven years combined. The pattern is the additive transition writ large: renewables and coal both growing, with the renewables absorbing demand growth and providing flexibility but not displacing coal in absolute terms.

**The cement and steel reality.** Chinese cement production peaked around 2014 at about 2.5 billion tonnes per year (more than half of global production), and has fallen modestly since but remains the largest in the world by far. Steel production peaked around 2020 at about 1 billion tonnes (about 55% of global output). These materials industries are the largest single industrial CO₂ source in China. Decarbonising them is a binding constraint on China's national net-zero target (announced for 2060).

**The strategic question.** China's combination of the world's largest fossil-fuel infrastructure, the world's largest renewable build-out, and the world's largest manufacturing base for the renewable supply chain is what determines whether global decarbonisation is possible. Western policy choices matter; Chinese physical and industrial choices matter more.

**For African development specifically.** China is the dominant infrastructure financier and builder in Africa. The materials and energy footprint of Chinese-built African infrastructure (Belt and Road, dams, ports, rail, power stations) is a substantial fraction of African development materials demand. Strategic analysis of African energy and infrastructure cannot ignore the Chinese supply chain and financing.

## How to Apply
1. **For any global energy claim, start with the Chinese number.** What does China do or use, and what fraction of the global total is it?
2. **Distinguish manufacturing from deployment.** China manufactures most of the world's renewable equipment; deployment is global.
3. **Engage the coal contradiction directly.** It is real and large. It cannot be wished away.
4. **For African infrastructure analysis, engage the Chinese financing and supply role.** It is dominant.
5. **Treat the 2060 net-zero target as aspirational.** The materials and infrastructure base implies decadal transition rates, not the implied annual reduction the target requires.

## Examples
**Situation:** A Western think-tank publishes a report celebrating global solar PV installation reaching 600 GW per year in 2024 as evidence the transition is on track.
**Application:** Note that about half of this was Chinese installation. Then ask what happened to Chinese coal consumption over the same period (continued absolute increase). The global story is renewables building out spectacularly while coal continues to grow. The "on track" narrative requires that absolute fossil-fuel use start falling, which it has not in China.
**Result:** Report is reframed to engage absolute global emissions, not capacity additions alone.

**Situation:** An African government is negotiating Belt and Road infrastructure financing for a port and rail corridor.
**Application:** The infrastructure will be built by Chinese contractors using Chinese cement and steel (often imported), financed in renminbi or dollars. The energy and materials footprint of the project is substantial. Cost competitiveness comes from Chinese supply chain efficiency and Chinese state-backed financing terms. Local content, technology transfer, and long-term maintenance economics are the key negotiating dimensions, not whether to engage at all.
**Result:** Negotiation strategy acquires substantive grounding.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Discuss global decarbonisation without China.
**Why:** Any global story that does not engage 30% of global emissions and 30% of global energy use has missed the central fact.

**Don't:** Treat China's renewable build-out and China's coal continuation as contradictory.
**Why:** They are simultaneous facts, both real. The energy system grew on both.

**Don't:** Apply Western net-zero rhetoric to Chinese policy without translation.
**Why:** The 2060 target operates in a different political and material economy. The targets are real signals but mean different things in different institutional contexts.
