# Food Systems and Nitrogen

## When to Use
- When food strategy is being discussed at national or global scale.
- When organic, regenerative, or "natural" agriculture is being promoted as universally adoptable.
- When the question is whether the world can be fed without synthetic fertiliser.
- When teaching the chemistry of why modern food output exists.

## Fails When
- **The question is cuisine, not system.** Smil's domain is the engineering of feeding eight billion people, not what they eat or how it tastes.
- **You ignore the genuine downsides.** Synthetic nitrogen has caused real ecological damage (eutrophication of waterways, NOₓ emissions). The critique that we should use less is legitimate.
- **You homogenise agriculture.** Wet-rice paddy, dryland wheat, intensive vegetable horticulture, livestock systems are very different problems.

## Core Concept
**The single most consequential industrial process for human survival is the Haber-Bosch synthesis of ammonia from atmospheric nitrogen and natural-gas-derived hydrogen.** It was developed by Fritz Haber (Nobel 1918) and scaled by Carl Bosch (Nobel 1931) at BASF. Without it, perhaps four billion of the eight billion people currently alive would not be alive.

The chemistry: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, conducted at high temperature (~450°C) and high pressure (~200 atm) with an iron catalyst. The hydrogen comes almost entirely from natural gas via steam methane reforming. The process is energy-intensive: producing one tonne of ammonia requires about 30-40 GJ of energy, depending on plant efficiency. Global ammonia production in 2024 was about 180 million tonnes per year. The Haber-Bosch process is the single largest industrial consumer of natural gas in the world.

Roughly 80% of global ammonia goes to fertiliser (the rest to industrial chemistry — plastics, explosives, refrigeration, mining). Synthetic nitrogen now supplies about half of the nitrogen in human food crops globally, with the other half coming from biological nitrogen fixation (legumes), manure, and atmospheric deposition.

**Why this matters.** Before Haber-Bosch (commercialised in 1913), global agriculture was nitrogen-limited. Yields were a fraction of current levels. The combination of synthetic nitrogen, improved seed varieties (the Green Revolution from the 1940s onwards), and mechanisation has roughly tripled global cereal yield per hectare since 1960. Without synthetic nitrogen, current global food output is not possible at current land areas. To feed the current population on pre-Haber-Bosch yields would require roughly doubling the global cropland area, which is not available without massive deforestation.

**The organic question.** Organic agriculture, in the strict sense that excludes synthetic nitrogen, has yields that are typically 20-40% lower than conventional intensive agriculture, more variable, and more sensitive to weather. The yield gap varies by crop and context. Organic methods can certainly be made more productive than they currently are, and they have real environmental advantages (lower NOₓ runoff, healthier soil structure, biodiversity). But the claim that organic agriculture can feed the current global population at current land areas is not supported by the agronomy. The strongest defensible position is that organic and conventional should be deployed alongside one another, with intensive synthetic methods continuing to do the bulk of cereal production while organic methods expand where they make sense.

**The downsides of synthetic nitrogen, properly weighted.** About half of applied synthetic nitrogen does not end up in the plant — it is lost to volatilisation (NH₃ to atmosphere), denitrification (N₂O to atmosphere, a potent greenhouse gas), or leaching (NO₃ to groundwater and surface water). The result is widespread eutrophication of waterways (dead zones in coastal waters), atmospheric pollution, and significant greenhouse-gas emissions. Improving nitrogen-use efficiency (precision agriculture, slow-release fertilisers, better timing) can reduce these losses substantially. This is one of the highest-leverage agricultural interventions globally.

**Green ammonia.** Producing ammonia without fossil fuels (using green hydrogen from electrolysis powered by renewables) is technically possible. It is at pilot scale in 2024. The energy demand is enormous: replacing global ammonia production with green ammonia would require roughly 1,000 TWh of renewable electricity per year, dedicated, on top of all other renewable demand. Commercial deployment is decades away from the dominant route. In the meantime, ammonia is the largest single industrial CO₂ emitter in the chemistry sector.

**For development.** Africa is the continent with the lowest application rates of synthetic nitrogen and correspondingly the lowest cereal yields per hectare. The Alliance for a Green Revolution in Africa (AGRA) and related initiatives have been trying to raise fertiliser use to bring yields up. The trade-off — more synthetic nitrogen for higher yield versus less for environmental reasons — is one of the central agricultural-development questions.

## How to Apply
1. **For any food-system strategy, identify the nitrogen story.** Is the strategy presuming continuing synthetic nitrogen, displacing it, or constraining it?
2. **For organic-at-scale claims, ask about yield and land.** Lower yield implies more land at constant output. Where does the land come from?
3. **Distinguish nitrogen-use efficiency improvement (achievable, high-leverage) from nitrogen-source substitution (much harder).**
4. **For African development specifically, engage the fertiliser-access politics.** Synthetic nitrogen is widely under-applied in sub-Saharan Africa; the constraint is usually cost and distribution, not philosophy.
5. **Be honest about the downsides.** The case for using synthetic nitrogen more efficiently is overwhelming. The case for abolishing it is not.

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor proposes funding a national "regenerative agriculture" transition that would substantially reduce synthetic nitrogen application.
**Application:** Ask the yield-per-hectare projection. If yields are projected to hold, what is the agronomic mechanism (cover crops, legume rotations, manure availability)? If yields are projected to fall, what is the food-security plan? Most regenerative claims at national scale fail at the agronomy when pressed for the specifics.
**Result:** The programme acquires a more nuanced position — regenerative methods where they work, intensive methods elsewhere, plus a nitrogen-use-efficiency programme that yields environmental gains without yield loss.

**Situation:** A sub-Saharan African municipality is designing its agricultural-support programme. Synthetic fertiliser application is currently low.
**Application:** Raising fertiliser use is probably the highest-leverage intervention available for staple-crop yield. The constraint is rarely philosophical; it is cost, distribution, and timing. A subsidised, well-targeted fertiliser programme combined with extension support on application timing typically dominates organic-promotion programmes for yield outcomes.
**Result:** Programme prioritises fertiliser access and use-efficiency over substitution.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat organic agriculture as universally adoptable at scale.
**Why:** The yield gap is real and material. Organic can be expanded, but it cannot replace intensive synthetic-nitrogen agriculture at global scale without expanding cropland.

**Don't:** Dismiss the downsides of synthetic nitrogen.
**Why:** Eutrophication and N₂O emissions are serious. The case for nitrogen-use efficiency is overwhelming.

**Don't:** Treat "green ammonia" as a near-term substitute.
**Why:** Commercial-scale green ammonia is at pilot scale. The cost gap with grey ammonia is substantial and will not close on a five- or ten-year horizon at current rates.
