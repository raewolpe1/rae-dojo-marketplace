# Why Energy Transitions Are Slow

## When to Use
- When a plan or projection assumes a transition pace with no historical analogue.
- When a forecast extrapolates the current solar / wind cost curve to 2030 or 2050.
- When the question is whether "this time is different" for energy.
- When teaching the base rates of historical energy transitions.

## Fails When
- **The audience wants moral urgency, not historical analysis.** Smil's point is descriptive, not normative — it does not argue against ambition, it argues for honesty about what ambition requires.
- **You confuse share-shift with absolute transition.** Renewables share rising while fossil fuels rise in absolute terms is not the transition the slogans imply.
- **You treat one country's case as global.** Denmark, Norway, Iceland are not the world. National decarbonisation success can coexist with global decarbonisation failure.

## Core Concept
Historical energy transitions have taken between 50 and 100 years to globally shift the dominant primary energy source. Wood dominated for essentially all of human history. Coal began significant commercial use in Britain in the seventeenth century but did not overtake biomass globally until roughly the First World War — a transition of about two and a half centuries from first commercial exploitation, or roughly one century from take-off. Oil began significant rise after the Drake well in 1859 and did not overtake coal as the world's largest primary energy source until about 1965 — about a century. Natural gas began commercial exploitation in the late nineteenth century and has still not overtaken coal globally; coal regained the top position around 2010.

Three structural features of past transitions explain the timescale:

**First, transitions have been additive.** New energy sources were added on top of the old. Wood is still burned in 2026 — about 1 billion people use it as a primary cooking fuel. Coal use is at or near all-time highs globally. Oil use is at all-time highs. Natural gas use is at all-time highs. There has never been a global energy transition that subtracted; previous transitions only shifted the relative shares while every absolute volume grew.

**Second, infrastructure lifetimes are long.** A coal-fired power plant has a 40-year design life. Oil refineries: 30-50 years. Pipelines, ports, distribution networks: longer still. The installed base of fossil-fuel infrastructure built between 1990 and 2020 implies fossil-fuel use for decades after, unless scrapped at large financial loss.

**Third, the engineering scale-up is slow.** A new energy technology must move from laboratory to demonstration to commercial deployment to mass scale. Each step typically takes a decade. Nuclear power went from first commercial reactor (1956) to its peak share of global electricity (about 17% in 1996), and has since declined. Wind power went from first commercial utility-scale turbines in the mid-1980s to about 8% of global electricity in 2024. Solar PV went from first commercial deployment in the late 1970s to about 6% of global electricity in 2024. These are not slow rates in absolute terms — they are at the upper end of what energy systems have historically achieved.

The current decarbonisation aspiration is for global net-zero by 2050. That implies replacing essentially all fossil-fuel-based primary energy — about 80% of the current mix — within roughly 25 years from a 2025 starting point. There is no historical precedent for that pace. It is not impossible. But it is being asked of a system that has historically been slow.

What might be different this time:
- Policy push (carbon pricing, mandates, IRA-style subsidies) is more deliberate than in past transitions.
- The technology cost curves (solar PV especially) have fallen faster than most models predicted.
- Electricity-as-final-energy enables electrification of sectors previously fuel-bound.

What is not different this time:
- The infrastructure replacement rate is constrained by capital cycle, permitting, and skilled labour.
- The energy-density and power-density gaps with fossil fuels are physics.
- The materials demand for the transition (copper, lithium, rare earths, plus steel and cement for infrastructure) is enormous.

## How to Apply
1. **Anchor every transition projection to a historical analogue.** What previous transition is being claimed as the comparable, and over what period?
2. **Distinguish absolute from relative.** Renewables share of mix can rise while absolute fossil-fuel use rises. Both can be true.
3. **Count the infrastructure replacement rate, not the new build alone.** Adding 200 GW of renewables per year does not retire fossil-fuel plant.
4. **Identify the binding constraint.** Often it is permitting, transmission, or skilled labour — not technology cost.
5. **Be specific about which sector you are claiming for.** Power-sector decarbonisation is plausible at speed; industrial-heat decarbonisation is much slower; transport varies by mode.

## Examples
**Situation:** A national energy plan projects 80% renewable electricity by 2035 starting from 30% in 2025.
**Application:** That is an annual share gain of 5 percentage points sustained for 10 years. The fastest comparable historical rate is the post-2000 European wind/solar build-out, which achieved about 2-3 percentage points per year in the strongest cases. The plan implies roughly double the fastest historical rate, in a country with grid and permitting constraints. Either there is a specific reason this country can exceed historical maxima, or the plan is aspirational.
**Result:** The number gets challenged. The plan either acquires specific build-rate evidence or is corrected.

**Situation:** A consulting firm is asked to assess whether South Africa can decarbonise its electricity by 2040.
**Application:** Start from the fossil-fuel asset base — Eskom's coal fleet, average remaining design life, the renewable build rate over the past five years, the transmission constraints, the political economy of Mpumalanga. The question is not whether decarbonisation is desirable; it is whether the historical and current rates support the implied trajectory. The honest answer for most national cases is: no, not at the implied speed.
**Result:** The strategy gets framed as a transition trajectory with realistic milestones, not as a target.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Extrapolate the past decade's renewable growth to net-zero by 2050.
**Why:** Linear extrapolation of recent growth ignores the bending of cost curves, infrastructure constraints, and the harder-to-abate sectors at the end of the decarbonisation queue.

**Don't:** Treat percentage-of-installed-capacity as percentage-of-energy-delivered.
**Why:** Capacity factor differences mean 30% renewable capacity delivers roughly 8-12% of generation. The distinction is routinely missed.

**Don't:** Mistake one fast country for a global trend.
**Why:** Norway's hydropower, Iceland's geothermal, Denmark's wind — these are real achievements made possible by specific natural endowments. They do not scale globally.
