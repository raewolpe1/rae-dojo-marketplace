# The Decarbonisation Paradox

## When to Use
- When renewable build-out figures are being celebrated as the transition.
- When net-zero targets are being treated as imminent rather than aspirational.
- When the global picture is being missed in country-level success stories.
- When teaching the gap between share-shift and absolute change.

## Fails When
- **The question is about a single country with genuine absolute reductions.** UK, Denmark, and parts of the EU have achieved absolute emissions reductions; the global picture is different.
- **You frame the paradox as an argument against renewables.** The point is that renewables are necessary but currently additive, not subtractive. They are not enough on their own, but they are not the problem either.
- **You let it become defeatism.** The paradox is a description of what has happened. It is not an argument against trying.

## Core Concept
The decarbonisation paradox: renewables capacity has grown faster than most forecasts predicted; absolute global fossil-fuel use has continued to grow nearly every year, with brief recession-related pauses; absolute global CO₂ emissions in 2024 were at all-time highs.

The numbers (approximate, 2024):
- Global primary energy consumption: ~620 EJ
- Fossil fuels (coal + oil + natural gas) share: ~80%
- Renewables (wind, solar, hydro, biomass, geothermal) share: ~15-17%
- Nuclear: ~4%
- Global CO₂ emissions from energy: ~37 Gt, an all-time high

Wind and solar generation grew at compound rates of 15-25% per year over 2010-2024. Globally installed solar PV capacity went from 40 GW in 2010 to over 1,500 GW in 2024 — a 35-fold increase. Wind from 200 GW to over 1,100 GW. These are remarkable absolute and relative increases.

And yet:
- Coal use globally was at or near all-time highs through 2024, driven primarily by Asia.
- Oil use was at all-time highs.
- Natural gas use was at all-time highs.
- Global CO₂ emissions from energy use were at all-time highs.

**Why?** Three reasons:

**First, total energy demand is growing.** Rising populations and rising per-capita energy use (especially in India, South-East Asia, parts of Africa, parts of the Middle East) mean total primary energy demand has grown by roughly 1-2% per year. Renewables have absorbed much of this growth without displacing existing fossil-fuel use.

**Second, additive transitions are historically the norm.** As covered in *why-energy-transitions-are-slow*, no historical energy transition has subtracted in absolute terms. The current one is being asked to. So far it has not.

**Third, the harder sectors haven't moved.** Power-sector decarbonisation is the easiest. Industrial heat, transport (especially aviation and shipping), and petrochemical feedstocks have barely moved. As power decarbonises in some countries, these sectors continue to consume fossil fuels at high rates.

**The implication for strategy.** The paradox does not invalidate the transition — it characterises where we are. A serious decarbonisation strategy must:
1. Be honest about absolute emissions, not just share of mix.
2. Address total energy demand, not just supply mix.
3. Address the harder sectors (industry, transport, feedstocks), not just power.
4. Engage the materials base of the transition itself (the renewables build requires copper, lithium, steel, cement, rare earths).

For a development consultant: this is the central diagnostic. Strategies that celebrate renewable build-out without engaging absolute emissions are misreading the situation. Strategies that engage absolute emissions can be honest about what is required.

## How to Apply
1. **Always cite absolute alongside relative.** Renewable share rising and renewable TWh rising say different things; both matter.
2. **Compare to total demand growth, not to total supply.** Is the new renewable capacity covering demand growth or displacing existing fossil-fuel supply?
3. **Disaggregate by sector.** Power, industrial heat, transport, feedstocks, buildings — different stories.
4. **Take a multi-decade view.** Annual fluctuations matter less than the decadal trend in absolute emissions.
5. **Distinguish national from global.** UK emissions are falling; Indian emissions are rising. The global is the sum.

## Examples
**Situation:** A national strategy document celebrates a doubling of renewable capacity over five years.
**Application:** Ask: what happened to total electricity demand over the same period? What happened to absolute fossil-fuel-based generation? If the doubling of renewables was absorbed by growing demand while fossil-fuel generation stayed flat or grew, the strategy has not decarbonised — it has grown two energy sources side by side. Reframe.
**Result:** The strategy acquires honest absolute baselines and recognises which sectors have actually moved.

**Situation:** A donor announces "$10 billion to accelerate Africa's energy transition" with renewable build-out as the headline.
**Application:** The framing presumes there is a transition to accelerate. In most African contexts, there is rising fossil-fuel use, especially for industrialisation, transport, and electrification. Renewable build-out is occurring but from a low base and not displacing existing demand growth. Honest framing: "renewable build-out in a context of rising total energy demand". Different conversation, different programme design.
**Result:** Programme acquires more realistic theory of change.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use the paradox as ammunition against renewables.
**Why:** Renewables remain a necessary part of any plausible decarbonisation. The paradox identifies what they are not currently doing (subtracting) and what additional interventions are needed.

**Don't:** Treat it as inevitable that the paradox continues.
**Why:** The paradox is a description of the last fifteen years. The next fifteen may differ. The point is to engage the actual numbers, not to assume any particular future.

**Don't:** Lose sight of the harder sectors.
**Why:** Even if power-sector decarbonisation accelerates, industry, transport, and feedstocks dominate the remaining problem. They are the strategic priority.
