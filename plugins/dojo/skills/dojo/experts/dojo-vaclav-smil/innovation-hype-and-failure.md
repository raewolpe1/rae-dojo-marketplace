# Innovation, Hype, and Failure

## When to Use
- When a breakthrough technology is being treated as imminent.
- When an investor or donor is being asked to back a transformative-tech thesis.
- When the question is which technologies in the current hype cycle will actually deliver.
- When teaching the long historical record of innovation that did not deliver as promised.

## Fails When
- **The technology has already deployed at industrial scale.** Solar PV is no longer a hype story. Lithium-ion batteries are no longer a hype story. Skepticism that applied at the demonstration stage does not apply at the deployed stage.
- **You become reflexively skeptical.** Smil is empirical, not nihilistic. Some technologies deliver; the discipline is to engage the specific evidence.
- **You confuse "decades to deploy" with "will never work".** Many things will work, eventually, just not on the promised timeline.

## Core Concept
Innovation's track record is far more humble than the popular story suggests. Smil's *Invention and Innovation: A Brief History of Hype and Failure* (2023) catalogues the long history of technologies that were promised, hyped, sometimes commercialised, and never delivered at scale, or delivered far later than promised, or delivered with consequences that turned out to be net negative.

A partial catalogue from Smil's body of work:

**Technologies that delivered but with long delay or modest scale:**
- Nuclear power. Predicted in the 1950s to provide "electricity too cheap to meter" within decades. Reached its peak share of global electricity (~17%) in 1996 and has since declined as the older fleet retires faster than new build.
- Battery-electric vehicles. Promised as transformative in the 1990s and again in the 2000s; took until the 2020s to reach meaningful market share, and only with massive Chinese manufacturing investment.
- Solar PV. Was a fringe technology for decades after its 1954 invention. Took 50+ years to become commercially competitive at scale.
- Direct fuel cells. Promised since the 1960s. Niche applications only.

**Technologies that did not deliver despite sustained promise:**
- Supersonic civilian flight beyond Concorde. Repeatedly predicted; Concorde retired in 2003; no successor in service.
- Fast breeder reactors. Promised as the energy source of the future since the 1950s. Multiple national programmes; no commercial fleet.
- Nuclear fusion. Promised as "30 years away" continuously since the 1950s. ITER is under construction but commercial fusion is not in sight.
- Carbon capture and storage at scale. Demonstration projects since the 1990s; global capture in 2024 about 0.05 GtCO₂/year against emissions of 37 GtCO₂.
- Hydrogen economy at consumer scale. Promised at intervals since the 1970s. Not delivered.

**Innovations that delivered but turned out badly:**
- Leaded petrol. Innovation of the 1920s, banned globally over many decades because of its neurotoxic effects, with massive cumulative population harm.
- CFC refrigerants. Innovation of the 1930s, banned after the ozone-layer damage was identified in the 1970s-80s.
- Various pharmaceuticals. Thalidomide, Vioxx, etc.
- DDT. Initial agricultural triumph; later ecological catastrophe.

**Innovations whose impact has been mainly distributional rather than productive:**
- Many financial innovations of the 1990s-2000s.
- Some categories of social media.
- Some categories of the gig economy.

**What this catalogue is for.** It is not a counsel of innovation pessimism. It is a calibration device. The popular story emphasises innovations that worked (the iPhone, the internet, mRNA vaccines). The catalogue emphasises the much larger set of innovations that did not. Honest expected-value calculation requires both.

**The pattern.** Innovations that look "transformative" in their demonstration phase have a high failure rate at the deployed-industry transition. Innovations that look modest at demonstration sometimes turn out transformative (the integrated circuit was modest at first; the steam engine took a century to become a transport revolution). The implication is that the deployment trajectory matters more than the demonstration excitement.

**The cost-curve question.** Many innovation narratives extrapolate an early cost curve indefinitely. Solar PV's cost curve was unusually long because the underlying physics was favourable and the manufacturing scale-up was sustained. Most cost curves bend earlier — raw-material costs, manufacturing complexity, regulatory friction, and grid integration impose floors. The blanket use of "Wright's Law" as a forecasting principle is unjustified.

## How to Apply
1. **For any innovation claim, ask: deployed at what scale today?** If pilot or demonstration, the gap to industry is typically a decade or more.
2. **Reference the catalogue.** What other innovation has made a similar promise and what was the outcome?
3. **Check the cost-curve extrapolation.** Is the proposed trajectory consistent with the curve having bent at the appropriate point, or does it assume indefinite continuation?
4. **Engage the unintended-consequences question.** Some innovations succeed in deployment but cause new problems. The honest analysis includes this risk.
5. **Hold uncertainty asymmetrically.** Innovations rarely arrive on the promised timeline; they sometimes arrive earlier than expected when underlying conditions align. Plan for both.

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor is being pitched fusion-energy funding as part of a climate strategy with a 2040 horizon.
**Application:** Fusion has been "30 years away" since the 1950s. ITER's first plasma is now expected in the 2030s, with commercial fusion (if ever) decades after. Fusion is not a 2040 climate solution. It may be a 2060+ solution. Reframe the funding case: research investment with very long horizons, not climate-strategy investment.
**Result:** Funding decision is made with honest time horizons.

**Situation:** A regional government is being asked to commit to a "transformative" green-hydrogen export strategy by 2035 with major subsidies.
**Application:** Green hydrogen exists at pilot scale. Commercial-scale green ammonia (the export carrier) is rare. The cost gap with grey hydrogen is large. Offtake markets at meaningful scale do not yet exist. A 2035 transformative export industry would require deployment rates faster than any previous fuel industry has achieved. Treat as a long-horizon option rather than a near-term industrial strategy.
**Result:** Regional commitments are scaled to what is actually buildable.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Generalise from the iPhone / internet / mRNA exceptions.
**Why:** They are exceptions. The catalogue of partial failures and outright failures is much longer.

**Don't:** Treat hype as evidence.
**Why:** Hype tells you about belief, not about deployment. The two are routinely disconnected.

**Don't:** Become a permanent skeptic.
**Why:** Some technologies do deliver. The discipline is to engage the specific deployment evidence for the specific technology, not to refuse the genre.
