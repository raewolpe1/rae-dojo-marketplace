# RAIN Practice

## When to Use
- When someone is caught in a difficult emotion — anxiety, shame, anger, grief — and needs a practice to work with it.
- When a structured, repeatable method for meeting difficulty is needed.
- When someone needs to move from reactive to responsive.

## Core Concept
RAIN is Brach's signature practice — four steps for meeting any difficult experience with mindfulness and compassion. Originally adapted from the Buddhist teacher Michele McDonald and refined by Brach over decades of clinical and teaching work, RAIN has become one of the most widely taught mindfulness practices in the West. In Brach's updated version (from her 2020 book Radical Compassion), the N stands for Nurture rather than the earlier "Non-identification," reflecting her growing emphasis on the active role of self-compassion.

**Recognize** means simply noticing what is happening. Not analysing, not judging, not fixing — just noticing. "I'm feeling anxious." "There's anger here." "My chest is tight." Recognition is the moment you step out of autopilot and become aware of your experience. Without it, reactivity runs the show.

**Allow** means letting the experience be there without trying to change it. This is harder than it sounds. The habitual response to difficulty is to resist it, suppress it, distract from it, or fix it. Allowing means none of that. It means consenting to the presence of the experience — "This is here, and it's allowed to be here." Allow does not mean approve. It means you stop fighting what is already present.

**Investigate** means turning toward the experience with gentle curiosity. "What is this feeling in my body? What does it need? What is it afraid of? What is the belief underneath this emotion?" Investigation in RAIN is not analytical — it is intimate. It is not the mind interrogating the feeling; it is awareness touching the feeling with kindness. The questions are felt, not solved. The body is the primary site of investigation, not the intellect.

**Nurture** means actively offering yourself care. This is the step Brach added to the original practice, and she considers it the most important. After recognising, allowing, and investigating, you place your hand on your heart and offer yourself the kindness you would give a dear friend. "It's okay." "I'm here with you." "You're not alone." Nurture is not an afterthought — it is the step where healing happens, because it is the step where you receive your own compassion rather than only extending it outward.

After RAIN, Brach describes a quality of resting in awareness — what she sometimes calls "After the RAIN" — where you simply notice what remains when you have met your experience with full presence and compassion. Often what remains is a sense of openness, spaciousness, or quiet that was not accessible when you were caught in reactivity.

## How to Apply
1. **Teach the steps sequentially.** Don't present RAIN as a list — walk through each step with enough time for it to be felt.
2. **Emphasise the body in the Investigate step.** "Where do you feel this in your body?" is more productive than "Why do you think you feel this?"
3. **Model the Nurture step.** Many people have never offered themselves deliberate kindness. They may need permission and a model.
4. **Don't treat RAIN as a fix.** The purpose is not to make the difficult feeling go away. It is to change your relationship to it.

## Examples
**Situation:** A project coordinator says "I'm so frustrated with my team — they keep missing deadlines and I'm about to lose it. I know I shouldn't feel this way but I can't help it."
**Application:** Brach would say: "Let's slow down and walk through this together. First — Recognize. You just did that: 'I'm frustrated.' Good. You've named it. Now notice the second thing you said: 'I shouldn't feel this way.' That's resistance — it's telling you your frustration is wrong. So you're carrying two burdens: the frustration itself, and the judgment about the frustration. Let's work with both. Allow. Can you let the frustration be here, just for this moment, without judging it? It doesn't mean you approve of the situation. It means you consent to the feeling being present. 'Frustration is here. It's allowed.' Now, Investigate — but gently. Where do you feel this frustration in your body? Your jaw? Your shoulders? Your chest? What does it feel like — tight? hot? clenched? Stay with the sensation. And ask quietly: 'What does this feeling need right now?' Not 'what does the project need' — what does the feeling need? Finally, Nurture. Put your hand on your chest. Say to yourself: 'It's hard to care about work and feel like you're carrying it alone. I see how much you care.' Stay with that for a moment. This is RAIN. You haven't fixed the deadline problem — but you've freed yourself from the reactive cycle, and from that place you can respond to the team with clarity rather than eruption."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Skip Nurture.
**Why:** Nurture is the step most people omit, and it is the one that makes RAIN transformative rather than merely analytical. Without Nurture, RAIN becomes cold observation.

**Don't:** Use RAIN to get rid of feelings.
**Why:** If your intention in practising RAIN is to make the feeling go away, you are not allowing — you are manipulating. RAIN works precisely because it is not trying to fix anything.
