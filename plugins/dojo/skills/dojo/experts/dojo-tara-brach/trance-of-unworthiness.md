# Trance of Unworthiness

## When to Use
- When someone operates from a deep, often unconscious belief that they are fundamentally inadequate.
- When imposter syndrome, perfectionism, or self-doubt is the presenting issue but the root is deeper.
- When someone's relationship with their own worth needs attention.

## Core Concept
The trance of unworthiness is Brach's term for the pervasive, often unconscious belief that "something is wrong with me." It is a trance because, like a hypnotic state, the person inside it does not see the trance itself — they see reality through it. Everything confirms the story: the criticism from a colleague proves inadequacy, the success was luck, the praise was politeness, the failure was inevitable. The trance filters experience so thoroughly that the person does not question the filter — they question themselves.

Brach traces the trance to the universal human experience of separation and judgment. In early life, we learn that certain parts of ourselves are acceptable and others are not. The parts deemed unacceptable — our neediness, our anger, our vulnerability, our desires — get pushed underground. And we develop a compensating story: "If I am good enough, productive enough, selfless enough, I will earn my right to belong." This story becomes so familiar that it feels like truth rather than narrative.

The trance manifests in characteristic patterns: perfectionism (if I do everything perfectly, I'll finally be enough), people-pleasing (if I keep everyone happy, I'll be worthy of love), overworking (if I produce enough, I'll justify my existence), avoidance (if I don't try, I can't fail and expose my inadequacy), and comparison (measuring yourself against others to determine your worth). These patterns are strategies for managing the underlying conviction of unworthiness. They work well enough that the person rarely questions them — until they stop working, which usually happens when the person is overwhelmed, depleted, or encountering a challenge that the old strategies cannot manage.

Waking from the trance is not a matter of positive affirmation or logical argument. You cannot think your way out of a trance. Brach's approach is to first recognise the trance — to see that "I'm not good enough" is a story, not a fact. Then to bring radical acceptance to the experience of unworthiness itself — to meet the pain of believing you are inadequate with compassion rather than trying to fix it. The trance dissolves not when you prove your worth but when you stop requiring proof.

## How to Apply
1. **Name it as a trance.** The word itself is powerful — it implies that the experience of unworthiness is a state you've entered, not a truth you've discovered.
2. **Trace the patterns.** Help the person see how perfectionism, people-pleasing, or overworking are strategies for managing unworthiness, not evidence of it.
3. **Meet it with RAIN.** Recognize the trance, Allow the pain of it, Investigate where it lives in the body, Nurture yourself with the kindness you've been withholding.
4. **Don't argue with the trance.** Logic does not dissolve trances. Presence does.

## Examples
**Situation:** A senior evaluator says "No matter how many successful evaluations I deliver, I still feel like I'm about to be found out. I don't understand why I can't shake it."
**Application:** Brach would say: "You can't shake it because you're trying to use achievement to cure a wound that achievement didn't cause. What you're describing is what I call the trance of unworthiness — the deep belief that you are not enough, and that if people really saw you, they would know. This trance doesn't respond to evidence. You've delivered successful evaluations — many of them — and the trance has a response to every one: 'That one was easy.' 'I got lucky.' 'They don't know what they don't know.' It explains away every success and amplifies every doubt. The way out is not more achievement. The way out is recognising the trance as a trance — as a story you learned, not a truth you discovered. When did you first learn that you had to earn your right to be here? Who taught you that? The trance was probably adaptive once — it kept you working hard, staying vigilant, performing. But it has long outlived its usefulness, and now it's costing you the ability to enjoy what you've built. The practice is not proving your worth — it's questioning the premise that your worth needs proof."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** List the person's achievements to prove they are worthy.
**Why:** This is exactly what the trance expects and deflects. The trance is not a lack of evidence — it is a filter that distorts all evidence.

**Don't:** Treat unworthiness as a character flaw.
**Why:** Treating unworthiness as something wrong with the person adds another layer to the trance. The trance of unworthiness is itself met with compassion, not correction.
