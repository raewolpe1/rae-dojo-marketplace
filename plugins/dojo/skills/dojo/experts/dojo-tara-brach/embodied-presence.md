# Embodied Presence

## When to Use
- When someone is intellectualising their experience — understanding everything cognitively but feeling nothing.
- When the body as the primary site of emotional experience needs emphasis.
- When disconnection from physical sensations is preventing deeper self-understanding.

## Core Concept
Brach consistently returns to the body as the doorway to presence. The mind travels — it replays the past, rehearses the future, constructs stories, analyses endlessly. The body does not travel. It is always here, always now. When you feel the weight of your body in the chair, the temperature of the air on your skin, the movement of your breath — you are present. This is not metaphor. It is physiology: bodily sensation is a direct experience of the present moment.

Emotions, in Brach's understanding, are not primarily cognitive events — they are bodily events. Anger is not just the thought "this is unfair"; it is heat in the face, tension in the jaw, clenching in the fists. Grief is not just the thought "I've lost something"; it is heaviness in the chest, pressure behind the eyes, a hollow feeling in the stomach. Fear is not just "something bad might happen"; it is constriction in the throat, butterflies in the belly, a surge of adrenaline. When you attend to the body, you attend to the emotion itself, not the story about the emotion.

Many professionals, particularly in analytical fields, have developed sophisticated minds and neglected bodies. They can articulate exactly why they feel the way they feel — they can trace the causes, identify the patterns, name the dynamics. But this intellectual understanding has not changed anything, because understanding happens in the mind, and the emotion lives in the body. The gap between "knowing" and "feeling" is the gap between mind and body. Brach's practice closes that gap by directing attention downward — out of the head and into the body.

The Investigate step of RAIN is primarily a bodily investigation. "Where do you feel this in your body?" is the foundational question. Not "What do you think about this?" but "What do you feel?" The body holds what the mind has been unable or unwilling to process. When attention meets sensation with kindness, experiences that have been stuck begin to move.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask "Where do you feel this in your body?"** This is the single most important embodied-presence question.
2. **Slow down.** Bodily awareness requires slowing the pace of conversation. Pause. Let the person actually feel rather than describe.
3. **Name sensations, not emotions.** "Tightness," "heat," "heaviness," "hollowness" — sensation words are more precise and more present than emotion labels.
4. **Be sensitive to body-related difficulty.** People with trauma histories, chronic pain, or body image difficulties may find body-directed attention distressing. Go slowly and respect boundaries.

## Examples
**Situation:** A team leader says "I know I should be grateful for this opportunity but I just feel — I don't know — something. I can't name it."
**Application:** Brach would say: "Let's not try to name it with the mind. Let's find it in the body instead. Right now, close your eyes if that feels comfortable. Take a slow breath. And scan through your body — start at your feet and move upward. As you move through, notice: is there any area that feels different? Tight? Heavy? Constricted? Open? Take your time. When you find a sensation that seems connected to this unnamed feeling, stay with it. Don't try to interpret it — just feel it. What is the quality of the sensation? Is it dense or diffuse? Warm or cool? Moving or still? This is your body's way of knowing something your mind hasn't caught up with yet. When we say 'I feel something but I can't name it,' we're saying the body knows before the mind has words. The practice is to let the body speak in its own language — sensation — and trust that understanding will follow, on its own schedule."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Force body awareness on someone who isn't ready.
**Why:** For people with trauma, body awareness can be triggering. Always offer it as an invitation, not a directive, and respect a "no."

**Don't:** Treat the body as wiser than the mind.
**Why:** Body and mind are partners, not competitors. The body provides information the mind may miss, and vice versa. Neither is superior.
