# Grief and Loss

## When to Use
- When someone is experiencing loss — death, ending of a role, project cancellation, transition, retirement, relationship ending.
- When grief is being suppressed, rushed, or intellectualised.
- When someone needs permission to grieve rather than strategies to overcome grief.

## Core Concept
Brach teaches that grief is not a problem to be solved but a natural response to loss that must be met with presence. The dominant cultural narrative around grief is one of stages, timelines, and resolution — the expectation that grief follows a predictable path and arrives at an endpoint called "closure." Brach challenges this. Grief is not linear, not predictable, and rarely arrives at the neat resolution that closure implies. It comes in waves — sometimes overwhelming, sometimes gentle, sometimes absent for long stretches before returning with full force.

The mindful approach to grief has three elements. First, allowing the grief to be present without trying to fix, accelerate, or transcend it. This means not telling yourself "I should be over this by now" or "I need to be strong." Grief is not weakness. It is the measure of what mattered. Second, staying embodied — feeling the grief in the body rather than only thinking about the loss. Grief lives in the body as heaviness, hollowness, fatigue, physical aching. When you meet grief somatically rather than cognitively, you are actually processing it rather than thinking about processing it. Third, holding grief with compassion — treating your own grieving self with the tenderness you would offer a friend in pain.

Professional contexts often have no room for grief. Projects end, funding stops, colleagues leave, organisational structures change — and the expectation is to move forward efficiently. But unprocessed grief accumulates. The cynicism that many long-career professionals develop is often frozen grief — the result of years of losses that were never acknowledged or mourned. Burnout frequently has a grief dimension that goes unrecognised because the culture does not legitimise professional grief.

## How to Apply
1. **Create space for the grief.** Simply acknowledging that loss has occurred and that grief is appropriate is sometimes the most important intervention.
2. **Don't fix it.** Resist the urge to offer solutions, silver linings, or meaning-making. Grief needs witness, not wisdom.
3. **Return to the body.** "Where does the grief live in your body right now?" is more useful than "Tell me about the loss."
4. **Normalise the waves.** "Grief comes and goes. A good day doesn't mean it's over. A hard day doesn't mean you're going backward."

## Examples
**Situation:** A long-serving programme officer says "They restructured the department and my programme no longer exists. Twenty years of work, gone. Everyone says 'new opportunities' but I just feel empty."
**Application:** Brach would say: "The people saying 'new opportunities' mean well, and they're not wrong — there may be new opportunities ahead. But right now, that's not what you need to hear, because it skips over something essential: you are grieving. Twenty years of work — relationships built, communities served, meaning made — has ended. That is a loss, and it deserves to be mourned. The emptiness you feel is grief, not failure. It is the measure of how much this work mattered to you. Right now, your only job is to be present with that emptiness. Don't rush to fill it. Don't force yourself to be excited about what's next. Let the grief be here. Feel it in your body — the heaviness, the hollowness. It is telling you something true: this mattered, and it's gone, and that hurts. Over time, the grief will make room for what comes next. But it will do that on its own schedule, not yours and not your organisation's. The kindest thing you can do for yourself right now is to stop pressuring yourself to move forward and instead be here — with the loss, with the emptiness, with the grief of a chapter that has ended."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Offer meaning prematurely.
**Why:** "This happened for a reason" is spiritual bypassing. Maybe meaning will emerge later. Right now, meaning is not what grief needs. Presence is.

**Don't:** Set timelines for grief.
**Why:** "You should be feeling better by now" is one of the most damaging things you can say to a grieving person. Grief takes as long as it takes.
