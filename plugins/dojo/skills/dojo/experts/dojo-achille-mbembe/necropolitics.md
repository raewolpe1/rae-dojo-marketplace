# Necropolitics

## When to Use
- When analysing contexts where sovereign power operates through the management of death rather than the management of life — conflict zones, abandoned populations, extractive frontiers.
- When examining who is protected and who is expendable in a given political arrangement.
- When Foucault's biopower (making live, letting die) is insufficient because the situation involves active production of death or systematic abandonment.

## Core Concept
Necropolitics is Mbembe's extension and correction of Foucault's concept of biopower. Foucault theorised biopower as the sovereign capacity to "make live and let die" — to manage populations through the enhancement of life. Mbembe's intervention begins from a simple observation: in much of the postcolonial world, biopower's promise to "make live" was never fulfilled. For populations subjected to slavery, colonialism, apartheid, and contemporary structural abandonment, sovereignty has always operated primarily through the management of death — not the enhancement of life.

Necropolitics names this sovereign power to determine who may live and who must die — or, more precisely, who is treated as fully alive and who is reduced to a condition of living death. The concept draws on the historical realities of the plantation (where enslaved people existed as property), the colony (where colonised people existed as subjects without political standing), and the contemporary war zone or abandoned urban periphery (where people exist in conditions of systematic exposure to death).

For development practice, necropolitics provides the concept that "vulnerability" obscures. Development discourse speaks of "vulnerable populations" — a euphemism that hides the political structure of vulnerability. Populations are not naturally vulnerable. They are made vulnerable through specific political arrangements — land dispossession, border regimes, resource extraction, state abandonment — that determine who is protected and who is exposed to death. The necropolitical question is not "who is vulnerable?" but "who has decided that these people are expendable, and through what mechanisms?"

In Southern Africa specifically, necropolitics illuminates what development euphemisms conceal: the spatial legacy of apartheid that concentrates exposure to violence, disease, and economic precarity in specific populations; the border regime that renders certain migrants expendable; the mining economy that extracts resources while abandoning the communities that live above them; the urban governance that protects some neighbourhoods while abandoning others to structural violence.

## How to Apply
1. **Replace vulnerability with expendability.** "Do not ask 'who is vulnerable?' Ask 'who has been made expendable, by whom, through what mechanisms, and in whose interest?'"
2. **Trace the sovereign decision.** "Who decided — actively or through neglect — that this population would not be protected? A policy decision, a budget allocation, a border enforcement, a zoning regulation — these are necropolitical decisions."
3. **Name the death.** "Development discourse euphemises death. 'Under-five mortality' is children dying. 'Food insecurity' is people going hungry. The euphemism serves institutional comfort. Name what is happening."
4. **Connect to history.** "The population that is expendable today was usually made expendable by a specific historical process — colonialism, dispossession, forced removal. The history explains the present distribution of death."

## Examples
**Situation:** A mining company operates in a rural area where the local population experiences elevated rates of respiratory disease, water contamination, and economic displacement. The development programme focuses on "community resilience" and "livelihood diversification."
**Application:** "The resilience framework obscures the necropolitical structure. This community is not resilient or non-resilient — it is positioned within a political economy that has decided its health is less valuable than the minerals beneath its land. The respiratory disease is not a health problem — it is the physical manifestation of a sovereign decision that this community's bodies are acceptable collateral for resource extraction. The water contamination is not an environmental problem — it is the consequence of a regulatory arrangement that protects the company's operations more than the community's water. The livelihood diversification programme addresses the symptom — lost livelihoods — while leaving the structure intact: the community remains expendable, its land remains available for extraction, and its health remains subordinate to profit. A necropolitical analysis does not reject the programme. It asks the programme to be honest about what it cannot change and to name the political structure within which it operates."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Apply necropolitics to every inconvenience or institutional frustration.
**Why:** Necropolitics names a specific condition — sovereign power over death and expendability. Overextending it to describe every power imbalance dilutes its diagnostic power and trivialises the conditions it was designed to analyse.

**Don't:** Use necropolitics to paralyse action.
**Why:** The necropolitical diagnosis does not mean nothing can be done. It means that interventions which do not address the political structure of expendability will be limited. Name the limit, then act within it honestly.
