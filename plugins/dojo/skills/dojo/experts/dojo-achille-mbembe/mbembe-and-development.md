# Mbembe and Development

## When to Use
- When analysing how the development industry — its institutions, frameworks, categories, and practices — reproduces colonial logics even when animated by progressive intentions.
- When the question is about the structural relationship between those who design interventions and those who receive them.
- When a practitioner wants to understand how Mbembe's analysis applies to their daily work.

## Core Concept
Mbembe does not write about development directly in the way that Escobar or Ferguson do. But his analysis of the postcolony, necropolitics, and the colonial production of subjectivity provides a framework for understanding development as a specific formation of power-knowledge — one that is structurally continuous with the colonial project even when its intentions are radically different.

The structural continuities are multiple. First, the geography: development flows from North to South, from former metropoles to former colonies, along the channels that colonialism carved. The direction of the flow carries with it an implicit hierarchy — knowledge, expertise, and resources originate in the North and are applied to the South. Second, the epistemology: development knowledge is produced in Northern universities and think tanks, validated through Northern methodological standards, and published in Northern journals. Southern knowledge — local, experiential, embedded in practice — is treated as data to be collected rather than analysis to be engaged with. Third, the subject: development produces its populations as subjects defined by deficit — lacking capacity, lacking institutions, lacking governance, lacking modernity. This deficit frame mirrors the colonial production of the colonised as beings defined by absence.

The development relationship is also an extractive relationship, though the extraction is often invisible. Data is extracted from communities through surveys and evaluations. Narratives are extracted through case studies and success stories. Knowledge is extracted through participatory processes that harvest local expertise and convert it into programme design. Career advancement for international staff is extracted from engagement with Southern populations. None of this extraction is acknowledged as extraction because it occurs within a framework that defines itself as giving.

The practical question is not whether to abandon development — material needs are real and resources to address them are necessary. The question is whether development can be practised differently: with honest acknowledgement of the power relationships it embodies, with genuine recognition of Southern knowledge and agency, with structural accountability to affected populations rather than to donors, and with the humility to recognise that the frameworks being applied may themselves be part of the problem.

## How to Apply
1. **Map the direction of flows.** "In this programme, which direction does knowledge flow? Money? Accountability? If knowledge flows North-to-South and accountability flows South-to-North (to the donor), the colonial structure is operating."
2. **Identify the deficit frame.** "How does the programme describe its population? If the description begins with what people lack — skills, capacity, institutions, governance — the programme is using the colonial frame of deficit rather than recognising existing capability."
3. **Ask who benefits structurally.** "Beyond the intended beneficiaries, who benefits from this programme? The implementing organisation's continued existence? Staff careers? The donor's accountability requirements? Whose interests does the programme serve beyond its stated purpose?"
4. **Examine the theory of change for colonial assumptions.** "Does the theory of change assume a trajectory from traditional to modern, from informal to formal, from local to global? These trajectories are colonial — they position the present as deficient and the desired future as resembling the North."

## Examples
**Situation:** An international donor commissions a governance programme in Malawi that will strengthen public financial management, improve procurement systems, and build capacity for evidence-based policy-making. The programme is designed by consultants in Washington and London, managed from a regional office in Nairobi, and implemented by a mix of international and local staff. The programme's theory of change assumes that better systems will produce better governance outcomes.
**Application:** "The programme reproduces the colonial geography of knowledge: design in the metropole, management from a regional hub, implementation at the periphery. The knowledge hierarchy is structural — the frameworks (PFM, procurement standards, evidence-based policy) originate in Northern governance traditions and are applied to Malawi as universal standards. The theory of change assumes that Malawi's governance challenges result from inadequate systems — a deficit that Northern expertise can fill. What the programme cannot see from within its own framework is that the 'deficient' governance it aims to reform may be functioning according to logics that the programme's categories cannot capture — the commandement, the extraction economy, the patron-client networks that deliver governance through relationships rather than systems. The programme does not ask whether its frameworks are appropriate. It does not consider whether Malawian governance traditions offer resources that the programme's frameworks foreclose. It does not acknowledge that 'evidence-based policy' is itself a specific epistemic tradition, not a universal standard. None of this makes the programme malicious. It makes it colonial in structure while progressive in intention — which is the condition of most development practice."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Conclude that all development is colonial imposition and should stop.
**Why:** Material needs — health, education, infrastructure, economic opportunity — are real. The question is not whether to meet them but how to meet them without reproducing the structures that created the need. Withdrawal is not solidarity.

**Don't:** Treat Mbembe's analysis as applicable only to large international programmes.
**Why:** The colonial logics operate at every scale — in the local NGO that positions itself as knowing more than the community, in the evaluation that extracts data without returning findings, in the consultant who arrives with a framework and leaves with a report. Scale does not determine structure.
