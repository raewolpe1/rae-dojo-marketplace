# Brutalisation and Violence

## When to Use
- When analysing how violence — physical, structural, epistemic, and slow — operates within the contexts where development programmes work.
- When development interventions operate in environments of endemic violence and the programme design treats violence as context rather than constitutive condition.
- When examining how development practice itself can produce forms of violence through exclusion, classification, and the administration of life.

## Core Concept
Brutalisation, for Mbembe, is not simply the occurrence of violent acts. It is a condition — a state of being in which violence becomes the ordinary medium through which social relationships are conducted, political authority is exercised, and economic life is organised. The colony was a zone of brutalisation: a space where the normal constraints on violence were suspended because the colonised were positioned outside the moral community within which violence is prohibited. The whip, the forced labour, the dispossession — these were not excesses. They were the ordinary operation of the colonial system.

The argument is that brutalisation did not end with colonialism. It was restructured. In the postcolony, violence operates through multiple registers simultaneously. State violence — the security forces, the police, the military — continues to operate with impunity against populations marked as threatening or expendable. Structural violence — the slow death produced by poverty, exclusion, and abandonment — kills more people than direct violence but is rendered invisible by its gradualness. Intimate violence — the violence within households and communities that intensifies under conditions of dispossession and humiliation — is connected to structural conditions even when it appears private. Economic violence — the extraction of labour, resources, and life-chances from populations who receive nothing in return — operates continuously but is normalised as "the market."

For development practice, the brutalisation analysis disrupts the common separation between "violence" and "development." Programmes routinely operate in contexts of endemic violence while treating violence as a contextual factor — a risk to be managed — rather than as the constitutive condition of the society in which they work. A livelihoods programme in a community where women face daily violence, where young men are recruited into gangs or security forces, where the police are themselves a source of brutality, cannot meaningfully address "livelihoods" without addressing the violence that structures every aspect of economic life. The separation of "development" from "violence" is itself an epistemic violence — it renders invisible the connections between economic deprivation and physical brutalisation.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the violence.** "What forms of violence operate in this context? Physical, structural, epistemic, economic, intimate? If the programme design does not name them, it is treating violence as invisible — which is itself a form of complicity."
2. **Trace the colonial genealogy.** "The violence in this community is not random or cultural. It has a history. Colonial dispossession, forced removals, apartheid policing — the contemporary violence is connected to structures installed by colonial and apartheid governance."
3. **Examine the programme's relationship to violence.** "Does the programme address violence or does it work around it? If it works around it — providing livelihoods training while ignoring the violence that prevents people from using their skills — it is accommodating the violence rather than confronting it."
4. **Ask about slow violence.** "The dramatic violence — the shooting, the assault — is visible. But what about the slow violence? The contaminated water, the toxic exposure, the malnutrition, the preventable disease? Slow violence kills more people but generates no headlines and triggers no emergency response."

## Examples
**Situation:** A youth development programme on the Cape Flats in Cape Town provides life skills training, mentorship, and job placement support. The programme reports good completion rates but poor employment outcomes. Staff note that many participants drop out after completing training, and those who find jobs often lose them within months. The programme attributes this to "lack of motivation" and "challenging home environments."
**Application:** "The programme is operating in a zone of brutalisation and treating it as a context factor rather than a constitutive condition. The Cape Flats is not a 'challenging environment' — it is a space produced by apartheid's forced removals, where state violence (apartheid policing) was replaced by gang violence, where the structural violence of unemployment and overcrowding produces intimate violence within households, and where young men in particular navigate daily between the programme's promise of a different future and the immediate reality that violence is the most available form of agency. The 'lack of motivation' the programme observes is the rational assessment by young people that the programme's offer — a certificate, a possible entry-level job — is insufficient to extract them from the brutalisation they inhabit. The programme treats violence as background. For the participants, violence is foreground — it structures their movement, their relationships, their sense of possibility. A programme that does not address the violence — that does not engage with the gangs, the policing, the intimate violence, the structural dispossession — cannot address the unemployment it claims to target, because the unemployment is inseparable from the brutalisation."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat violence as cultural or inherent to specific communities.
**Why:** Brutalisation is produced by specific historical structures — colonialism, apartheid, economic dispossession, state abandonment. Attributing violence to "culture" reproduces the colonial logic that positioned the colonised as inherently violent.

**Don't:** Assume development programmes are automatically non-violent.
**Why:** Programmes can produce violence through their operations — exclusion criteria that humiliate, conditionality that punishes, targeting that stigmatises, data extraction that exposes. The violence of administration is real even when it involves no physical force.
