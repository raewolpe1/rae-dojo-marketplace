# Borders and Mobility

## When to Use
- When analysing how borders — colonial borders, national borders, administrative borders — produce categories of people: citizens and non-citizens, belongs and not-belongs, protected and expendable.
- When development programmes operate in contexts of migration, displacement, or refugee situations.
- When xenophobic violence or exclusionary politics affects programme populations.

## Core Concept
Borders do not merely separate spaces — they produce categories of people. The colonial border, drawn in Berlin in 1884–85, divided communities, separated kinship networks, and created "nationals" and "foreigners" out of people who had been neighbours. These borders persist — they are the borders of contemporary African states — and they continue to produce the categories they were designed to create. The border produces the citizen and the migrant, the national and the foreigner, the person who belongs and the person who does not.

Mbembe's analysis connects borders to necropolitics: the border determines who is protected by the state and who is exposed to violence, exploitation, and abandonment. The undocumented migrant in Johannesburg occupies a necropolitical position — they exist within the state's territory but outside its protection. They can be exploited, brutalised, or deported because the border has positioned them as non-belonging. Xenophobic violence — recurrent in South Africa since 2008 — is the eruption of this border logic within the national territory: the violent enforcement of belonging by citizens against those the border has marked as foreign.

For development practice in Southern Africa, the border analysis is immediately relevant. Programmes that target "South African citizens" reproduce the border's logic of exclusion — they provide services to those who belong and exclude those who do not, regardless of need. Refugee programmes that confine people to camps or specific areas exercise a spatial governance that echoes the colonial location or apartheid township. Migration programmes that frame movement as a problem to be managed rather than a right to be protected reproduce the sovereign prerogative to determine who may move and who must stay.

The deeper point is that mobility is a condition of African life — it always has been. Pre-colonial Africa was characterised by movement: seasonal migration, trade networks, pastoral mobility, labour migration. Colonial borders attempted to fix populations in place — to make them legible to governance and available for extraction. The contemporary obsession with "managing migration" continues this colonial project of fixation.

## How to Apply
1. **Denaturalise the border.** "This border was drawn by colonial powers in the 19th century. The communities it divided did not consent to the division. The 'foreigners' it produces may be members of communities that predate the border by centuries."
2. **Ask who the border protects.** "The border protects citizens from competition for resources. It also exposes non-citizens to exploitation, violence, and abandonment. Whose interests does this arrangement serve?"
3. **Connect xenophobia to structure.** "Xenophobic violence is not irrational hatred. It is the violent enforcement of a border logic that produces belonging and non-belonging. The violence reflects a structural arrangement, not merely individual prejudice."
4. **Design for mobility.** "If the programme excludes non-citizens, it reproduces the border's logic. Can the programme serve people based on need rather than nationality? If not, name the political choice being made."

## Examples
**Situation:** A livelihoods programme in Gauteng serves "local communities" but excludes Zimbabwean and Mozambican migrants who live in the same areas, work in the same informal economy, and face the same economic challenges. Staff explain that the programme is funded for South African beneficiaries.
**Application:** "The programme has reproduced the colonial border within its targeting criteria. The Zimbabwean and Mozambican residents are not naturally excluded — they are excluded by a political decision embedded in the funding agreement, which treats the colonial border as the boundary of moral obligation. The people excluded share the same economic space, face the same challenges, and often live in the same households as those included. The exclusion is not based on need — it is based on the category the border produces: national vs. foreign. In a context where xenophobic violence recurrently targets exactly these populations, the programme's exclusion reinforces the logic that produces the violence: these people do not belong, these people are not our responsibility, these people are expendable. The practical solution may be limited — the funder requires national targeting. But the programme should at minimum name what it is doing: it is reproducing a colonial border as a criterion for assistance, and this reproduction has consequences for excluded populations that the programme should monitor and report."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat migration as inherently problematic.
**Why:** Mobility is a normal condition of human life and a fundamental feature of African societies. The framing of migration as a "crisis" or "challenge" reproduces the colonial project of fixing populations in place for governance purposes.

**Don't:** Ignore the gendered dimensions of mobility.
**Why:** Borders affect men and women differently. Women migrants face specific forms of exploitation, violence, and exclusion that the general analysis must address.
