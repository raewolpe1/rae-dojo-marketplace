# Subjectivity and Selfhood

## When to Use
- When analysing how colonialism, the postcolony, and development practice produce specific kinds of subjects — and foreclose other possibilities of selfhood.
- When the question is about dignity, recognition, and the capacity to define oneself rather than being defined by others.
- When development programmes create identity categories — beneficiary, vulnerable person, empowered woman, young entrepreneur — and these categories shape how people understand themselves.

## Core Concept
Mbembe's work on subjectivity begins with the colonial production of "the Black" — the creation of a category of person defined by lack, deficit, and expendability. The colonial subject was not merely governed differently. The colonial subject was produced as a different kind of being — not fully human, not fully rational, not fully capable of self-governance. This production was not incidental to colonialism. It was constitutive. The colony required a subject who could be legitimately governed without consent, extracted from without compensation, and brutalised without moral consequence.

The postcolonial question of subjectivity is whether and how people who were produced as colonial subjects can produce themselves as something else. This is not simply a matter of political liberation — the end of formal colonialism — but of the deeper work of self-making: the capacity to define oneself, to narrate one's own story, to imagine one's own future rather than inhabiting the futures imagined by others.

For development practice, the subjectivity question is acute. Development programmes routinely produce subjects: the "beneficiary" who receives, the "vulnerable person" who needs protection, the "empowered woman" who has been transformed by the intervention, the "young entrepreneur" who has been equipped with skills. These categories are not neutral descriptions. They are subject positions — they define who the person is within the programme's logic, what they can do, and what they can become. The question Mbembe would press is whether these subject positions enable self-making or foreclose it — whether the programme's categories expand the person's capacity to define themselves or constrain it within externally defined identities.

The deeper issue is recognition. Mbembe draws on Fanon and Hegel to argue that the colonial wound is fundamentally a wound of recognition — the refusal to recognise the colonised as fully human. Development programmes that position their populations as recipients of external expertise, objects of external knowledge, and targets of external intervention risk reproducing this refusal of recognition, however benevolently intended.

## How to Apply
1. **Examine the subject positions the programme creates.** "What kind of person does this programme produce? A beneficiary? A client? A partner? A citizen? Each category positions the person differently in relation to the programme and to their own agency."
2. **Ask about self-definition.** "Do the people in this programme define themselves the way the programme defines them? If the programme calls them 'vulnerable women' and they call themselves 'mothers who are managing' — whose definition structures the intervention?"
3. **Look for the deficit frame.** "Is the programme designed around what people lack (skills, knowledge, resources, capacity) or around what people have and do? The deficit frame reproduces the colonial production of subjects defined by absence."
4. **Assess the recognition relationship.** "Does the programme recognise its population as people with knowledge, agency, and the capacity for self-governance? Or does it position them as objects of intervention who need to be taught, trained, and transformed?"

## Examples
**Situation:** A women's economic empowerment programme in KwaZulu-Natal defines its beneficiaries as "previously disadvantaged women who lack access to economic opportunities." The programme provides business skills training, mentorship, and seed funding. M&E indicators measure the number of women who start businesses and their income levels. Programme communications feature before-and-after narratives: women who were dependent and are now independent, who were passive and are now active, who were unskilled and are now skilled.
**Application:** "The programme has produced a subject: the disempowered-woman-becoming-empowered. This subject is defined entirely by the programme's narrative — she begins in deficit (lacking skills, opportunities, independence) and arrives at surplus (equipped, connected, autonomous) through the programme's intervention. The actual women — who managed households, raised children, maintained social networks, navigated violence, sustained communities, and exercised agency throughout their lives — disappear behind this narrative. The 'before' erases their existing capabilities. The 'after' credits the programme for capacities the women may have developed independently. The before-and-after structure reproduces a colonial logic: the subject begins in darkness (pre-intervention, pre-development, pre-modernity) and arrives in light through external intervention. This is not to say the programme does nothing. It may provide genuinely useful resources. But the narrative through which it understands itself — transformation of deficient subjects into capable ones — denies the selfhood that existed before the programme arrived and claims credit for the selfhood that exists after."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Romanticise pre-programme subjectivity.
**Why:** The point is not that people were fine before the programme and the programme ruined them. The point is that people had existing selfhoods — complex, constrained, resourceful, damaged — that the programme's categories either engage with or erase.

**Don't:** Treat subjectivity as only an individual matter.
**Why:** Selfhood in African philosophical traditions is fundamentally relational — it is constituted through relationships with others, with community, with ancestors, with the living world. The liberal individual subject is one possibility, not the universal form.
