# Extraction and Dispossession

## When to Use
- When analysing how extractive economic relationships — colonial in origin — persist in contemporary economic arrangements.
- When development programmes operate in contexts of resource extraction, land dispossession, or economic exploitation.
- When examining whether development interventions themselves reproduce extractive logics — extracting data, knowledge, or narrative from communities.

## Core Concept
Extraction is not merely economic — it is ontological. To be extracted from is to have your life, your labour, your resources, and your future treated as raw material for someone else's project. The colonial relationship was fundamentally extractive: African land, labour, minerals, and agricultural products were extracted for the benefit of European metropoles. The colony existed to produce value that was consumed elsewhere.

Mbembe's argument is that extraction did not end with colonialism — it was restructured. The postcolonial economy in much of Africa continues to be organised around the extraction of primary commodities for global markets, with minimal domestic value addition. The mineral wealth of the DRC, the oil of Nigeria, the platinum of South Africa — these produce enormous global value while the populations that live above them remain among the world's poorest. This is not a failure of development. It is the continuation of the colonial economic logic by other means.

For development practice, extraction operates at multiple levels. At the economic level, programmes that promote export-oriented agriculture or facilitate extractive industry without addressing the distribution of value are participating in the extractive logic. At the epistemic level, development research that extracts data and knowledge from communities — conducting surveys, collecting stories, documenting practices — and converts them into publications, reports, and career advancement for researchers reproduces extraction at the knowledge level. At the narrative level, development communications that use African suffering as material for fundraising — the "poverty porn" critique — extract narrative value from people's pain.

The dispossession dimension is particularly relevant in Southern Africa. Land dispossession under colonialism and apartheid is the foundational structure of economic inequality in the region. Development programmes that address poverty without addressing land — who owns it, who was dispossessed of it, who benefits from it — are treating consequences while ignoring the cause.

## How to Apply
1. **Map the extraction chain.** "Where does the value go? From the community to the mining company to the global market. From the beneficiary's data to the evaluation report to the donor's accountability framework. Extraction always has a direction."
2. **Ask what stays.** "Of the value generated by this programme or economic activity, what stays in the community and what leaves? If most of the value leaves, the relationship is extractive regardless of what it is called."
3. **Examine epistemic extraction.** "Is this research or evaluation extracting knowledge from the community? Do the people surveyed benefit from the knowledge produced? Will they see the report? Will it change anything for them? If not, the knowledge extraction serves the researcher, not the researched."
4. **Connect to dispossession.** "The poverty you are addressing — what historical dispossession produced it? Land? Labour rights? Education? Until the dispossession is named, the poverty programme treats a symptom."

## Examples
**Situation:** An international NGO conducts a large-scale household survey in rural Mozambique to inform a food security programme. The data is analysed at headquarters in Europe, published in a report that advances staff careers, and used to design an intervention. The communities surveyed never see the report.
**Application:** "This is epistemic extraction. The community's knowledge — their food practices, their economic strategies, their coping mechanisms — has been extracted through the survey instrument, transported to Europe, converted into institutional knowledge that serves the NGO's purposes, and the community receives nothing in return except the eventual intervention designed from their own extracted knowledge processed through external categories. The parallel to colonial extraction is structural, not metaphorical: raw material (data) is extracted from the periphery (rural Mozambique), processed in the metropole (European headquarters), and returned as a finished product (the programme) designed to meet the metropole's standards (donor accountability) rather than the community's self-defined needs. This does not make the survey evil. But it makes it extractive. A non-extractive alternative would return the findings to the community, involve the community in the analysis, and allow the community to shape the intervention that their knowledge informs."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat all North-South economic relationships as extraction.
**Why:** Trade, investment, and collaboration can be mutually beneficial. The question is whether the relationship is structured to extract value from one party for the benefit of another, or to generate and distribute value equitably.

**Don't:** Ignore African agency in extractive relationships.
**Why:** African governments, elites, and communities are not passive victims of extraction. They negotiate, resist, appropriate, and sometimes perpetuate extractive relationships. The analysis must include African agency, not only external imposition.
