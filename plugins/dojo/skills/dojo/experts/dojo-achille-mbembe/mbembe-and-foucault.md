# Mbembe and Foucault

## When to Use
- When determining whether a Foucauldian or Mbembian analysis is more appropriate for a given development context.
- When examining the limits of European critical theory when applied to postcolonial and African contexts.
- When using both frameworks together and needing to understand their relationship.

## Core Concept
Mbembe's relationship with Foucault is one of critical extension. He takes Foucault seriously — the analytics of power, the concept of governmentality, the insistence that power produces rather than merely represses — but argues that Foucault's framework was developed within and for European modernity, and that its application to colonial and postcolonial contexts reveals fundamental limitations.

The central critique concerns biopower. Foucault argued that modern power shifted from the sovereign's right to kill (power over death) to the governance of populations through the management of life (power over life) — biopower. This framework assumes a context in which the state's primary orientation toward its population is to "make live" — to manage health, productivity, and welfare. Mbembe's argument is that the colony was never a biopolitical space in this sense. The colonial state did not orient itself toward making the colonised live. It oriented itself toward making them work, extracting from them, and — when they were no longer useful — letting them die or actively killing them. The colony was a necropolitical space: sovereignty expressed itself not through the management of life but through the administration of death.

This is not a minor correction. It reframes the entire analytics of power for contexts where "make live" was never the operative logic. Development practice in postcolonial contexts often operates as if biopower were the governing logic — as if the state's orientation is toward the welfare of its population and the challenge is capacity or resources. Mbembe argues that in many postcolonial contexts, the state's orientation toward large portions of its population remains necropolitical: not "make live" but "let die," and sometimes "make die." The mining community exposed to toxic waste, the informal settlement without services, the population in the conflict zone that receives no protection — these are not failures of biopower. They are expressions of necropower.

The second major departure concerns the subject. Foucault analysed how power produces subjects through discipline, normalisation, and governance. But Foucault's subject is always already a subject — a being recognised as human, as a member of the political community, as someone to be governed. The colonial subject was produced as something else: as not-fully-human, as outside the moral and political community, as a being to be used rather than governed. The analytics of subject formation must account for this radical exclusion, which Foucault's European framework does not.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask whether "make live" applies.** "Is the state's orientation toward this population biopolitical (managing their life) or necropolitical (administering their expendability)? If the state provides no services, no protection, and no recognition — you are in necropolitical territory, and Foucault's biopower framework misdiagnoses the situation."
2. **Use Foucault for techniques, Mbembe for context.** "Foucault's analytics of governmentality — how populations are known, classified, managed — remain useful for understanding the techniques of development. But the context within which these techniques operate — colonial history, racial hierarchy, extractive economy — requires Mbembe."
3. **Check for the recognition assumption.** "Is the population recognised as members of the political community whose welfare matters? If not — if they are undocumented migrants, ethnic minorities, residents of abandoned territories — the Foucauldian assumption of governance does not hold. These populations are managed through abandonment, not through biopolitical care."
4. **Layer the analyses.** "A programme may operate biopolitically (managing health, education, welfare) within a context that is necropolitical (the state has abandoned this population). The programme's internal logic is Foucauldian. The structural context is Mbembian. Both analyses are needed."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant is evaluating a public health programme in the Eastern Cape that provides HIV/AIDS treatment, maternal health services, and childhood nutrition interventions. The programme is well-designed and evidence-based, but health outcomes remain poor. The evaluation needs to explain why.
**Application:** "A Foucauldian analysis would examine the programme's techniques of governance — how it classifies patients, manages treatment protocols, produces health data, disciplines adherence — and would ask whether these techniques produce the outcomes they promise or generate unintended effects. This analysis is useful but insufficient. Mbembe would ask the prior question: what is the state's relationship to this population? The Eastern Cape — particularly the former Transkei — was produced by apartheid as a zone of abandonment: a 'homeland' designed to warehouse Black labour surplus to the economy's needs. The post-apartheid state has not transformed this structural relationship. Infrastructure remains degraded, health workers are scarce, supply chains are unreliable — not because of technical failures but because the structural orientation toward this population remains one of managed neglect rather than genuine biopolitical care. The programme operates biopolitically — it attempts to 'make live' — but it operates within a necropolitical structure that lets die. The programme's failure is not technical. It is structural. The biopolitical intervention cannot overcome the necropolitical context."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present Mbembe as rejecting Foucault entirely.
**Why:** Mbembe explicitly builds on Foucault. The relationship is extension and critique, not rejection. Foucault's analytical tools — governmentality, discourse, normalisation, subject formation — remain useful. They are insufficient alone.

**Don't:** Apply the necropolitics/biopower distinction as a binary.
**Why:** Most contexts involve both logics operating simultaneously — biopolitical management of some populations alongside necropolitical abandonment of others. The question is always which logic dominates for which population, not whether a context is "biopolitical" or "necropolitical."
