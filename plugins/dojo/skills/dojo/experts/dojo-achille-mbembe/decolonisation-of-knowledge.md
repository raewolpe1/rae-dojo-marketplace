# Decolonisation of Knowledge

## When to Use
- When examining how knowledge hierarchies in development — whose theories, whose methods, whose evidence — reproduce colonial epistemologies.
- When engaging with demands for decolonisation of development practice, research, or evaluation in practical rather than rhetorical terms.
- When the question is about what it would actually mean to produce knowledge differently.

## Core Concept
Mbembe's contribution to the decolonisation of knowledge debate — catalysed in South Africa by the #RhodesMustFall and #FeesMustFall movements of 2015–2016 — goes beyond the common framing of inclusion and representation. The issue is not simply that African scholars are underrepresented in global knowledge production, or that African perspectives are insufficiently cited. The issue is structural: the very categories through which knowledge is produced, validated, and circulated were forged in the colonial encounter and continue to carry its logics.

The university — the primary institution of knowledge production — is a European invention that was transplanted to the colonies as an instrument of colonial governance. It taught the colonised to think in the coloniser's categories, to value the coloniser's knowledge, and to measure their own intellectual traditions against the coloniser's standards. The postcolonial university in Africa continues to operate largely within this framework: the curricula, the methods, the publication standards, the promotion criteria, and the languages of instruction remain oriented toward European and North American knowledge traditions.

For development practice, the decolonisation of knowledge has three dimensions. The first is methodological: whose methods structure how knowledge is produced? The dominance of quantitative methods, RCTs, logframes, and standardised indicators is not methodologically neutral. These are specific epistemic traditions with specific assumptions about what counts as evidence, what can be known, and how knowledge should be organised. They are powerful but partial, and their dominance marginalises other ways of knowing — narrative, experiential, relational, embodied.

The second dimension is categorical: whose categories structure how reality is understood? "Poverty," "governance," "gender," "resilience" — these are not neutral descriptors. They are categories produced within specific intellectual traditions, carrying specific assumptions about what matters and how it should be addressed. The question is whether these categories capture the realities they claim to describe or whether they impose a framework that distorts as much as it reveals.

The third dimension is institutional: whose institutions control how knowledge circulates? Academic journals, donor reporting systems, evaluation databases, policy platforms — these institutions determine what knowledge becomes visible, what knowledge influences decisions, and what knowledge disappears. They are overwhelmingly controlled from the Global North.

## How to Apply
1. **Audit the knowledge chain.** "Who designed the framework? Who defined the indicators? Who chose the methods? Who will analyse the data? Who will write the report? Who will read it? If the answers follow a North-to-South gradient, the knowledge chain reproduces colonial epistemology."
2. **Question the categories.** "Is 'resilience' a local concept or an imported one? Does it capture what communities actually do, or does it impose a framework that positions them as absorbers of shocks they did not create? The category is not neutral."
3. **Distinguish cosmetic from structural decolonisation.** "Adding African scholars to advisory boards is cosmetic. Allowing African intellectual traditions to shape the research questions is structural. The test is whether the framework would be different if it were genuinely designed from African knowledge — not whether African names appear in the credits."
4. **Create knowledge return loops.** "If knowledge is extracted from communities, it must be returned to communities in forms they can use. The evaluation report written in English for the donor is not a return. A community discussion of findings in local language, facilitated by local researchers, might be."

## Examples
**Situation:** A South African university research centre conducts a multi-year study on urban informality in Johannesburg. The study uses ethnographic methods, produces high-quality publications in international journals, and generates significant academic impact. The communities studied — Alexandra, Diepsloot, inner-city Johannesburg — are never informed of the findings. The conceptual frameworks used — informality, precarity, the right to the city — are drawn from European urban theory.
**Application:** "The research reproduces colonial knowledge extraction with South African institutional clothing. The extraction is epistemic: community knowledge — how people organise informal economies, negotiate access to services, build social networks, create liveable spaces — is captured through fieldwork, transported to the university, processed through European theoretical frameworks, and converted into academic publications that advance researchers' careers. The communities receive nothing. The conceptual frameworks — informality as defined by Castells or Roy, precarity as defined by Standing, the right to the city as defined by Lefebvre — may or may not capture how residents of Alexandra understand their own situation. The question is never asked because the knowledge hierarchy positions the researcher as the one who understands and the community as the one who is understood. Structural decolonisation would require the research to begin from the community's own categories, to involve the community in analysis, and to return findings in forms that serve community interests — not merely researcher interests."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Equate decolonisation with rejecting all Western knowledge.
**Why:** Mbembe is explicit: the goal is not to replace one provincialism with another. African intellectual traditions can engage with European, Asian, and other traditions from a position of equality rather than subordination. Decolonisation means ending the hierarchy, not reversing it.

**Don't:** Treat decolonisation as primarily about language or symbols.
**Why:** Translating a logframe into isiZulu does not decolonise the logframe. The structure — inputs, outputs, outcomes, impact in linear sequence — carries specific epistemic assumptions regardless of the language it is expressed in. Decolonisation requires questioning the structure, not translating it.
