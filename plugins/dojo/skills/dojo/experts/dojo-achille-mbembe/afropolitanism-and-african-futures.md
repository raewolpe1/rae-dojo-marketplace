# Afropolitanism and African Futures

## When to Use
- When moving beyond diagnostic critique of colonial and postcolonial structures to imagine alternative futures.
- When considering what development, governance, or economy could look like if designed from African thought and experience.
- When the question is about possibility, not just diagnosis.

## Core Concept
Afropolitanism is Mbembe's term for a way of being African in the world that neither retreats into nativist essentialism nor capitulates to Western universalism. It names the condition of Africans who inhabit multiple cultural worlds simultaneously — who are shaped by African, European, Asian, and American influences and who refuse to choose one identity over the others. It is not rootless cosmopolitanism — it is rooted in African experience while remaining open to the world.

The deeper concept is African futures — the insistence that the diagnostic critique of colonialism and the postcolony must lead to the imaginative question: what could Africa become? Mbembe refuses both Afro-pessimism (the conclusion that Africa is permanently broken) and Afro-optimism (the cheerful narrative that Africa is rising). Both are reductive. The question is harder: given the structures that colonialism installed and the postcolony perpetuates, what genuinely different forms of community, governance, economy, and selfhood are possible?

For development practice, this is the hardest and most important question. Development as currently practised imagines futures for Africa based on trajectories defined elsewhere — GDP growth, institutional modernisation, democratic consolidation, market integration. These are not African futures. They are European futures projected onto Africa. An African future might draw on ubuntu's relational ethics rather than liberal individualism, on communal land tenure rather than private property, on circular economies rather than extractive growth, on regional integration across colonial borders rather than national development within them.

The practical challenge is immense: imagining otherwise requires both the freedom to think beyond existing frameworks and the material conditions that make alternatives viable. Mbembe does not underestimate this difficulty. But he insists that the failure to imagine is itself a form of colonisation — the colonisation of the future by the categories of the present.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask what African alternatives exist.** "Before importing a framework, ask: is there an African intellectual tradition, an African institutional form, or an African practice that addresses this challenge differently? There almost always is."
2. **Refuse the single trajectory.** "Development is not a single path from traditional to modern. Multiple trajectories are possible. Which ones are being foreclosed by the current programme design?"
3. **Centre African creativity.** "African artists, writers, musicians, entrepreneurs, and communities are already imagining and building alternative futures. What can the programme learn from them?"
4. **Distinguish imagination from fantasy.** "African futures must be imagined within the constraints of material reality — but those constraints are not as fixed as they appear. Apartheid seemed permanent until it was not."

## Examples
**Situation:** A regional economic programme in Southern Africa is designed around standard trade facilitation — reducing tariffs, harmonising regulations, building infrastructure for cross-border commerce. The programme uses European integration as its reference model.
**Application:** "The programme imagines an African economic future modelled on the European Union — but the EU emerged from European history, European institutions, and European political economy. What would regional integration look like if it began from African realities instead? From the cross-border kinship networks that already move goods, people, and resources across colonial borders daily? From the informal trade that constitutes the majority of cross-border economic activity but is invisible to formal trade statistics? From ubuntu's relational ethics rather than market competition? An African economic future might look less like the EU and more like the amplification of existing African economic relationships — relationships that colonial borders interrupted but did not destroy. This does not mean rejecting trade facilitation. It means asking whether trade facilitation as currently conceived — focused on formal trade, measured by customs data, benchmarked against European integration — captures or misses the economic future that Africans are already building."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce afropolitanism to a lifestyle brand for elite urban Africans.
**Why:** Afropolitanism is a philosophical concept about navigating multiple identities and refusing essentialist closure. It is not a marketing category for Lagos and Johannesburg creative classes.

**Don't:** Present African futures as requiring rejection of all external ideas.
**Why:** Mbembe is explicit: afropolitanism means openness to the world, not closure. African futures will incorporate ideas from everywhere — but the selection, adaptation, and integration must be driven by African agency, not external prescription.
