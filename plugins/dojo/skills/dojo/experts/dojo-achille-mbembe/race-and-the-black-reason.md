# Race and the Black Reason

## When to Use
- When analysing how racial categories — often unmarked and unnamed — structure development practice, resource allocation, and governance.
- When examining the colonial production of "the Black" as a category and its persistence in contemporary development discourse.
- When the question is about who is valued, who is governed, and who is expendable — and whether race structures these determinations.

## Core Concept
The Black reason (Critique de la raison nègre) is Mbembe's term for the system of discourse, knowledge, and practice through which "the Black" was produced as a category — a category of exclusion, extraction, and expendability that was essential to the construction of European modernity. Mbembe's argument is that race is not incidental to modernity — it is constitutive. The Enlightenment's universal reason required its other: the "unreasonable" Black body that could be enslaved, colonised, and extracted from precisely because it was positioned outside the universal.

For development practice in Southern Africa, race is not a historical footnote. South Africa's development challenges — spatial inequality, land distribution, economic exclusion, differential access to services — are structured by race. Apartheid was not merely a political system — it was a comprehensive racial governance apparatus that determined where people could live, what work they could do, what education they could receive, and what futures were available to them. The post-1994 dispensation dismantled the legal apparatus but the structural effects persist: spatial patterns, wealth distribution, institutional cultures, and knowledge hierarchies continue to reflect racial ordering.

The development sector often treats race as a demographic variable — a category for disaggregating data — rather than as a structural force that shapes who designs programmes, whose knowledge counts, who makes decisions, and who benefits. The result is that racial power operates within development institutions without being named or examined. The international consultant (often white or from the Global North) designs the framework. The local staff (often Black African) implement it. The beneficiary (almost always Black African) receives the intervention within categories they did not design. This is not a conspiracy — it is a structure. And structures persist precisely because they do not require conscious intent.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the racial structure.** "In this programme, who designs and who implements? Who produces knowledge and who is known? If these roles correlate with racial categories, the correlation is not incidental — it is structural."
2. **Historicise the present.** "The current spatial distribution of poverty in South Africa is not random. It is the physical legacy of racial zoning under apartheid. The programme that addresses poverty without addressing its racial genealogy treats a symptom as a cause."
3. **Examine the knowledge hierarchy.** "Whose theoretical frameworks are used? Whose methods are privileged? If the frameworks originate from predominantly white institutions and the populations studied are predominantly Black, the knowledge hierarchy reproduces a racial hierarchy."
4. **Refuse colourblindness.** "The claim that 'we don't see race, we see need' is itself a racial position — it is the position of someone for whom race is not a daily material constraint. In Southern Africa, not seeing race is a luxury that race affords."

## Examples
**Situation:** A South African economic development programme targets "previously disadvantaged communities" — the standard post-apartheid euphemism. The programme's theory of change focuses on skills development and market access. Staff avoid discussing race, framing the work as addressing "economic disadvantage."
**Application:** "The euphemism is the problem. 'Previously disadvantaged' is a racial category with the race removed. These communities were not disadvantaged by accident or misfortune — they were disadvantaged by a comprehensive racial governance system that dispossessed them of land, confined them to specific areas, denied them education, and excluded them from economic opportunity on the basis of race. The programme's theory of change — skills and market access — addresses the consequences while avoiding the cause. Skills development cannot compensate for the absence of inherited wealth, land, networks, and institutional access that racial dispossession produced. Market access cannot overcome the structural advantages that racial privilege accumulated over centuries. This does not make the programme useless. It makes it insufficient — and dishonest if it claims to address economic disadvantage without naming the racial structure that produced it. In South Africa, economic disadvantage has a name. It is racial dispossession. The programme that cannot say this cannot address this."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat race as only a South African or American issue.
**Why:** Race structures development globally — who designs, who funds, who implements, who benefits, who evaluates. The racial hierarchy operates in every international development relationship, though its specific forms vary by context.

**Don't:** Reduce race to individual attitudes.
**Why:** Racism as personal prejudice is the least interesting dimension. Racial structure — the systemic organisation of institutions, resources, and knowledge along racial lines — is what Mbembe analyses. You can have non-racist individuals operating within a racist structure.
