# Mbembe Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When translating Mbembe's analytical framework into guidance for practitioners working within the development industry.
- When navigating the tension between understanding colonial continuities and continuing to do development work.
- When the question is: "I understand the critique — now what do I do?"

## Core Concept
The practitioner's dilemma with Mbembe is this: the analysis reveals that the development industry reproduces colonial structures, but the practitioner works within that industry and the populations the industry serves have material needs that require resources. Walking away is not solidarity — it is abandonment. But continuing without acknowledging the structures one operates within is complicity dressed as benevolence. The question is how to practise honestly within a compromised structure.

Mbembe's work suggests five orientations for honest practice.

First, structural honesty. Name what you are doing. If the programme extracts data, say so. If the accountability flows to the donor rather than the community, say so. If the theory of change assumes a trajectory defined in the North, say so. The naming does not solve the problem but it creates the conditions for genuine engagement with it. Most development dishonesty is not deliberate — it is structural. The programme genuinely believes it is empowering beneficiaries when it is producing subjects defined by external categories. Structural honesty requires seeing the structure, not just the intention.

Second, epistemic humility. The frameworks you bring — logframes, theories of change, results-based management, evidence-based practice — are specific intellectual traditions, not universal truths. They capture some things well and others poorly. The knowledge that communities hold — about their own situations, their own priorities, their own capacities — is not raw data waiting for your analysis. It is analysis in its own right. Epistemic humility means treating local knowledge as knowledge, not as data.

Third, reverse accountability. Development accountability flows upward — to the donor, to the board, to the funder. This direction reproduces the colonial structure of extraction. Reverse accountability asks: what does the programme owe the people it claims to serve? Not gratitude for services rendered, but genuine accountability for decisions made, resources allocated, and outcomes produced. What would it look like if communities could hold programmes accountable rather than the reverse?

Fourth, naming what cannot be changed. Some structural constraints are beyond the practitioner's control — donor requirements, institutional mandates, political contexts. Honest practice names these constraints rather than pretending they do not exist. The practitioner who says "I cannot change the donor's targeting criteria but I can document the consequences of excluding non-citizens" is practising more honestly than the practitioner who pretends the targeting is needs-based.

Fifth, imagination. Mbembe insists that critique without imagination is incomplete. What would this programme look like if it were designed from African knowledge? If accountability flowed to communities? If the categories were generated locally rather than imported? The imaginative exercise may not be immediately implementable, but it reveals the distance between what is and what could be — and that distance is the space for practice.

## How to Apply
1. **Start every design process with a structural audit.** "Before designing the intervention, map the colonial continuities: who designs, who implements, who benefits, who is accountable to whom, whose knowledge counts, whose categories structure the programme. This is not self-flagellation. It is due diligence."
2. **Build knowledge return into every research activity.** "Every survey, every evaluation, every participatory process extracts knowledge. Build a return mechanism: findings returned in accessible forms, community interpretation sessions, shared analysis. This is not optional — it is the minimum ethical standard."
3. **Create spaces for community categories.** "Alongside the programme's categories — beneficiary, indicator, outcome — create space for the community's categories. What do they call the problem? How do they measure change? What outcomes matter to them? The programme does not have to abandon its framework, but it should know what its framework cannot see."
4. **Document the structural constraints honestly.** "When donor requirements, political contexts, or institutional mandates constrain the programme, document them explicitly. The midterm review that says 'targeting excludes non-citizens due to donor requirements, with the following consequences' is more honest than the midterm review that presents the targeting as needs-based."
5. **Practise strategic imagination.** "In every programme design, ask: what would this look like if it were genuinely decolonised? The answer may not be implementable within current constraints, but it identifies the direction of travel and reveals what the current design forecloses."

## Examples
**Situation:** A development consultant based in Cape Town is designing a mid-term evaluation of a rural livelihoods programme in the Eastern Cape. The donor requires a logframe-based assessment with quantitative indicators. The programme team wants to show positive results. The communities have not been consulted about what they consider meaningful change.
**Application:** "The consultant faces the practitioner's dilemma in concentrated form. The evaluation framework is externally defined. The accountability flows to the donor. The knowledge extraction — surveys, interviews, data collection — will produce a report that serves institutional purposes. Honest practice within these constraints might look like this: (1) Conduct the logframe assessment as required, but supplement it with community-defined indicators of change. Present both. (2) Return preliminary findings to communities before writing the report. Allow their interpretation to inform the analysis. (3) In the report, name the structural constraints: 'This evaluation uses donor-defined indicators. Community-defined indicators of change suggest a different picture.' (4) Acknowledge the extraction: 'This evaluation collected data from X households. Findings will be shared with communities through [specific mechanism].' (5) In the recommendations, include one section on what the programme would look like if redesigned from community priorities rather than donor frameworks. None of this transforms the colonial structure of the evaluation. But it practises honestly within it, names what it cannot change, and creates openings for different practice."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Offer decolonisation as a set of techniques to be added to existing practice.
**Why:** Decolonisation is not a toolkit. Adding "participatory sessions" or "community consultation" to a programme designed in Washington does not decolonise it — it adds a participatory veneer to a colonial structure. The structure must be questioned, not decorated.

**Don't:** Use Mbembe's analysis to produce paralysis.
**Why:** The fact that development reproduces colonial structures does not mean development practitioners should stop working. It means they should work honestly, name the structures they operate within, and create openings for different practice. Paralysis is a luxury that the populations development serves cannot afford.
