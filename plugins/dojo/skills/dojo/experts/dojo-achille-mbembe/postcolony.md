# The Postcolony

## When to Use
- When analysing the specific forms of governance, authority, and everyday life in postcolonial African states.
- When development interventions encounter governance patterns — personalised authority, performative power, extractive institutions — that cannot be understood through European political theory.
- When the question is about what colonialism left behind and how it shapes contemporary governance.

## Core Concept
The postcolony, as Mbembe theorises it in On the Postcolony (2001), is not simply "what comes after colonialism." It is a specific formation of power, time, and subjectivity characterised by the entanglement of multiple temporalities — the pre-colonial, the colonial, and the postcolonial existing simultaneously rather than sequentially. The postcolony is not a failed version of the European nation-state. It has its own logics of power, its own modes of governance, and its own forms of subjectivity that must be understood on their own terms.

Three features characterise the postcolony. First, the commandement — the specific form of sovereign authority that operates through spectacle, violence, and the demand for submission. The commandement does not govern through bureaucratic rationality or democratic legitimation. It governs through the display of power — the presidential motorcade, the public rally, the arbitrary exercise of authority — and through the demand that subjects acknowledge this power through performance and submission. Second, the simulacrum — the postcolonial state's production of an elaborate performance of order, modernity, and governance that exists alongside the reality of extraction, abandonment, and institutional decay. The state performs statehood without delivering on its promises. Third, the economy of extraction — the continuation of the colonial economic logic in which the primary function of governance is to extract resources from the territory and its people, whether through taxation, licensing, corruption, or the management of external aid.

For development practice, the postcolony analysis is essential because it explains why governance interventions based on European models of bureaucratic rationality, democratic accountability, and rule of law persistently fail to produce the expected results. These interventions misdiagnose the problem as a deficit of capacity or political will. The postcolony analysis reveals that the governance is not failing — it is functioning according to a different logic, one installed by colonialism and perpetuated by postcolonial elites who benefit from it.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the commandement.** "In this governance context, how is authority exercised? Through bureaucratic procedure or through personalised power? Through democratic legitimation or through spectacle and demand? The answer reveals what kind of governance actually operates."
2. **Look for the simulacrum.** "Is the institution performing a function it does not actually fulfil? The development plan that exists as a document but not as a practice, the policy that is enacted but not enforced — these are simulacra of governance."
3. **Trace the extraction.** "Where does the money go? The colonial state extracted resources from colony to metropole. The postcolonial state extracts resources from periphery to centre — from rural to urban, from citizens to elites, from the state to private accounts."
4. **Do not diagnose failure — diagnose function.** "The postcolonial state is not failing to be a European state. It is succeeding at being a postcolonial state. Understand its logic before prescribing reform."

## Examples
**Situation:** A governance programme aimed at strengthening local government service delivery has built capacity for years but finds that trained officials are regularly transferred, budget allocations do not follow plans, and communities report no improvement in services.
**Application:** "The programme has encountered the postcolony. The transfers are not administrative inefficiency — they are the commandement exercising its prerogative to move officials, preventing the accumulation of local power bases that might challenge central authority. The budget allocations that do not follow plans are not poor financial management — they are the extraction economy redirecting resources toward priorities that serve the commandement rather than the community. The trained officials are not incapable — they are operating within a system whose actual logic differs from its official logic. The programme's theory of change — train officials, plan budgets, deliver services — assumes a bureaucratic-rational state. The postcolonial state operates through a different rationality: the display of governance alongside the practice of extraction. Understanding this does not excuse it. But it does explain why capacity building that ignores the political economy of governance produces trained officials within unchanged structures."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use the postcolony analysis to excuse corruption or poor governance.
**Why:** Mbembe is as critical of postcolonial elites as of colonial structures. Understanding the logic of the commandement does not mean accepting it. It means understanding what you are working within and against.

**Don't:** Assume all African states are identical postcolonies.
**Why:** The postcolony is a analytical concept, not a uniform description. South Africa, Botswana, Somalia, and DRC are all postcolonial but their specific formations of power differ dramatically. Attend to specificity.
