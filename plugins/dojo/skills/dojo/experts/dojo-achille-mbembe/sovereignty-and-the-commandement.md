# Sovereignty and the Commandement

## When to Use
- When analysing how sovereign authority operates in postcolonial contexts — through personalised power, spectacle, and the demand for submission rather than through bureaucratic procedure and democratic legitimation.
- When development programmes encounter governance patterns that cannot be understood through European political theory: the transferred official, the diverted budget, the policy that exists on paper but not in practice.
- When the question is about why governance reform persistently produces the forms of governance it was designed to replace.

## Core Concept
The commandement is Mbembe's term for the specific form of sovereign authority that characterises the postcolony. It is not simply authoritarianism — it is a mode of rule that operates through three intertwined mechanisms: spectacle (the display of power through ceremony, motorcades, uniforms, and public performance), arbitrariness (the exercise of authority that is deliberately unpredictable, because unpredictability is itself a form of power), and the demand for submission (the requirement that subjects demonstrate their subjection through participation in the spectacle — applauding, attending rallies, performing loyalty).

The commandement is not a deviation from proper governance. It is the colonial form of governance perpetuated after independence. The colonial state did not govern through consent, representation, or bureaucratic rationality. It governed through command — the direct exercise of force backed by racial supremacy and extractive purpose. When postcolonial elites inherited the colonial state, they inherited its mode of rule. The commandement is the colonial governor's authority exercised by the postcolonial president.

For development practice, the commandement explains why governance interventions modelled on Weberian bureaucracy — clear hierarchies, rule-bound procedure, merit-based appointment, separation of office from person — persistently fail. These interventions assume that the existing governance is a deficient version of bureaucratic rationality. It is not. It is a different form of governance with its own rationality: the accumulation and display of personal power, the distribution of patronage to maintain loyalty, the strategic use of unpredictability to prevent the consolidation of rival power bases. The commandement is not irrational — it is rational according to a logic that governance reform programmes refuse to acknowledge.

The relationship between the commandement and development is also direct: development resources — aid money, projects, technical assistance — become material for the commandement to appropriate, redistribute, and display. The new hospital is not primarily a health facility. It is a demonstration of the sovereign's capacity to deliver, to be seen delivering, to demand gratitude for delivering. This does not make the hospital useless. But it means the hospital operates within the logic of the commandement, not the logic of the health system plan.

## How to Apply
1. **Distinguish the commandement from corruption.** "Corruption implies a deviation from a norm. The commandement is the norm — it is the operating logic of governance, not a failure of governance. Calling it corruption misdiagnoses the problem as individual deviance rather than structural logic."
2. **Identify the spectacle.** "Where is power being displayed rather than exercised through procedure? The groundbreaking ceremony, the presidential visit, the branded project vehicle — these are not incidental. They are the commandement's mode of operation."
3. **Map the arbitrariness.** "Where are decisions unpredictable, and whose interests does unpredictability serve? The official transferred without explanation, the budget redirected without consultation — unpredictability is not incompetence. It is a technique of power."
4. **Assess whether the programme reinforces the commandement.** "Does the programme provide resources that the commandement can appropriate for its own purposes? Does it create visibility opportunities that serve the spectacle? If so, the programme is functioning within the commandement's logic regardless of its own intentions."

## Examples
**Situation:** A decentralisation programme has transferred decision-making authority and budgets to district governments. After three years, district officials report that all significant decisions still require approval from the provincial governor, who personally approves or blocks projects, transfers staff between districts without notice, and occasionally redirects district budgets to provincial priorities.
**Application:** "The programme has encountered the commandement. The governor is not failing to implement decentralisation — the governor is exercising sovereignty. Personal approval of decisions demonstrates that authority resides in the person, not the procedure. Staff transfers prevent district officials from building independent power bases. Budget redirections demonstrate that resources flow from the sovereign's will, not from bureaucratic allocation. The decentralisation framework assumes that authority derives from legal mandate and flows through institutional channels. The commandement operates differently: authority derives from the capacity to exercise it, and it flows through personal relationships. The programme designed a bureaucratic-rational system and installed it within a commandement. The commandement absorbed it. This does not mean decentralisation is impossible. But it means decentralisation that ignores the commandement will produce decentralised structures that operate according to the commandement's logic — which is precisely what has happened."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present the commandement as uniquely African.
**Why:** Personalised authority, spectacle, and arbitrary power exist in all political systems. The specificity of the commandement is its colonial genealogy and its relationship to the postcolonial state form — not some essential African characteristic.

**Don't:** Use the commandement analysis to argue that governance reform is futile.
**Why:** Understanding the logic of the commandement is the precondition for effective reform, not an argument against it. Reform that ignores the commandement will fail. Reform that engages with it — understanding the incentive structures, the power dynamics, the interests at stake — has a chance.
