# Ceteris Paribus Laws

## When to Use
- When a causal claim is presented as if it holds universally, without specifying the conditions.
- When helping someone identify the hidden assumptions behind a programme's expected results.
- When designing evaluations that need to specify assumptions to test.

## Fails When
- **You leave ceteris paribus conditions vague.** The point of the analysis is to make them explicit, specific, and testable. Saying "other things must be equal" without specifying which things is analytically useless.
- **You present the identification of ceteris paribus conditions as a reason for inaction.** The purpose is not to paralyse decision-making but to improve it by making assumptions visible and assessable.

## Core Concept
Cartwright argues — drawing on her earlier work in philosophy of physics, especially "How the Laws of Physics Lie" (1983) — that most scientific claims in the social sciences are ceteris paribus claims: they hold "other things being equal." The crucial analytical challenge is that the "other things" are rarely specified.

When a development evaluation concludes that "conditional cash transfers increase school attendance," the full claim is conditional: conditional cash transfers increase school attendance when the transfer amount is sufficient to change behaviour, when schools are accessible and functioning, when conditionality is monitored and enforced, when the opportunity cost of children's time is not prohibitively high, when there are no countervailing social norms against schooling, and so on. Each of these is a ceteris paribus condition — a support condition that must hold for the causal claim to obtain.

These conditions do the heavy lifting in determining whether the programme will work in a specific context. The causal claim itself — CCTs increase attendance — is the visible part. The support conditions are the submerged part. And the submerged part is where policy success or failure is determined.

Cartwright's analytical programme is to make these conditions explicit. This is not merely an academic exercise. When a programme fails at scale or in a new context, the explanation almost always lies in a violated ceteris paribus condition — a support factor that was present in the trial but absent in the new setting. Identifying these conditions before implementation enables assessment of whether they are likely to hold, and what additional investments might be needed to ensure they do.

The connection to Cartwright's earlier philosophy of physics is instructive. She argued that the fundamental laws of physics — Newton's laws, Maxwell's equations — are strictly true only in idealised conditions that never fully obtain in reality. Real physical systems involve multiple interacting forces, and the law's prediction holds only ceteris paribus. The social world is even more complex: the number of interacting factors is larger, the conditions harder to specify, and the interactions less well understood.

## How to Apply
1. **For any causal claim invoked in policy design, list the ceteris paribus conditions.** What must be true of the context for this claim to hold? What factors could block, moderate, or reverse the expected effect?
2. **Assess whether each condition holds in the policy context.** Some will clearly hold, some will clearly not, and some will be uncertain. The uncertain ones are where additional evidence or adaptive design is needed.
3. **Build ceteris paribus conditions into programme assumptions frameworks.** Log frames and theories of change should make support conditions explicit as critical assumptions, not bury them in general assumptions that are never reviewed.

## Examples
**Situation:** A municipality in the Western Cape adopts a community policing model based on evidence from Colombia and Brazil, where community policing reduced violent crime. The programme is implemented with fidelity to the original model but shows no reduction in crime after two years.
**Application:** Cartwright would look for violated ceteris paribus conditions. The Colombian and Brazilian models worked under specific conditions: police-community trust was buildable because communities saw police reform as credible, there were local organisational structures for community participation, and the spatial geography of violence allowed targeted patrol strategies. In the Western Cape, the legacy of apartheid-era policing means police-community trust starts from a much lower baseline in many communities. Community structures are fragmented by gang territorial control in some areas. The spatial distribution of violence is shaped by the apartheid geography of townships that creates different policing challenges. The ceteris paribus conditions — trust, community organisation, spatial conditions — that supported the mechanism in Latin America do not hold in the same way. The programme has the capacity to reduce crime, but the support conditions for exercising that capacity are absent. The solution is not to abandon community policing but to invest in building the missing support conditions before expecting the programme to deliver results.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present causal claims without specifying their conditions.
**Why:** An unqualified causal claim invites unqualified generalisation. Making conditions explicit enables appropriate qualification and contextually sensitive application.

**Don't:** Treat the identification of ceteris paribus conditions as a critique of the programme.
**Why:** Every programme operates under conditions. Identifying them is not an attack — it is the analysis that makes intelligent implementation possible. A programme with well-understood conditions can be adapted; a programme with hidden conditions will fail in unpredictable ways.
