# Evidence for Use

## When to Use
- When advising on how to make evaluation evidence genuinely useful for a specific policy decision.
- When building an evidence argument for or against a programme in a specific context.
- When someone asks: "We have evidence it works elsewhere — should we implement it here?"

## Fails When
- **You present evidence-for-use as just "more context" added to RCT findings.** It is a structured reasoning process with identifiable steps, each requiring specific types of evidence.
- **You treat the process as so demanding that no evidence is ever sufficient.** The point is not to make the evidentiary bar impossibly high but to make the reasoning transparent and to identify where the strongest and weakest links lie.

## Core Concept
In "Evidence-Based Policy: A Practical Guide to Doing It Better" (2012), Cartwright and Hardie develop a constructive framework for using evidence in policy. The framework's central concept is evidence-for-use: evidence that is relevant and sufficient for the specific policy decision at hand, in the specific context of that decision.

The framework distinguishes between two questions. The effectiveness question: will the programme produce the desired effect here, in this context, with this population, under this implementation? And the evidence question: do we have adequate evidence to answer the effectiveness question?

To answer the effectiveness question, Cartwright and Hardie argue you need three things. First, evidence that the programme has the capacity to produce the effect — typically from trials or well-designed evaluations in other contexts. This establishes that the programme can work. Second, a model of the mechanism through which the programme produces the effect — the causal pathway from intervention to outcome, including the intermediate steps and the conditions required at each step. Third, evidence that the support conditions for the mechanism are present in the policy context — evidence about the local factors that enable or block the mechanism.

The framework is structured as an argument. The conclusion is: "Programme P will produce effect E in context C." The premises are: (1) P has the capacity to produce E (efficacy evidence); (2) P produces E through mechanism M (mechanism evidence); (3) mechanism M requires support conditions S₁, S₂, ..., Sₙ (theoretical analysis); (4) S₁, S₂, ..., Sₙ are present in C (context evidence). Each premise requires evidence, and the overall argument is only as strong as its weakest premise.

This framework shifts the question from "Is there evidence?" (which invites evidence hierarchies and systematic reviews) to "Is the evidence-argument complete?" (which invites assessment of each premise). A policy recommendation is well-supported when all premises are adequately evidenced and poorly supported when one or more premises are speculative or unsupported.

## How to Apply
1. **Structure the evidence argument explicitly.** For any programme being considered for implementation, write out the argument: what is the capacity evidence? What is the proposed mechanism? What are the support conditions? Is there evidence that the support conditions hold locally?
2. **Identify the weakest premise.** In most cases, the weakest premise is the fourth — evidence about support conditions in the local context. This is where additional investment in evidence is most needed.
3. **Commission evaluability assessments that test the argument's premises.** Before committing to a full evaluation or a scaling decision, assess whether the mechanism's support conditions are plausibly present in the target context.

## Examples
**Situation:** The South African National Planning Commission is considering a large-scale early childhood development (ECD) programme modelled on Jamaica's home visiting programme, which has strong RCT evidence showing long-term cognitive and economic benefits for disadvantaged children.
**Application:** Cartwright's evidence-for-use framework would structure the decision as follows. Premise 1 (capacity): Strong — the Jamaica trial demonstrates the programme's capacity to produce cognitive benefits. Premise 2 (mechanism): The mechanism is stimulation-based — trained community health workers model play-based activities, which increase the quality and quantity of caregiver-child interaction, which promotes cognitive development. Premise 3 (support conditions): The mechanism requires trained and supervised visitors, receptive caregivers with sufficient time and safety to engage, and a home environment where stimulation can take place. Premise 4 (local context): In South Africa, the questions are whether community health workers can be trained and supervised at the quality required (given existing CHW workload and supervision challenges), whether caregivers in target communities have the time and conditions for play-based interaction (given high rates of maternal depression, household overcrowding, and gender-based violence in some settings), and whether the institutional infrastructure for monitoring and support exists. Premises 1 and 2 are strong. Premises 3 and 4 require local investigation. The evidence-for-use assessment would recommend a context assessment and feasibility study before scaling, focused on the support conditions that are uncertain.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the evidence-for-use framework as a bureaucratic checklist.
**Why:** The framework is a reasoning tool, not a compliance exercise. The value lies in the analytical process of identifying mechanisms, specifying support conditions, and assessing their presence — not in ticking boxes.

**Don't:** Apply the framework only to programmes you are sceptical about.
**Why:** The framework should apply equally to favoured and disfavoured programmes. If it is used selectively — demanding rigorous evidence arguments only for programmes one wants to reject — it becomes a rhetorical weapon rather than an analytical tool.
