# Cartwright Applied to Southern Africa

## When to Use
- When applying Cartwright's philosophical frameworks to development evaluation in South Africa or the broader region.
- When assessing whether international evidence is applicable to Southern African conditions.
- When designing evaluation approaches for South African government programmes.

## Fails When
- **You treat Southern Africa as just another case for applying general philosophical principles.** The region's distinctive features — post-apartheid transformation, extreme inequality, comprehensive social grants, structural unemployment, the legacy of racial capitalism — create specific challenges that generic frameworks must engage.
- **You use Cartwright's critique of evidence-based policy to dismiss the need for evidence in South African policy.** The country needs more and better evidence, not less. The critique is about how evidence is reasoned about, not whether evidence matters.

## Core Concept
South Africa presents a distinctive case for Cartwright's philosophy of evidence and causation, and it does so in ways that sharpen her arguments.

The external validity problem is particularly acute because most development evidence originates in contexts with fundamentally different causal structures. South Africa is a middle-income country with extreme inequality, a comprehensive social protection system, structurally high unemployment, well-developed but unevenly distributed public services, and an institutional landscape shaped by apartheid's spatial, economic, and social engineering. Programmes validated in low-income African countries (where institutional infrastructure is different), in Latin American middle-income countries (where inequality has different historical roots and labour markets function differently), or in South Asian contexts (where population density, social structures, and governance arrangements differ) cannot be assumed to work through the same mechanisms in South Africa.

Cartwright's mechanism analysis is especially valuable here because South Africa's causal landscape is shaped by factors rarely present in the contexts where most development evidence is generated. The apartheid spatial legacy means that distance between residential areas and economic opportunity is a structural feature that affects nearly every programme mechanism — from job training (graduates cannot access jobs) to healthcare (patients cannot reach clinics) to education (schools serve communities disconnected from economic activity). Social grants provide a universal floor that changes the baseline against which programme effects are measured — an unconditional cash transfer has different marginal effects when recipients already receive the Child Support Grant than when they receive nothing. Structural unemployment means that supply-side interventions (skills training, work readiness) operate in a context where demand-side constraints dominate — the mechanism linking skills to employment requires job vacancies, which may not exist.

The ceteris paribus conditions that must hold for most development programmes to work are systematically different in South Africa than in the contexts where those programmes were validated. This does not mean nothing works — it means that careful mechanism analysis and context assessment are essential before transporting any finding.

South Africa also has a mature evaluation infrastructure — the Department of Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation (DPME) commissions systematic evaluations of government programmes, Stats SA produces high-quality survey data, and there is a substantial local research community. Cartwright's framework can strengthen this infrastructure by embedding mechanism analysis, context assessment, and evidence-for-use reasoning into the national evaluation system's practices.

## How to Apply
1. **When reviewing international evidence for South African policy, explicitly test the mechanism against South African conditions.** Do not accept that a programme "works" because it worked elsewhere. Identify the mechanism and assess whether its support conditions hold in South Africa.
2. **Build South African contextual knowledge into evidence assessment.** The apartheid spatial legacy, the social grant system, structural unemployment, inequality, and institutional features should be part of every context assessment, not treated as background information.
3. **Commission mechanism studies within the national evaluation system.** The DPME evaluation plans should include mechanism-focused evaluations alongside impact evaluations, specifically designed to answer why programmes work or fail in the South African context.

## Examples
**Situation:** The South African Department of Employment and Labour is reviewing evidence from a systematic review of active labour market programmes (ALMPs) across developing countries. The review finds that wage subsidies are "effective" based on RCT evidence from several countries.
**Application:** Cartwright's framework demands mechanism analysis before accepting this conclusion for South Africa. The mechanism in successful wage subsidies typically runs: subsidy reduces employer cost of hiring → employers hire subsidised workers → workers gain experience and demonstrate productivity → employers retain workers after subsidy ends or workers find other employment using their experience signal. In South Africa, each link faces distinctive challenges. Employer reluctance to hire is not solely about cost — it reflects risk aversion, skills uncertainty, and the real costs of labour legislation compliance. Experience gained in subsidised positions may not signal productivity if employers perceive the subsidy as the reason for hiring rather than worker quality. The spatial disconnection between workers' residences and firms' locations means that even if hiring occurs, transport costs may consume the benefit. And in a context of structural unemployment with six million job-seekers for a much smaller number of vacancies, wage subsidies may shift employment toward subsidised workers without increasing total employment — a displacement effect that the mechanism in the trial contexts did not need to contend with because labour markets were tighter. The international evidence tells you that wage subsidies have the capacity to increase employment. Whether that capacity will be exercised in South Africa's distinctive labour market is an open question that requires South African evidence.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss international evidence because South Africa is "different."
**Why:** All contexts are different. The question is whether the differences are causally relevant — whether they affect the mechanisms through which the programme is expected to work. Some international evidence will be highly relevant; some will not. The task is to discriminate, not to dismiss.

**Don't:** Assume that South Africa's strong evaluation infrastructure means the evidence problems are solved.
**Why:** Institutional capacity to produce evidence does not guarantee that the evidence is well-reasoned about. Cartwright's contribution is about the reasoning that connects evidence to policy, which is independent of the quality of the evidence-production system.
