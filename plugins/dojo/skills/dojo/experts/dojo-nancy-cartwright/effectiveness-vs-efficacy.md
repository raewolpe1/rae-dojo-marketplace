# Effectiveness vs Efficacy

## When to Use
- When distinguishing between what a programme can do under ideal conditions and what it does do under real-world conditions.
- When advising on the implications of trial results for scaling decisions.
- When explaining why a programme that succeeded in a pilot fails at scale.

## Fails When
- **You reduce the gap to implementation quality.** The efficacy-effectiveness gap is not just about whether the programme is implemented well. It is about whether the conditions that supported the result in the trial are present in the real-world setting — conditions that go far beyond programme fidelity.
- **You treat efficacy evidence as sufficient for scaling decisions.** Efficacy establishes proof of concept. Scaling requires effectiveness evidence, which addresses a fundamentally different question.

## Core Concept
Cartwright borrows the efficacy/effectiveness distinction from medicine and applies it to social policy with added philosophical precision. Efficacy is demonstrated when a programme produces its intended effect under controlled conditions — typically in a trial with carefully selected participants, well-resourced implementers, and close monitoring. Effectiveness is demonstrated when a programme produces its intended effect under real-world conditions — with typical populations, government implementers, routine budgets, and minimal researcher oversight.

The gap between efficacy and effectiveness is not a minor implementation detail. It is, for Cartwright, the central problem of evidence-based policy. The conditions that support efficacy in a trial include: researcher selection of motivated implementers, training and supervision levels far above what government systems provide, Hawthorne effects from observation, selection of compliant or motivated participants, and exclusion of complex cases. These conditions are part of the trial, not part of the programme, and they do not travel when the programme is scaled.

More fundamentally, the gap reflects the difference between demonstrating a capacity and exercising it. An efficacy trial demonstrates that the programme has the capacity to produce the effect — it can work. Whether it will work in a specific real-world context depends on whether the support conditions for exercising that capacity are present. These support conditions include not just programme fidelity but also institutional capacity, population characteristics, complementary services, political support, and the absence of countervailing forces.

Cartwright argues that the evidence-based policy movement systematically conflates efficacy and effectiveness. When a systematic review concludes that a programme "works" based on trial evidence, it has established efficacy. The leap to the policy conclusion — we should implement this programme — requires effectiveness evidence that is rarely available and seldom demanded.

## How to Apply
1. **Label evidence as efficacy or effectiveness evidence.** When citing trial results, be explicit that they establish efficacy — proof that the programme can work — not effectiveness — evidence that it does work under typical conditions.
2. **Design effectiveness studies alongside or after efficacy trials.** Before scaling, evaluate the programme under conditions that approximate real-world implementation. This means typical implementers, typical budgets, minimal researcher involvement, and representative populations.
3. **Map the conditions that differ between the trial and the implementation context.** These differences are the efficacy-effectiveness gap. Assess which differences are likely to matter for the causal mechanism and which are likely to be benign.

## Examples
**Situation:** A youth development programme piloted in three urban sites in Gauteng shows strong positive effects on youth employability in an RCT. The pilot was implemented by a well-resourced NGO with trained facilitators, small group sizes, and intensive mentoring. The national government proposes to scale the programme through the Department of Social Development, using existing community development workers as facilitators.
**Application:** Cartwright would identify a systematic efficacy-effectiveness gap. The pilot conditions differed from the scaling conditions on nearly every dimension relevant to the mechanism. The mechanism — intensive, skilled facilitation building employability competencies through sustained engagement — requires: skilled facilitators (the NGO's trained staff versus overburdened community development workers), manageable caseloads (small pilot groups versus government's requirement to reach large numbers), sustained engagement (the pilot's intensive schedule versus government's intermittent delivery), and accountability systems (the NGO's performance management versus government's compliance-focused monitoring). Each of these differences threatens the mechanism. The efficacy finding tells you the programme can work; it does not tell you that it will work under government implementation conditions. The scaling decision requires effectiveness evidence — ideally from a government-implemented pilot — not extrapolation from an NGO-implemented trial.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that "implementing with fidelity" closes the efficacy-effectiveness gap.
**Why:** Fidelity to the programme model is necessary but not sufficient. The gap also includes the conditions external to the programme that supported the trial result — characteristics of the implementers, participants, institutional environment, and complementary services that may not be replicable.

**Don't:** Dismiss efficacy evidence as irrelevant because "real-world conditions are different."
**Why:** Efficacy evidence is valuable — it establishes proof of concept and identifies a mechanism that can produce the desired effect. The error is treating it as sufficient for scaling, not treating it as useless.
