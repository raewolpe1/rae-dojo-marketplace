# Evidence-Based Policy Critique

## When to Use
- When someone invokes "evidence-based" as a quality seal rather than a reasoning process.
- When evaluating the logic behind evidence hierarchies that rank methods by type.
- When advising on how to build genuinely evidence-informed policy systems.

## Fails When
- **You frame the critique as anti-evidence.** Cartwright is not against evidence. She is against bad reasoning about evidence — specifically, the hidden logical leaps in moving from "the evidence shows X" to "we should do Y."
- **You present this as a purely academic debate.** The logical errors Cartwright identifies have real policy consequences: programmes adopted because they "worked" elsewhere fail when transported to contexts where the causal mechanism does not operate.

## Core Concept
Cartwright's critique of evidence-based policy, developed most fully in "Evidence-Based Policy: A Practical Guide to Doing It Better" (with Jeremy Hardie, 2012), targets the logical structure of the movement, not its aspirations. The aspiration — that policy should be informed by evidence — is unimpeachable. The problem is in the logic that connects evidence to policy.

The standard evidence-based policy model runs: identify what works (through systematic reviews of high-quality evidence, principally RCTs) → implement what works → achieve desired outcomes. Cartwright identifies three hidden logical problems.

First, "what works" is ambiguous. It might mean: the programme worked in the study context (internal validity), the programme works in general (a regularity claim), or the programme will work here (an external validity prediction). These are different claims requiring different evidence. The evidence-based policy model slides between them without distinguishing them.

Second, evidence hierarchies rank evidence by method type (RCTs at the top, observational studies below, qualitative evidence at the bottom) rather than by relevance to the policy question. An RCT that tests the wrong programme in the wrong context is ranked above a well-designed case study that tests the right programme in the right context. Form trumps relevance, which is logically backwards.

Third, the model assumes that once you know "what works," implementation follows. But knowing that a programme can work does not tell you how to make it work in a specific context. Implementation requires understanding mechanisms, support conditions, institutional capacity, and local context — none of which are provided by the efficacy evidence alone.

Cartwright's alternative is not to abandon evidence but to demand better reasoning about it. Evidence for policy requires: evidence that the programme has the capacity to produce the desired effect (efficacy), evidence about the mechanism through which it produces the effect (mechanism analysis), and evidence that the support conditions for the mechanism are present in the policy context (context assessment).

## How to Apply
1. **When reviewing evidence for a policy decision, assess relevance before rigour.** Ask: does this evidence bear on the specific decision we are making, in the specific context we are making it? Then assess methodological quality within the set of relevant evidence.
2. **Replace "what works" with "what works, for whom, in what conditions, through what mechanism."** The unqualified question has no useful answer. The qualified question is answerable and policy-relevant.
3. **Build evidence systems that collect three types of evidence.** Efficacy evidence (can it work?), mechanism evidence (why does it work?), and context evidence (will the conditions for it to work hold here?).

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's Department of Basic Education (DBE) is reviewing international evidence on school feeding programmes. Systematic reviews rated as "high quality" show significant effects on attendance and learning in studies from India, Ghana, and Kenya. The DBE proposes to expand the National School Nutrition Programme (NSNP) based on this evidence.
**Application:** Cartwright would point out that the evidence establishes that school feeding has the capacity to improve attendance and learning — it can work. But the mechanism in the cited studies was that feeding addressed hunger that was the binding constraint on attendance and concentration. In South Africa, the NSNP already reaches over 9 million learners; the question is not whether to feed but whether the current feeding programme is reaching the right children and whether hunger is still the binding constraint in contexts where feeding is already provided. The international evidence is irrelevant to this question. What the DBE needs is evidence about the mechanism in the South African context — is hunger the constraint on learning for the children who are currently underperforming? — and evidence about implementation quality — is the NSNP actually delivering adequate nutrition at the point of consumption? The systematic review answers a question the DBE is not asking.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use "evidence-based" as a rhetorical device to close debate.
**Why:** Claiming that a policy is "evidence-based" often functions as a way to avoid engaging with objections. But evidence does not determine policy — it informs it. Reasonable people can disagree about what policy follows from the same evidence, because policy involves values, trade-offs, and contextual judgments that evidence alone cannot resolve.

**Don't:** Respond to Cartwright's critique by demanding "more RCTs."
**Why:** The critique is not that we have too few RCTs. It is that the logical framework connecting evidence to policy is flawed. More RCTs do not fix a logical error. Better reasoning about evidence — including evidence from many methods — is the solution.
