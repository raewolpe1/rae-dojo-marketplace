# Theory and Evidence

## When to Use
- When someone claims that "the data speaks for itself" or that evidence points directly to a conclusion.
- When discussing theory-based evaluation and its relationship to empirical evidence.
- When helping someone understand why the same data can support different conclusions depending on the theoretical framework.

## Fails When
- **You use theory-dependence to argue that all interpretations are equally valid.** They are not. Some theoretical frameworks are better supported, more coherent, and more productive than others. Theory-dependence means evidence must be interpreted, not that all interpretations are equal.
- **You present theory and evidence as opposed rather than complementary.** Theory without evidence is speculation; evidence without theory is noise. The productive relationship is mutual constraint and illumination.

## Core Concept
Cartwright, drawing on a long tradition in philosophy of science (Duhem, Quine, Kuhn, and beyond), argues that evidence does not speak for itself. Every piece of evidence must be interpreted — and interpretation requires a theoretical framework that specifies what the evidence means, what it is evidence for, and how it relates to the question at hand.

Consider a simple example. An evaluation finds that a job training programme had no statistically significant effect on employment. What does this evidence mean? Under one theoretical framework (the programme was correctly designed and implemented, and the mechanism should have operated), it means the programme approach does not work. Under another framework (the mechanism is sound but the implementation was compromised), it means the programme was not properly tested. Under a third framework (the mechanism requires labour market conditions that were absent during the evaluation period), it means the conditions for the programme to work were not met. The same evidence — null effect — supports different conclusions under different theoretical interpretations.

This does not mean that evidence is meaningless or that anything goes. The theoretical frameworks are themselves subject to evidential assessment. The point is that the relationship between evidence and conclusion always passes through interpretation, and making the interpretation explicit is essential for honest reasoning.

For evaluation practice, this means that the theory of change is not just a planning tool — it is the interpretive framework through which evaluation findings acquire meaning. A null result with a well-specified theory of change is informative: it enables diagnosis of where the causal chain broke. A null result without a theory of change is a dead end: you know the programme did not work but you do not know why, and therefore you do not know what to change.

Cartwright extends this argument to evidence synthesis. Systematic reviews aggregate findings across studies as if the findings are self-interpreting. But different studies may use different measures, different theoretical frameworks, and different definitions of the outcome. Pooling their results without attending to these differences produces a precise estimate of an ill-defined quantity.

## How to Apply
1. **Make the interpretive framework explicit in evaluation reports.** State the theoretical assumptions under which the findings are interpreted, and indicate how the conclusions would change under alternative assumptions.
2. **Use theories of change as interpretive tools, not just planning documents.** A theory of change specifies the expected causal chain, and evaluation should assess each link — not just the final outcome. This enables diagnostic interpretation of results.
3. **When evidence is ambiguous, explore multiple interpretations rather than selecting the one that fits prior commitments.** Honest analysis acknowledges interpretive uncertainty.

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluation of a land reform programme in the Eastern Cape finds that beneficiaries' agricultural income has not increased five years after receiving land. The evaluators conclude: "Land reform has failed to improve livelihoods."
**Application:** Cartwright would challenge the interpretive leap. The evidence — no income increase — is consistent with multiple theoretical interpretations. Under a market failure theory, the programme failed because beneficiaries lacked access to credit, inputs, extension services, and markets that would have enabled productive use of the land. Under an institutional theory, communal tenure arrangements on the redistributed land created investment uncertainty that discouraged agricultural development. Under a structural theory, the quality and location of the land transferred was insufficient to generate viable farming operations. Each interpretation implies a different policy response: credit provision, tenure reform, or better land selection. The conclusion "land reform failed" collapses these distinctions into a single verdict that obscures the diagnostic information the evidence contains. The evaluators should present the evidence alongside the competing interpretations and the additional evidence that would discriminate between them.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Claim that "the evidence is clear" when the interpretation is contested.
**Why:** Clarity of evidence and clarity of interpretation are different things. The data may be unambiguous while its meaning is not. Asserting that the evidence is clear when the interpretation is debatable is a rhetorical move, not an analytical one.

**Don't:** Dismiss theory-based evaluation as "not rigorous" compared to experimental evaluation.
**Why:** Theory-based evaluation and experimental evaluation answer different questions with different types of rigour. Theory-based evaluation provides interpretive rigour — the rigour of understanding why something works or fails. Experimental evaluation provides estimation rigour — the rigour of quantifying effects. Both are forms of rigour; neither is sufficient alone.
