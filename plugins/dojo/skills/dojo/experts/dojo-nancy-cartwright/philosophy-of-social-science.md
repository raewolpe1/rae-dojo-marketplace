# Philosophy of Social Science

## When to Use
- When discussing the nature and limits of social scientific knowledge.
- When someone invokes natural science analogies to justify social science methods.
- When advising on what "rigour" means in the context of development evaluation.

## Fails When
- **You present Cartwright as arguing that social science is hopeless or unscientific.** She argues that it is different from natural science in ways that matter for method, not that it is inferior.
- **You reduce the philosophy of social science to abstract theorising disconnected from practice.** Cartwright's philosophy is relentlessly practical — the philosophical distinctions she draws have direct implications for how evaluation is designed and how evidence is used.

## Core Concept
Cartwright's work in philosophy of social science builds on her earlier philosophy of physics, particularly the arguments in "How the Laws of Physics Lie" (1983) and "The Dappled World" (1999). Her central claim is that the world — including the social world — is dappled: governed not by universal laws but by a patchwork of regularities, capacities, and mechanisms that operate differently in different domains and contexts.

In the social sciences, this dappled character is more pronounced than in physics. Social regularities are deeply context-dependent: they hold under specific institutional, cultural, historical, and political conditions. The ceteris paribus clauses that qualify social science claims are longer, more complex, and harder to specify than those in the natural sciences. And the mechanisms through which social causes produce effects involve human agency, interpretation, and strategic behaviour in ways that physical mechanisms do not.

This does not make social science impossible. It makes it different. The tools of natural science — universal laws, controlled experiments, mathematical models — can be adapted for social science use, but the adaptation must respect the distinctive features of the social domain. Universal laws become ceteris paribus generalisations. Controlled experiments become context-specific efficacy demonstrations. Mathematical models become approximate representations that require constant calibration against local conditions.

For evaluation specifically, the implication is that the aspiration to produce context-free, universally applicable knowledge about "what works" is misguided. Social programmes work through mechanisms that operate within institutional and cultural contexts. Knowledge about these programmes is inevitably contextual, and the aspiration should be to produce knowledge that is transparent about its conditions of application rather than knowledge that claims universal validity.

Cartwright's philosophy of social science thus supports methodological pluralism — the use of multiple methods matched to the specific questions at hand — rather than methodological hierarchy — the ranking of methods by a single criterion of rigour. Different methods capture different aspects of social reality, and the most complete understanding comes from combining them.

## How to Apply
1. **Resist the temptation to rank social science methods by analogy with experimental physics.** The experimental method is one tool among many in the social sciences, valuable for what it reveals and limited in what it cannot.
2. **Embrace the contextuality of social science knowledge.** Instead of seeking context-free findings, seek findings that are explicit about their conditions of application. This is more modest but more honest and more useful.
3. **Design evaluations that match the complexity of the social reality under study.** Simple interventions in simple contexts may be amenable to simple methods. Complex interventions in complex contexts require methods that can capture that complexity — mixed methods, theory-based approaches, and attention to emergence and adaptation.

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor agency issues a call for proposals to evaluate a governance reform programme in three Southern African countries. The call specifies that the evaluation must use "rigorous methods, preferably experimental or quasi-experimental designs."
**Application:** Cartwright would challenge the assumption that rigour is synonymous with experimental design. Governance reform is a complex, system-level intervention operating through institutional mechanisms — changes in rules, incentives, norms, and power relations. These mechanisms are deeply context-dependent: the same formal rules produce different outcomes depending on informal institutions, political dynamics, and social structures. An experimental design is poorly suited to this object of study — you cannot randomise governance reforms, the relevant outcomes are systemic rather than individual, the time horizons are long, and the causal pathways are multiple and interacting. Rigour in this context means: specifying the theory of change with precision, identifying the mechanisms through which reform is expected to produce effects, collecting evidence on whether those mechanisms are operating, and assessing the institutional and political conditions that enable or block them. This requires process tracing, institutional analysis, comparative case studies, and theory-based evaluation — methods that are rigorous in the sense that matters for this question, even though they are not experimental. The donor's equation of rigour with experimental design reflects a philosophy of science that does not fit the object of study.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Invoke the complexity of the social world as a reason to abandon systematic evidence.
**Why:** Complexity makes evidence more necessary, not less. The challenge is to develop methods that can capture relevant complexity, not to retreat into impressionism.

**Don't:** Assume that the methods of natural science are automatically the gold standard for social science.
**Why:** The natural and social sciences study different kinds of objects. Methods must be appropriate to the object of study. Importing methods from physics into development evaluation without attending to the differences between physical and social causation can produce precise answers to the wrong questions.
