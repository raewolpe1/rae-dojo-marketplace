# Cartwright and Evaluation Practice

## When to Use
- When translating Cartwright's philosophical arguments into practical guidance for evaluators.
- When designing evaluations that address mechanism, context, and support conditions.
- When reviewing evaluation terms of reference, designs, or reports with philosophical rigour.

## Fails When
- **You present the philosophical insights as too abstract for practitioners.** Cartwright's ideas have direct, specific implications for how evaluations are designed, conducted, and used. The practical applications are concrete.
- **You suggest that incorporating Cartwright's ideas requires abandoning conventional evaluation methods.** The ideas complement and strengthen conventional methods; they do not replace them.

## Core Concept
Cartwright's philosophy translates into evaluation practice through several concrete implications.

First, evaluation questions should be specified with precision about the type of causal claim being investigated. "Does the programme work?" is ambiguous. "Does the programme have the capacity to produce the intended effect?" (an efficacy question), "Is the programme currently producing the intended effect under implementation conditions?" (an effectiveness question), and "Through what mechanism does the programme produce its effects?" (a mechanism question) are three distinct evaluation questions requiring different designs.

Second, theories of change should be treated as testable hypotheses about mechanisms, not as planning documents to be filed and forgotten. Each link in the theory of change represents a causal claim with its own ceteris paribus conditions. Evaluation should test each link, not just the overall input-to-outcome relationship. When an evaluation finds that the overall effect is null, testing intermediate links enables diagnosis of where the causal chain broke.

Third, evaluations should explicitly assess support conditions — the factors external to the programme that must hold for the mechanism to operate. This requires evaluability assessment before evaluation: is the mechanism plausibly operating? Are the support conditions present? If not, the evaluation will confirm programme failure without illuminating whether the programme was flawed or whether the context was inhospitable.

Fourth, evidence synthesis should be organised around mechanisms, not just around effect sizes. A systematic review that pools effect sizes across contexts without attending to mechanisms is averaging across different causal processes. A mechanism-based synthesis groups studies by the causal pathway they tested and assesses under what conditions that pathway operates — which is the information policymakers need.

Fifth, evaluation reports should distinguish clearly between what the evidence establishes (the finding), what interpretation the evidence supports under specified assumptions (the conclusion), and what the conclusion implies for policy given stated values and constraints (the recommendation). These are different intellectual operations, and conflating them reduces the transparency and usefulness of evaluation.

## How to Apply
1. **In evaluation design, specify the causal question type and match the method to it.** Build this specification into terms of reference as a required component of evaluation proposals.
2. **Include mechanism testing in all programme evaluations.** At minimum, measure intermediate outcomes along the theory of change. Ideally, include process evaluation components that can identify whether and how the mechanism is operating.
3. **Conduct context assessments before full evaluations.** Evaluability assessments should test whether the support conditions for the programme's mechanism are plausibly present. If they are not, the evaluation may need to be redesigned or the programme adjusted before evaluation is worthwhile.
4. **In evaluation reports, separate findings from interpretations from recommendations.** Make the reasoning transparent at each step.

## Examples
**Situation:** A South African provincial Department of Health commissions an evaluation of its community-based chronic disease management programme. The terms of reference request an evaluation to determine "whether the programme is effective in improving health outcomes for people with chronic diseases."
**Application:** Cartwright-informed practice would restructure the evaluation around specified causal questions. Question 1 (mechanism): Through what pathway is the programme expected to improve health outcomes? The theory of change posits: community health workers conduct home visits → patients receive adherence counselling and monitoring → medication adherence improves → clinical indicators improve → health outcomes improve. Question 2 (support conditions): What must hold for this mechanism to operate? CHWs must be trained and supervised, patients must be reachable, medication must be consistently available, CHW caseloads must be manageable, and referral pathways to clinics must function. Question 3 (effectiveness): Is the mechanism producing the expected effect under current implementation conditions? The evaluation design should include: a process evaluation testing each link in the causal chain (are home visits happening? is adherence counselling being delivered? is medication available?); a context assessment of support conditions (CHW training quality, supervision frequency, medicine supply chain, caseload ratios); and an outcome evaluation measuring both intermediate outcomes (adherence, clinical indicators) and final outcomes (health status). This design produces far more policy-useful evidence than a simple outcome evaluation, because when an effect is absent, the mechanism and context evidence tells you why and what to fix.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat theory of change as a box-ticking exercise completed at programme design and never revisited.
**Why:** The theory of change is the evaluation's interpretive framework. It should be tested, refined, and used to interpret findings. A theory of change that sits unexamined in a logframe is analytically useless.

**Don't:** Commission impact evaluations without process evaluation components.
**Why:** An impact evaluation that finds a null effect but cannot explain why provides no guidance for programme improvement. The mechanism and context evidence provided by process evaluation is what makes impact findings actionable.
