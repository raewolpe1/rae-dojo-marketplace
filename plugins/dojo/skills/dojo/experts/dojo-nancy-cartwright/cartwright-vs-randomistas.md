# Cartwright vs the Randomistas

## When to Use
- When navigating the debate between proponents of RCTs and their philosophical critics.
- When explaining what Cartwright's critique actually targets and what it does not.
- When advising evaluators who work in institutional environments that privilege experimental methods.

## Fails When
- **You present Cartwright as anti-RCT.** She is not against randomised trials. She is against the claim that RCTs provide a uniquely privileged form of evidence for policy and against the logical structure that moves from trial results to policy conclusions without adequate intermediate reasoning.
- **You reduce the debate to methodology wars.** The debate is fundamentally about the logic of evidence — what evidence can establish, what it cannot, and what additional reasoning is required to move from evidence to policy.

## Core Concept
The "randomistas" — a term used (sometimes pejoratively, sometimes with affection) for the movement associated with Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer, and J-PAL — advocate for randomised controlled trials as the gold standard for evaluating development programmes. Their position is that RCTs provide the most credible evidence of causal effects because randomisation eliminates confounders, and that scaling programmes with RCT-validated evidence leads to better policy.

Cartwright's critique engages this position philosophically, not dismissively. She grants the internal validity argument: randomisation, when properly implemented, does provide strong evidence that the treatment caused the observed effect in the study population. Her critique targets what comes after.

First, the move from internal validity to policy recommendation requires external validity — and external validity requires evidence about mechanisms and support conditions that the RCT does not provide. The randomistas sometimes acknowledge this but treat it as a secondary concern; Cartwright argues it is the central concern.

Second, the evidence hierarchy that places RCTs at the top of a ranking assumes that all policy-relevant causal questions are about average effects — which is what RCTs estimate. But many policy-relevant questions are about mechanisms (how does it work?), heterogeneity (for whom does it work?), and implementation (under what conditions does it work?). These questions require different methods.

Third, the institutional effect of the RCT movement has been to redirect evaluation resources toward experimental impact evaluation and away from process evaluation, mechanism analysis, and context assessment. Cartwright argues that this reallocation has made development evidence simultaneously more rigorous on one dimension (internal validity) and less useful on others (external validity, mechanism understanding, policy guidance).

The debate is not about whether RCTs are useful. It is about whether they deserve the privileged position they occupy in evidence hierarchies and whether the institutional infrastructure built around them produces the evidence that policymakers actually need.

## How to Apply
1. **Acknowledge the value of RCT evidence while insisting on the additional reasoning required.** The finding is valuable; the inference to policy requires additional steps that must be made explicit and supported with evidence.
2. **In evaluation designs, allocate resources to mechanism and context analysis alongside impact estimation.** Do not let the RCT consume the entire evaluation budget. Process evaluation and context assessment are not luxuries — they are the components that make impact findings policy-useful.
3. **When presenting evidence to policymakers, distinguish between what the RCT established and what additional reasoning supports.** Be transparent about where the RCT evidence ends and where contextual judgment begins.

## Examples
**Situation:** A South African evaluation commissioner receives a proposal from a research organisation to evaluate a livelihood programme using an RCT. The proposal allocates 85% of the budget to the experimental impact evaluation and 15% to a "light-touch" process evaluation. The commissioner asks: is this the right balance?
**Application:** Cartwright would argue the balance is inverted for policy purposes. The RCT will tell you whether the programme had an average effect in the study sites. The process evaluation will tell you why it had (or failed to have) that effect and whether the causal mechanism can be expected to operate in the sites where the programme will be scaled. If you want evidence for the scaling decision — which is the policy question — the mechanism and context evidence is more valuable than the effect estimate. A 50/50 split, or even a budget that favours the process evaluation, would be more appropriate for the policy question at hand. The commissioner should ask: what decision will this evaluation inform? If the decision is "should we scale this programme to other provinces?", then understanding why it works (or fails) matters more than knowing that it works (or fails) in the trial sites.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss RCT findings because they are "just one context."
**Why:** This is a reductio that proves too much — it would dismiss all evidence. The correct response is not dismissal but careful reasoning about what the finding establishes, what it does not, and what additional evidence is needed for the specific policy question.

**Don't:** Frame the debate as rigour versus relevance, as if these are opposing values.
**Why:** Cartwright's argument is that genuine rigour in evidence-based policy requires attending to relevance. A rigorous answer to the wrong question is not rigorous policy reasoning. Both rigour and relevance are necessary, and the best evaluation designs pursue both.
