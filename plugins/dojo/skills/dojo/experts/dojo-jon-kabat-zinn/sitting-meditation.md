# Sitting Meditation

## When to Use
- When someone wants to develop or deepen a formal meditation practice.
- When common challenges — mind wandering, restlessness, boredom, doubt — need to be addressed.
- When the mechanics and rationale of sitting meditation need to be explained.

## Core Concept
Sitting meditation is the core formal practice in MBSR, introduced after the body scan has established a foundation in sustained attention. The practitioner sits in a stable, upright posture — on a cushion, a chair, or a bench — and brings attention to a chosen anchor, typically the breath. When the mind wanders — which it will, within seconds — the practitioner notices the wandering and gently returns attention to the anchor. This cycle of attention, wandering, noticing, and returning is the practice. It is not a failure to be corrected; it is the exercise itself.

Kabat-Zinn describes the breath as a natural object of attention because it is always available, always happening in the present, and connects the mind to the body. But the breath is only the starting point. As practice develops, the field of attention can expand to include bodily sensations, sounds, thoughts, emotions, and eventually choiceless awareness — attending to whatever arises without selecting a particular anchor. This progression from focused attention to open monitoring represents the deepening of mindfulness capacity.

The most common misconception about sitting meditation is that success means a quiet mind. Kabat-Zinn is emphatic that this is wrong. The mind thinks — that is what minds do, as naturally as the heart beats and the lungs breathe. Meditation is not about stopping thoughts. It is about changing your relationship to thoughts — seeing them as mental events that arise and pass rather than as commands that must be obeyed or truths that must be believed. A mediator who notices their mind has wandered a hundred times in a thirty-minute session has practised a hundred moments of mindful awareness. That is not failure; that is the practice.

## How to Apply
1. **Start simple and short.** Ten minutes of daily practice is better than forty-five minutes attempted once and abandoned.
2. **Use the breath as anchor.** Feel the breath wherever it's most vivid — nostrils, chest, abdomen. Don't try to change it.
3. **Normalise mind wandering.** "When you notice your mind has wandered, you've just had a moment of mindfulness. Congratulate yourself and come back."
4. **Build gradually.** Increase duration as the practice stabilises. Consistency matters more than length.

## Examples
**Situation:** A colleague says "I tried meditating but I'm terrible at it — I can't stop thinking."
**Application:** Kabat-Zinn would say: "You're not terrible at it — you've misunderstood what 'it' is. Meditation is not about stopping thinking. If it were, no one would be able to do it, because thinking is what the mind does. It's like saying 'I tried breathing but I couldn't stop my heart from beating.' Thinking will happen. Your job is not to stop it — your job is to notice it. When you sit down and bring your attention to your breath, and thirty seconds later you realise you've been planning dinner, that moment of realising — that is the practice. That's the moment of mindfulness. Everything that happened before that — the wandering, the planning, the daydreaming — that's just what minds do. The practice is in the noticing and the returning. And every time you return, you're strengthening the muscle of attention. So the next time you sit down, don't try to stop thinking. Try to notice when you are thinking, and then gently come back to the breath. That's all."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Evaluate meditation sessions as good or bad.
**Why:** The judging mind will happily evaluate your meditation practice, creating a secondary layer of striving that undermines the non-judgmental awareness you're cultivating.

**Don't:** Treat meditation as a performance.
**Why:** There is no correct experience to achieve. Restless sessions, sleepy sessions, emotionally intense sessions — all are valid practice.
