# Everyday Mindfulness

## When to Use
- When someone wants to integrate mindfulness into daily activities without adding formal practice time.
- When the concept of informal practice needs to be explained.
- When the bridge between meditation on the cushion and awareness in daily life is the question.

## Core Concept
Formal practice — sitting meditation, body scan, mindful movement — develops the capacity for mindful attention. Informal practice — bringing that attention to the activities of daily life — is where the capacity is applied. Kabat-Zinn considers both essential. Formal practice without informal practice is like training at the gym but never using your fitness in daily life. Informal practice without formal practice is like trying to run a marathon without training — the intention is good but the capacity hasn't been developed.

Everyday mindfulness means bringing full, non-judgmental attention to ordinary activities: eating, walking, showering, driving, listening, washing dishes. These activities are normally performed on autopilot — the body goes through the motions while the mind is elsewhere, planning, worrying, reviewing, daydreaming. Kabat-Zinn's invitation is to periodically bring the mind and the body back into the same place and time. When you eat, actually taste the food. When you walk, feel your feet on the ground. When you listen to someone, actually hear them — not the version of what they're saying that you're editing in your head.

The raisin exercise — a staple of the first MBSR session — illustrates this vividly. Participants spend several minutes eating a single raisin: looking at it as if they've never seen one, feeling its texture, smelling it, placing it in the mouth, noticing the flood of saliva, chewing slowly, tasting. Most people have eaten thousands of raisins but have never actually experienced one. The exercise is a compressed demonstration of how much of life is consumed on autopilot, and how much richness becomes available when attention is genuinely present.

The deeper point is that there is no such thing as an unimportant moment. Every moment is your life. If you are only present for the "important" moments — the meetings, the decisions, the milestones — and autopilot through the rest, you are absent for most of your existence. Everyday mindfulness is the practice of treating each moment as worthy of attention, not because it is extraordinary, but because it is here.

## How to Apply
1. **Choose one daily activity as a mindfulness anchor.** Eating breakfast, walking to work, washing dishes. Do this one thing with full attention.
2. **Use transitions as reminders.** Walking through a doorway, picking up the phone, starting the car — let transitions prompt a moment of present-moment awareness.
3. **Practise single-tasking.** Do one thing at a time, fully. Multitasking is the antithesis of mindfulness.
4. **Don't judge the quality of your attention.** Some moments you'll be present; most you won't. Noticing that you weren't present is itself a moment of presence.

## Examples
**Situation:** A busy consultant says "I don't have time to meditate — my schedule is completely packed."
**Application:** Kabat-Zinn would say: "You don't need to find extra time. You need to inhabit the time you already have. Right now, while we're talking, you're probably also thinking about the next meeting, checking something on your screen, or composing the email you need to send afterward. What if, for just this conversation, you were fully here? Not multi-processing, not planning, just listening and responding? That is mindfulness practice. It doesn't require a cushion or a timer. It requires the decision to be where you are, doing what you're doing, for the duration that you're doing it. Start small: one fully attended meal per day. One walk where you notice the sky and the air and your feet on the ground. One conversation where you actually listen. These are not additions to your schedule. They are changes in how you inhabit your schedule. And they may be more transformative than you expect, because they reveal how little of your life you're actually present for."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present informal practice as a shortcut that replaces formal practice.
**Why:** The capacity for sustained attention must be developed through formal training. Without it, the intention to "be present during the day" rarely survives first contact with a busy schedule.

**Don't:** Make mindfulness sound like yet another task.
**Why:** It is not another thing to do. It is a different way of doing the things you are already doing.
