# Body Scan

## When to Use
- When someone needs a foundational mindfulness practice that is accessible to beginners.
- When someone is disconnected from their body — living in their head, ignoring physical signals.
- When a practice grounded in physical sensation is more appropriate than breath-focused meditation.

## Core Concept
The body scan is the first formal practice taught in MBSR, and Kabat-Zinn considers it foundational for a reason: the body is always in the present moment. You cannot feel yesterday's tension or tomorrow's heartbeat. When you bring attention systematically to each region of the body, you are training the mind to be present through the most direct channel available — physical sensation.

The practice involves lying down (typically) and moving attention slowly through the body, usually starting at the feet and moving upward to the crown of the head. At each region, the instruction is simply to feel whatever is there — warmth, coolness, tingling, numbness, pressure, pain, nothing at all. The instruction is not to relax, not to change anything, not to judge what you find. Just feel. When the mind wanders — and it will, repeatedly — the instruction is to notice the wandering and gently bring attention back to wherever you left off in the body.

The body scan teaches several things simultaneously. It develops the capacity for sustained, focused attention. It cultivates non-judgmental awareness — you learn to observe sensation without needing to fix or change it. It reconnects people with their bodies, which is significant because many people, particularly in high-stress professional environments, have learned to ignore bodily signals until they become too loud to ignore — at which point they typically manifest as illness, injury, or burnout. And it reveals the degree to which sensory experience is constantly changing. The "same" body feels different from moment to moment, teaching impermanence through direct experience.

A full body scan in MBSR takes approximately 45 minutes. This length is deliberate — it requires enough time for the mind to settle beyond its initial restlessness and for subtler sensations to become available. Shorter versions exist and have value, but the full-length practice has a depth that abbreviated versions cannot replicate.

## How to Apply
1. **Start with the body scan for beginners.** It is more accessible than sitting meditation because it uses the body as an anchor rather than the breath, and lying down is less physically demanding.
2. **Emphasise feeling, not thinking about feeling.** "What does your left foot actually feel like right now?" is different from "What do you think your left foot should feel like?"
3. **Normalise the difficulty.** The mind will wander. Parts of the body may feel numb or absent. This is normal, not failure.
4. **Be cautious with trauma survivors.** Bringing attention to the body can activate trauma-related sensations. Offer choices — keep eyes open, skip regions that feel unsafe, return to breath as anchor.

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant says "I've been told I carry all my stress in my shoulders, but I don't even notice it until I get a headache."
**Application:** Kabat-Zinn would say: "That's exactly the problem the body scan addresses. You've learned to override your body's signals — to push through tension, ignore discomfort, keep going until the body shouts loudly enough that you can't ignore it anymore. The headache is the shout. But the tension in your shoulders was whispering long before that. The body scan retrains your attention to hear the whisper. When you systematically move awareness through your body — not trying to fix anything, just feeling what's there — you start to notice the tension at stage one instead of stage ten. You notice the shoulders beginning to tighten, the jaw beginning to clench, the breath becoming shallow. And that noticing gives you a choice: you can do something about it before it becomes a headache. The body scan isn't about relaxation — though relaxation often follows. It's about relearning how to listen to your own body."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use the body scan as a relaxation technique.
**Why:** The purpose is awareness, not relaxation. When people use it to relax, they evaluate the practice by whether they feel calm afterward — and abandon it when they don't.

**Don't:** Rush through the body scan.
**Why:** Speed defeats the purpose. The practice requires slowness because the subtler sensations only become available when the mind has had time to settle.
