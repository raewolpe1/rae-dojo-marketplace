# Mindful Movement

## When to Use
- When someone finds sitting meditation too challenging or physically uncomfortable.
- When the role of yoga and walking meditation in MBSR needs to be explained.
- When movement-based mindfulness practice would be more appropriate than stillness-based practice.

## Core Concept
MBSR includes two forms of mindful movement: gentle hatha yoga and walking meditation. Both serve the same purpose as sitting meditation and body scan — cultivating present-moment, non-judgmental awareness — but they use movement rather than stillness as the vehicle. For many participants, especially those with chronic pain or restlessness, movement practices are more accessible than sitting still.

Kabat-Zinn's approach to yoga in MBSR is radically different from the yoga found in most studios. There is no emphasis on flexibility, strength, or achieving particular postures. The instruction is to bring the same quality of attention to movement that you bring to the breath in sitting meditation: feel what is actually happening in the body as it moves, notice where there is ease and where there is limitation, and meet whatever you find without trying to change it. The poses are simple and the pace is slow because the purpose is not exercise but awareness. When you reach the edge of your range in a stretch, the instruction is to stay there — to explore the boundary between comfort and discomfort with curiosity rather than pushing through it or retreating from it.

Walking meditation applies the same principle to the most ordinary of human activities. Walking slowly and deliberately, feeling each component of each step — the lifting, the moving, the placing, the shifting of weight — reveals an astonishing complexity in an activity we normally perform on complete autopilot. Walking meditation makes visible how much of life is lived without awareness, and it offers a bridge between formal practice and everyday life.

## How to Apply
1. **Offer movement practices to people who find stillness difficult.** Not everyone can sit for extended periods. Movement-based mindfulness is equally valid.
2. **Emphasise awareness, not performance.** "How does this movement feel?" not "How far can you stretch?"
3. **Use walking meditation as a bridge to everyday mindfulness.** If you can be mindful while walking, you can be mindful while doing anything.
4. **Respect physical limitations.** MBSR yoga is designed to be adapted to any body. The practice is in the attention, not the posture.

## Examples
**Situation:** A development professional says "I've tried meditation but I just can't sit still — my body gets restless and my mind goes into overdrive."
**Application:** Kabat-Zinn would say: "Then don't sit still. Stillness is one vehicle for mindfulness, but it's not the only one. In MBSR we teach mindful yoga and walking meditation precisely for people like you. The practice is not about being still — it's about being aware. If your body wants to move, let it move — but move with attention. Feel each step as you walk. Feel each stretch as you move. Notice the breath flowing through movement. The restlessness you're describing is not a problem — it's information. Your body is telling you something. Mindful movement lets you listen to that message rather than overriding it by forcing yourself to sit still. Some of the deepest practitioners I've known do most of their practice in movement. The question is not whether you're still — it's whether you're present."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat MBSR yoga as a fitness programme.
**Why:** The moment you focus on flexibility or achievement, you've left mindfulness and entered performance. The practice is attention, not accomplishment.

**Don't:** Present walking meditation as less serious than sitting meditation.
**Why:** Both develop the same capacity. Walking meditation has the added advantage of being directly transferable to daily life.
