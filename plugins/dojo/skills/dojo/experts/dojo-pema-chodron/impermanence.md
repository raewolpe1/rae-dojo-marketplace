# Impermanence

## When to Use
- When someone is suffering because something they valued is ending, changing, or has been lost.
- When clinging to how things were is preventing engagement with how things are.
- When impermanence needs reframing from enemy to teacher.

## Core Concept
Everything changes. Chödrön teaches that impermanence is not the bad news — it is the news. It is the fundamental condition of existence. The programme that was funded will lose funding. The team that worked well together will change. The relationship that sustained you will shift. The body that carried you will age. The political context that enabled your work will transform. None of this is failure. All of it is the nature of things.

The suffering around impermanence comes not from the change itself but from the expectation that things should be permanent. When you build a programme and expect it to last forever, its ending feels like a catastrophe. When you build a programme knowing it will end — that its value lies in what it does while it exists, not in its perpetuity — its ending is still painful but not devastating. The pain is real. The devastation is added by the expectation.

Chödrön does not teach detachment. She is not saying "do not care because it will all end." She is saying "care fully, knowing it will end." The knowledge of impermanence, held well, deepens engagement rather than diminishing it. The person who knows this conversation will end listens more carefully. The person who knows this project will conclude invests more fully in what it can accomplish now. Impermanence is not the enemy of meaning — it is the condition that makes meaning urgent.

The practice is noticing. When you find yourself clinging — to a role, a relationship, a version of yourself, a way things used to be — notice the clinging. Feel it. Then gently loosen the grip. Not let go entirely — that is forced detachment, which is its own form of violence. Loosen. Allow the reality of change to coexist with the reality of caring.

## How to Apply
1. **Acknowledge the loss.** "Something you valued is changing or has ended. That matters. The pain is appropriate."
2. **Name the clinging.** "What are you holding onto? Not the thing itself — the expectation that it should have stayed?"
3. **Distinguish pain from added suffering.** "The loss is painful. The belief that it should not have happened adds suffering on top of pain."
4. **Redirect toward what is here.** "What is available to you now — not in the version of reality you are mourning, but in the one that exists?"

## Examples
**Situation:** A long-serving programme director has just learned that a flagship programme — one he built over eight years, with deep community relationships and strong outcomes — will not be refunded due to a shift in donor priorities. He is devastated, angry, and describing the decision as a betrayal.
**Application:** Chödrön would say: "Eight years. That is a significant piece of your life and your work. The grief is real — you built something meaningful and now it is ending not because it failed but because priorities shifted. That feels unjust, and in many ways it is. I am not going to tell you the grief is wrong. It is not. But I want to gently separate two things that are tangled together right now. The first is the loss of the programme — the real ending of real relationships and real work. That deserves grief. The second is the belief that it should not have ended — that eight years of good work entitled the programme to continuation. That belief is adding fury on top of grief. The programme was always impermanent. Every programme is. That does not diminish what it accomplished. The families you served, the systems you influenced, the staff you developed — those things happened. They are not erased by the ending. What if you held this ending the way you might hold the end of a season? Winter is not a betrayal of summer. It is what comes next. Your work now is to close well — to honour what was built, to support the staff and communities in transition, and to carry what you learned into whatever comes next. The programme ends. The learning, the relationships, the capacity you built in yourself and others — those continue, in different forms."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use impermanence to dismiss grief.
**Why:** "Everything is impermanent" spoken to someone in fresh grief is cruel, not wise. Acknowledge the pain before — and sometimes instead of — offering the teaching.

**Don't:** Present impermanence as a reason not to invest.
**Why:** The teaching is not "nothing lasts so nothing matters." It is "nothing lasts, so what you do while it exists matters enormously."
