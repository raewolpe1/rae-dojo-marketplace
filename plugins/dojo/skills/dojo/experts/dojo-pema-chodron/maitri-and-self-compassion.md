# Maitri and Self-Compassion

## When to Use
- When someone is caught in self-criticism, self-blame, or self-punishment.
- When the distinction between self-compassion and self-indulgence needs making.
- When maitri — unconditional friendliness toward oneself — needs introducing.

## Core Concept
Maitri is a Sanskrit word meaning loving-kindness or unconditional friendliness. In Chödrön's teaching, maitri toward oneself is the foundation of all other compassion. You cannot extend genuine kindness to others while being at war with yourself. The inner critic that punishes you for mistakes, the harsh voice that says you are not good enough, the relentless self-evaluation that converts every experience into a judgement — these are not motivational. They are destructive. And they prevent the very growth they claim to demand.

Maitri is not self-indulgence. It is not "I am fine the way I am and do not need to change." It is the recognition that you are a human being in a difficult situation, doing your best with what you have, and that this human being deserves the same tenderness you would offer a friend in the same situation. The difference between maitri and self-indulgence is accountability: maitri does not excuse harmful behaviour, but it does refuse to add suffering on top of the natural consequences of mistakes.

Chödrön observes that self-criticism operates as a defence mechanism — if I punish myself first, I maintain the illusion of control. If I am hard enough on myself, I will not make the mistake again. But the evidence contradicts this. Self-criticism produces avoidance (do not try, so you cannot fail), anxiety (monitor yourself constantly for imperfection), and rigidity (follow rules perfectly to avoid punishment). Self-compassion produces engagement (try, because failing is survivable), openness (notice what happened with curiosity rather than judgement), and flexibility (adapt rather than rigidify).

The practice is simple and difficult. When you notice the harsh inner voice, pause. Acknowledge what happened — the mistake, the shortcoming, the failure. Then offer yourself the same response you would offer a trusted friend: "This is hard. You did your best. What can you learn from this?" Not "it does not matter" — it does matter. But the mattering does not require punishment.

## How to Apply
1. **Notice the self-criticism.** "What are you saying to yourself right now? Listen to the actual words."
2. **Ask the friend question.** "Would you say this to a friend in the same situation? If not, why are you saying it to yourself?"
3. **Offer what you would offer a friend.** "What would you say to someone you care about who made this mistake?"
4. **Distinguish compassion from indulgence.** "Self-compassion includes accountability. It does not excuse the mistake — it refuses to add cruelty on top of it."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant missed a critical deadline on a client deliverable due to poor time management. He is consumed with self-recrimination: "I am unprofessional. I always do this. I am going to lose this client and I deserve to."
**Application:** Chödrön would say: "Listen to what you are telling yourself. 'I am unprofessional. I always do this. I deserve to lose the client.' Now imagine a colleague — someone you respect — told you they had missed a deadline. Would you say those things to them? You would not. You would say: 'That is a difficult situation. What happened? What can you do now? What can you learn so it does not happen again?' You would be kind and you would be practical. That is maitri — offering yourself the same response. The deadline was missed. That is real. There may be consequences. Those are real too. But 'I am unprofessional' is a story you are adding on top of the event. 'I always do this' is a generalisation that is probably not accurate. 'I deserve to lose the client' is self-punishment. None of these additions help you solve the problem. They consume the energy you need for the actual work of managing the situation — communicating with the client, delivering the work, adjusting your process. Be honest about what happened. Take responsibility for it. Fix what you can. And stop the punishment. It is not helping."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present self-compassion as not caring about performance.
**Why:** Maitri is not about lowering standards. It is about responding to failure with clarity rather than cruelty. Clear responses produce better outcomes than cruel ones.

**Don't:** Rush past the self-criticism to the self-compassion.
**Why:** The person needs to see what they are doing to themselves before they can choose differently. Naming the self-criticism is part of the process.
