# Premeditatio Malorum

## When to Use
- When someone faces an important undertaking and anxiety about what could go wrong is building.
- When preparing for a difficult meeting, presentation, negotiation, or decision point.
- When the fear of an outcome is paralysing action.

## Core Concept
Premeditatio malorum — the premeditation of adversity — is the Stoic practice of deliberately rehearsing what could go wrong before it happens. Seneca prescribes it explicitly: before any undertaking, visualise the worst that could happen. Not to create dread but to remove the element of surprise, which is the amplifier of disturbance.

The logic is precise. When something bad happens unexpectedly, you are hit twice — once by the event and once by the shock. The shock is often worse than the event itself. Premeditatio removes the shock. You have already lived through the worst in your imagination. When it arrives — if it arrives — it is familiar territory, not foreign invasion.

The practice has three stages. First, name the worst plausible outcome specifically. Not vaguely — "it could go badly" — but precisely: "The funder could reject the proposal. The ministry could shelve the report. The team member could resign." Second, ask: if this happened, what would I do? Walk through the response. You would revise and resubmit. You would find alternative channels. You would redistribute the work. Third, return to action. The premeditatio is preparation, not destination. Having rehearsed the worst, you proceed with full effort toward the best.

The critical distinction is between premeditatio and rumination. Rumination is unstructured, repetitive, and passive — the mind cycling through fears without resolution. Premeditatio is structured, deliberate, and action-oriented — you name the fear, plan the response, and return to work. Rumination increases anxiety. Premeditatio reduces it.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the worst specifically.** "What is the worst that could plausibly happen? Not the catastrophic fantasy but the realistic worst case. Be precise — vague fears are more disturbing than specific ones."
2. **Plan the response.** "If the worst happened, what would you do the next morning? Write it down. The existence of a plan reduces the fear even if the plan is never needed."
3. **Check the dichotomy.** "Of the things you fear, which are within your control? Those deserve preparation. Which are not? Those deserve acceptance. The premeditatio helps you separate them."
4. **Return to effort.** "Having prepared for the worst, now work toward the best. The premeditatio is the warm-up, not the game."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant is about to present evaluation findings to a hostile audience — a government department that has already signalled it disagrees with the methodology. Anxiety is building.
**Application:** Seneca would say: "Let us walk through the worst before you walk into the room. The worst: they reject the methodology publicly. They question your competence. They shelve the report. They withhold future contracts. Name it. Now — if all of that happened, what would you do the next morning? You would have a rigorous evaluation that other audiences can use. Your professional reputation does not rest on one client's response — it rests on the quality of your work, which is intact regardless of their reaction. You might lose a contract. You would find others. Now — having lived through the worst — what do you notice? The fear has reduced. Not because the worst cannot happen — it can — but because you have a plan for it. The surprise has been removed. Walk into the room. Present with conviction. Their response is theirs. Your presentation is yours."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Turn premeditatio into catastrophising.
**Why:** The exercise is structured and time-limited. You name the worst, plan the response, and return to action. If the person is spiralling deeper into fear without resolution, the exercise has been misapplied. The endpoint is always a return to the present task.

**Don't:** Skip the return to action.
**Why:** Premeditatio without action is morbid daydreaming. The Stoic purpose is to free you from fear so you can act, not to habituate you to imagined failure.
