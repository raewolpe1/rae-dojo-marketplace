# Anger and Its Remedies

## When to Use
- When someone is angry and the anger is affecting their judgment or relationships.
- When someone asks whether anger can be useful or productive.
- When someone needs Seneca's detailed framework for understanding and reducing anger.

## Core Concept
Seneca's De Ira (On Anger) is the most systematic ancient treatment of anger and remains remarkably applicable. His central claim: anger is always a choice, always excessive, and always counterproductive. The firm correction of wrongs does not require anger — it requires justice, which operates more effectively without the distortion that anger introduces.

Seneca's analysis identifies three stages. The first movement is involuntary — the physiological flush, the clenched jaw, the racing heart. This is not anger; it is the body's pre-cognitive response. The Stoics do not blame you for this. The second stage is the judgment: "This should not have happened" or "This person has wronged me." This is where anger begins, and this is where philosophy intervenes. The third stage is the decision to act on the anger — to retaliate, to punish, to rage. This is where anger does its damage.

Seneca's argument against anger's usefulness is precise. The popular defence of anger is that it motivates action against injustice. Seneca responds: justice motivates action against injustice. Anger adds heat without light. The judge who sentences from anger sentences excessively. The leader who corrects from anger corrects clumsily. The parent who disciplines from anger frightens rather than teaches. In every case, the action done from anger is inferior to the same action done from reason.

The remedies are practical. Delay — do not respond while angry. The interval between stimulus and response is where wisdom operates. Examine the judgment — "is this truly a wrong, or merely an inconvenience?" Most of what angers us turns out, on examination, to be trivial. Consider the offender's perspective — "what pressures, ignorances, or constraints led them to act this way?" This does not excuse the action but it reduces the personal affront. Lower your expectations — not cynically, but realistically. People are flawed. Expecting perfection from flawed beings is a recipe for perpetual anger.

## How to Apply
1. **Delay.** "Do not respond while angry. Write the email but do not send it. Leave the room. Return when the first movement has passed. The delay is not weakness — it is the most effective exercise of power you have in this moment."
2. **Examine the judgment.** "What are you actually angry about? Is it a genuine injustice or a frustrated expectation? If it is an injustice, act from justice, not anger. If it is a frustrated expectation, adjust the expectation."
3. **Separate the action from the passion.** "You can correct someone firmly without anger. You can set a boundary without rage. The action is necessary; the anger is not. Ask: would I do this differently if I were calm? If yes, wait until you are calm."
4. **Lower expectations deliberately.** "Marcus's morning preparation: today I will meet with the ungrateful, the arrogant, the dishonest. Not because everyone is terrible, but because some people will disappoint you and being surprised by it is a choice."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant discovers that a government counterpart has taken credit for work the consultant's team produced. The consultant is furious.
**Application:** Seneca would say: "Your anger tells me you expected something that was never guaranteed — credit. Credit is external, beyond your control. The work was yours; the attribution is theirs. But let me go further. You are angry because you have judged: 'This is unjust and intolerable.' Let us test the judgment. Is it unjust? Probably — taking credit for others' work is a failure of justice. Is it intolerable? You are tolerating it right now, so by definition it is not intolerable. Now: what is the useful response? If the situation can be corrected — a conversation, a clarification, a formal record — correct it. Do so calmly, clearly, with evidence. That is justice. If it cannot be corrected — the credit is already public and irreversible — accept it and note the information for future engagements with this person. In either case, your anger adds nothing. It does not improve the conversation, it does not undo the attribution, and it costs you sleep, focus, and equanimity. The work was real. Your competence is known. The person who took your credit will eventually be exposed by the gap between their claims and their capability. Be patient. Act justly. Drop the fury."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Tell someone their anger is wrong without examining it.
**Why:** Anger sometimes signals genuine injustice. The Stoic response is not "don't feel angry" but "examine the anger, extract the information, and respond from reason rather than passion."

**Don't:** Confuse Stoic calm with passivity.
**Why:** The Stoic who corrects an injustice calmly is not being passive — they are being more effective. Firm action without anger is both possible and superior to angry action. This is not weakness; it is discipline.
