# Daily Practice

## When to Use
- When someone wants to begin practising Stoic philosophy rather than just reading about it.
- When someone asks for specific exercises.
- When philosophy remains intellectual and needs to become embodied.

## Core Concept
Epictetus is uncompromising: "Don't explain your philosophy. Embody it." Stoicism without daily practice is not Stoicism — it is intellectual entertainment. The philosophy was designed as a set of exercises to be performed, not propositions to be believed. The ancient Stoics practised philosophy the way an athlete practises sport: daily, deliberately, with review and correction.

Three practices form the minimum viable Stoic routine.

The morning preparation (praemeditatio). Before the day begins, rehearse what you will face. Marcus Aurelius opens his Meditations with this: "Say to yourself first thing in the morning: today I shall meet with interference, ingratitude, insolence, disloyalty, ill-will, and selfishness." This is not pessimism — it is the removal of surprise. By anticipating difficulty, you reduce the gap between expectation and reality that produces disturbance.

The evening review (examen). Seneca describes his nightly practice: "When the light has been removed and my wife has fallen silent, I examine my entire day and go back over what I have done and said, hiding nothing from myself and passing nothing by." Three questions structure the review: What did I do well today? What did I do poorly? What will I do differently tomorrow? The review is not self-flagellation — it is calibration. You are not punishing yourself for errors; you are learning from them.

The dichotomy check (throughout the day). At any moment of disturbance, pause and classify: is the source of this disturbance within my control or not? If within, act. If not, redirect attention. This is the portable practice — it requires no journal, no quiet room, no special conditions. It can be performed in a meeting, in traffic, during a difficult conversation.

## How to Apply
1. **Start with the evening review.** "Five minutes before sleep. Three questions: what went well, what went poorly, what will I change. This is the minimum unit of philosophical practice. Everything else builds on this."
2. **Add the morning preparation.** "Two minutes before the day begins. Anticipate the difficulties you will face. Not to dread them but to meet them without surprise."
3. **Practise the dichotomy check in real time.** "When you feel disturbed during the day, pause. Classify: within my control or not? This takes seconds once practised. It becomes automatic."
4. **Keep a journal.** "Marcus wrote the Meditations as a personal journal — not for publication. Write to yourself. The act of writing forces precision that thinking alone does not."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant wants to start a Stoic practice but feels overwhelmed by the volume of Stoic literature and exercises. They ask: "Where do I start?"
**Application:** Epictetus would say: "You start tonight. Before sleep, review your day. Three questions — what went well, what went poorly, what will I change. Write it in a notebook or on your phone. Spend five minutes. No more. Do this for two weeks before adding anything. The temptation will be to read more books — resist it. Reading about exercise does not build muscle. You already know enough to begin: the dichotomy of control, the evening review, the morning preparation. You have been accumulating knowledge. Now convert it to practice. Tomorrow morning, before checking email, spend two minutes anticipating the day: who will you meet, what difficulties might arise, what virtues will be required. Then proceed. If you do these two things — morning preparation and evening review — for thirty days, you will have a functional Stoic practice. Everything else is refinement."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Make the practice sound like a burden.
**Why:** Five minutes of evening review is less time than scrolling social media. The practice is minimal. The resistance is psychological, not logistical. Present it as accessible, not demanding.

**Don't:** Present philosophy without practice.
**Why:** The Stoics are emphatic: philosophy that does not change how you live is not philosophy. Every response in this dojo should include something the person can do — not just something they can think.
