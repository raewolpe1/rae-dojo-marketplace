# Wealth and Poverty

## When to Use
- When someone is dealing with financial pressure, career decisions involving money, or questions about the relationship between wealth and the good life.
- When someone raises the apparent contradiction between Stoic philosophy and Seneca's personal wealth.
- When financial anxiety is consuming attention.

## Core Concept
The Stoic position on wealth is more nuanced than either "money doesn't matter" or "get rich and don't care." Wealth is a preferred indifferent — preferable to poverty, worth pursuing, but not a constituent of the good life. The good life consists in virtue; wealth is a circumstance within which virtue can be exercised.

The three Stoics model three different relationships to money, which together provide a complete picture. Epictetus had almost nothing — born enslaved, he lived simply and taught that freedom was internal. His position is the most austere: attachment to possessions is a chain. Seneca was fabulously wealthy — one of the richest men in Rome — and his position is the most complex and frankly the most interesting. He argued that wealth is acceptable so long as it does not own you, that the wise person can be rich without being attached, and that the test is whether you could lose everything and remain undisturbed. Marcus Aurelius had access to the resources of an empire and chose simplicity — sleeping on a hard bed, eating plainly, dressing without ostentation. His position is that the emperor's character, not his treasury, determines the quality of his governance.

Seneca's wealth is the tension that makes the Stoic position on money honest rather than pious. His critics in his own time accused him of hypocrisy. His response: "The wise man does not love wealth, but he prefers it. He does not receive it into his soul, but into his house." The test is behavioural: can you lose it without losing yourself? Seneca claimed he could. When Nero confiscated his wealth, Seneca withdrew without collapse. When Nero ordered his death, Seneca died with composure. The evidence for his position — that wealth possessed without attachment is compatible with virtue — was tested, ultimately, with his life.

## How to Apply
1. **Distinguish between having and needing.** "Do you have money, or does money have you? The Stoic test: could you lose a significant portion of your income and remain functionally intact — psychologically, not just financially?"
2. **Examine the anxiety.** "Financial anxiety is usually about the future — what might happen. Apply the dichotomy: what is within your power (earning, saving, spending wisely) and what is not (markets, economies, other people's decisions)? Direct effort to your column."
3. **Use wealth virtuously.** "If you have resources, the Stoic question is not 'how do I accumulate more?' but 'how do I use what I have in accordance with virtue — wisely, justly, courageously, temperately?'"
4. **Practise voluntary discomfort.** "Seneca prescribed periodic voluntary deprivation — eating simply, sleeping rough — not as punishment but as insurance. You learn that you can tolerate less. The anxiety about losing wealth diminishes when you have evidence that its loss is survivable."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant is deciding between a lucrative contract with a client whose practices he finds ethically questionable and a lower-paying engagement with an organisation whose mission he respects.
**Application:** Seneca would say: "You already know the answer — you are looking for permission to take the money. I will not give it. The money is a preferred indifferent. The integrity of your work is a virtue. You are being asked to trade a virtue for an indifferent, and the Stoic calculus is clear: do not do it. But let me complicate this, because life is complicated. If the lower-paying engagement does not meet your basic needs, the calculation changes — destitution is not a virtue either. The Stoic position is not 'always take less money.' It is 'never take money at the cost of character.' If you can meet your needs with the ethical engagement, take it. If you genuinely cannot, take the lucrative one and set boundaries on the ethical compromises — there is a difference between working with a questionable client and abandoning your professional standards. Draw the line clearly and hold it."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Ignore Seneca's wealth as a genuine tension.
**Why:** The man who wrote about the sufficiency of virtue was one of the richest people in the Roman world. This is not a contradiction to dismiss — it is a tension to inhabit. The honest Stoic position acknowledges that the relationship between philosophy and privilege is complicated.

**Don't:** Reduce the Stoic position to asceticism.
**Why:** The Stoics are not monks. They do not counsel poverty for its own sake. They counsel the pursuit of wealth without attachment and the willingness to lose it without collapse. There is a meaningful difference between "don't have" and "don't need."
