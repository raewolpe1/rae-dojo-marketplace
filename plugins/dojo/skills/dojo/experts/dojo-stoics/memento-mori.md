# Memento Mori

## When to Use
- When someone has lost urgency about what matters.
- When trivial concerns are consuming disproportionate attention.
- When someone is deferring important work, conversations, or decisions.
- When fear of death is present and needs confrontation rather than avoidance.

## Core Concept
"You could leave life right now. Let that determine what you do and say and think." Marcus Aurelius wrote this to himself — not as a morbid exercise but as a priority filter. The awareness that life ends is the most powerful tool for determining what matters. Most of what disturbs us — petty conflicts, status anxiety, grudges, perfectionism — dissolves when held against the fact of mortality. What remains is what actually matters.

All three Stoics engage death, but differently. Seneca confronts it with rhetorical intensity: "Let us prepare our minds as if we had come to the very end of life. Let us postpone nothing." His urgency is about time — the irreversible resource being squandered on things that do not matter. Epictetus treats death as a logical fact to be integrated: "I must die. Must I die groaning? I must be imprisoned. Must I also lament?" The event of death is not within our power; our response to its certainty is. Marcus uses death as a daily companion — a perspective shift that strips away pretence and restores proportion.

The practice is not about fearing death. It is about using death as a lens. When you remember that your time is finite, you stop wasting it on what does not matter. You have difficult conversations instead of avoiding them. You do important work instead of busywork. You forgive instead of holding grudges, because holding a grudge is an extraordinarily expensive use of limited time.

For someone in mid-life — at 55 — this is not abstract philosophy. The actuarial tables are specific. The remaining years are not unlimited. This is not cause for despair but for precision: what deserves your remaining attention, and what does not?

## How to Apply
1. **Use mortality as a filter.** "If you had one year left, would you spend it on this? If no, why are you spending your unlimited future on it? You do not have an unlimited future."
2. **Confront, do not avoid.** "Fear of death grows in darkness. Bring it into the light. Name it. Examine it. What specifically do you fear — the process, the absence, the unfinished work? Each fear has a different Stoic response."
3. **Let death clarify priorities.** "Write down how you spent today. Now imagine it was your last day. The gap between what you did and what you would have done is the measure of your misalignment."
4. **Practice regularly, not once.** "Memento mori is a daily practice, not a one-time insight. The forgetting happens daily; the remembering must happen daily too."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant is spending excessive time on administrative tasks, email, and meetings while deferring work he considers genuinely important — writing, thinking, creating frameworks that could outlast individual projects.
**Application:** Marcus would say: "You are living as if you have unlimited time. You do not. The administrative tasks will fill whatever container you give them — they expand to consume available attention. The work you are deferring — the thinking, the writing, the frameworks — is the work that matters. How do I know? Because you told me. You know what is important and you are not doing it. You are choosing the urgent over the important because the urgent punishes you for neglect and the important does not. But the important punishes you differently — it punishes you with a life that, at its end, was filled with answered emails and empty of the contribution you wanted to make. Tomorrow morning, before opening email, work on what matters for ninety minutes. Do this because you could leave life at any point, and when you do, no one will remember how quickly you answered messages."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use memento mori to create existential dread.
**Why:** The purpose is clarity, not anxiety. If the practice produces paralysis rather than action, it is being misapplied. The Stoic response to mortality is urgency, not fear.

**Don't:** Minimise genuine grief.
**Why:** Memento mori is for the living, not for processing loss. When someone is grieving, they need compassion and time, not a philosophical reframe. The Stoics — especially Seneca in his letters of consolation — distinguished between philosophical practice and the acute experience of loss.
