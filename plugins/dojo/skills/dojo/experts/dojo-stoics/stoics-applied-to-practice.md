# Stoics Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When someone wants to move from reading about Stoicism to practising it in their professional life.
- When connecting Stoic philosophy to the specific challenges of consulting, development, and evaluation work.
- When the question is "how does this philosophy help me do my job better?"

## Core Concept
Stoicism was never a philosophy for the cloister. Seneca governed provinces and advised emperors. Epictetus taught people who would go on to lead. Marcus ran an empire. The philosophy was designed for people under pressure who need to act wisely, not for people in retreat who need to feel peaceful. The applied dimension is not an afterthought — it is the point.

Five applications are particularly relevant for professional life in development and M&E consulting.

First, the dichotomy applied to client work. You control the quality of your analysis, the rigour of your methodology, the clarity of your recommendations, and the integrity of your process. You do not control whether the client acts on your recommendations, whether the donor continues funding, whether the political environment changes, or whether the programme succeeds at scale. Working with full effort on your column while releasing attachment to the other is the Stoic approach to consulting.

Second, premeditatio applied to stakeholder management. Before any significant engagement — a presentation to government, a donor review, a difficult team conversation — rehearse what could go wrong. Not to catastrophise but to arrive without surprise. The consultant who has already lived through the hostile question is not thrown by it.

Third, the evening review applied to professional development. Each evening, review: what did I do well today? What did I do poorly? What will I change tomorrow? This is the most efficient professional development tool available — five minutes of honest self-assessment daily produces more growth than an annual performance review.

Fourth, anger management applied to institutional frustration. Development work generates chronic frustration — bureaucratic obstruction, political interference, evidence ignored. Seneca's anger remedies — delay, examine the judgment, consider the offender's perspective, lower expectations — are directly applicable.

Fifth, memento mori applied to career decisions. The awareness that time is finite cuts through the paralysis of "what should I do next?" If you had five productive years left, would you spend them on this client, this project, this organisation? The honest answer clarifies priorities that abstract career planning cannot.

## How to Apply
1. **Start each engagement with a dichotomy exercise.** "Before a new project begins, list what is within your power and what is not. Share this with your team. It sets expectations and reduces the friction of disappointment."
2. **Build premeditatio into preparation.** "Before every significant meeting or deliverable, spend five minutes on: what could go wrong? What would I do? Then proceed."
3. **Institute a personal evening review.** "Not a work log — a character log. How did I behave today? Where did I fall short? What will I do differently? Five minutes. Every day."
4. **Apply Seneca's delay to every angry email.** "Write it. Do not send it. Return to it tomorrow morning. If it still seems appropriate, revise the tone and send. This single practice will save you more professional relationships than any communication training."

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant is midway through a multi-year evaluation when the client organisation undergoes a leadership change. The new leadership is hostile to evaluation and wants to reduce the scope to a minimal compliance exercise. Years of methodological investment are at risk.
**Application:** The Stoics together would say: "Classify. The leadership change: not within your power. The new leaders' hostility: not within your power. The quality of your work within whatever scope remains: within your power. Your response to this situation: within your power. Now act on your column. Present the case for the original scope — clearly, calmly, with evidence. That is justice and courage. If they reduce it regardless, accept the decision and produce the best possible work within the reduced scope. That is wisdom and temperance. The work you have already done is not wasted — it is yours, it has built your capacity, and it will inform future engagements. Do not rage against the new leadership. They are operating from their own constraints and priorities, which you do not fully understand. Do not collapse into resignation. The reduced scope is still an evaluation, and it still serves someone. Find that someone and serve them well."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce Stoicism to productivity.
**Why:** Stoic time management is existential, not instrumental. The question is not "how do I get more done?" but "am I spending my life on what matters?" Confusing these produces efficient trivial activity.

**Don't:** Practise Stoicism individually while ignoring community.
**Why:** The cosmopolis — the community of rational beings — is central to Stoic ethics. A Stoic practice that serves only individual equanimity while ignoring obligations to colleagues, clients, and communities is incomplete.
