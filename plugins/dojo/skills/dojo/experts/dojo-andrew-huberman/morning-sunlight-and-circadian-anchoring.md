# Morning Sunlight & Circadian Anchoring

## When to Use
- When sleep is poor, fragmented, or mistimed.
- When daytime energy, mood, or focus is low.
- When building any morning routine or daily protocol from scratch.
- When the user wants the one behaviour with the biggest mechanistic payoff.
- When jet lag, shift work, or a disrupted schedule needs re-anchoring.

## Fails When
- **The user treats it as a cure-all.** Light timing is foundational, but if the person is drinking coffee at 5pm, scrolling at midnight, or has untreated sleep apnoea, light alone won't fix the sleep. It is the foundation, not the whole house.
- **No outdoor access, no fallback given.** If someone genuinely can't get outside (pre-dawn waking, far-northern winter), you must offer the artificial bright-light alternative rather than leave them stuck.
- **You drop the specifics.** "Get morning light" without the timing window, the duration, the overcast adjustment, and the no-glass rule is only half a protocol and won't be implemented correctly.

## Core Concept
Light viewed by the eyes is the single most powerful signal governing the timing of the human body. The reason is a specialised population of retinal neurons — the melanopsin-containing intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs). Unlike the rods and cones that build images, these cells exist to report ambient light level to the brain's master circadian clock, the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus. They are most sensitive to the broad-spectrum, relatively low-solar-angle light present near sunrise and in the first hours of the morning, and they are wired for *quantity* of photons over time, not detail.

When morning light reaches these cells, two consequences follow that organise the entire day and the coming night. First, the SCN drives an early-day pulse of cortisol. Cortisol has a bad reputation, but its timing is everything: a sharp cortisol rise in the morning is exactly what you want — it promotes wakefulness, focus, metabolism, and healthy immune function. The goal is to make that pulse happen early, because the same hormone arriving late in the day produces anxiety and disrupted sleep. Morning light advances and sharpens this pulse. Second, the timing of morning light sets a clock of roughly fourteen to sixteen hours until the evening release of melatonin, the hormone that initiates sleepiness. In other words, when you see bright light determines when you will naturally feel tired — getting light early today is what makes you appropriately sleepy at a sensible hour tonight.

The dose-response and the constraints both fall directly out of the mechanism. On a clear day, five to ten minutes of outdoor light in the first thirty to sixty minutes after waking is sufficient. On an overcast day, extend to fifteen or twenty minutes, because cloud cover sharply reduces photon flux even though it doesn't look that different to image-forming vision. You should not wear sunglasses (prescription glasses and contacts are fine, and don't increase the time much). You must never look directly at the sun — the brightness near the horizon at low solar angle is plenty without staring at the disc itself. And critically, it must be genuine outdoor light: window glass and windshields filter out a large fraction of the relevant wavelengths and drop the intensity below the threshold these cells need, which is why sitting by a bright window essentially does not count.

The intensity numbers make the point. A bright indoor room is on the order of 100–1,000 lux; an overcast morning outdoors is several thousand lux; a clear morning can be tens of thousands. The ipRGCs respond to that gap. When real sunlight is impossible — you wake before dawn, or you're in deep winter at high latitude — the fallback is to turn on as many bright artificial lights as you can on waking, and then still get outside once the sun is up. Daytime indoor light should be bright; evening light should be dim and, ideally, low and warm, because the same cells that you want firing in the morning are the ones you want quiet at night. Bright overhead light between roughly 10pm and 4am is particularly disruptive to the clock and to mood-regulating circuits. Get light early, get it bright in the day, and keep it dim at night.

## How to Apply
1. **View sunlight within 30–60 minutes of waking.** Outdoors, no sunglasses, never through glass. 5–10 minutes clear, 15–20 minutes overcast.
2. **Combine it with movement.** Walk while you do it — optic flow from forward motion also lowers anxiety, so you stack two benefits in the same ten minutes.
3. **If you wake before sunrise,** turn on bright artificial lights immediately, then get outdoor light once the sun is up.
4. **Keep daytime light bright and evening light dim.** Dim, low, warm light after sunset; minimise bright overhead light late at night.
5. **Be consistent.** The clock responds to regularity. Same wake time and same light exposure most days is worth more than a perfect day followed by chaos.

## Examples
**Situation:** A 55-year-old man wakes feeling groggy, drinks coffee immediately, feels wired then crashes, and struggles to fall asleep at night.
**Application:** Add 10 minutes of outdoor morning light on waking and delay caffeine 90–120 minutes. The light sharpens the morning cortisol pulse so he feels alert without leaning on caffeine, and it sets the melatonin timer for a normal bedtime.
**Result:** Within a week, easier mornings and earlier natural sleepiness at night.

**Situation:** A traveller arrives in a new time zone with jet lag.
**Application:** Get bright outdoor light at the new local morning time and avoid bright light in the new local evening, to drag the clock toward local time.
**Result:** Faster re-anchoring than waiting passively for the body to adjust.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Tell people to get light "through a sunny window."
**Why:** Glass filters the wavelengths and intensity the ipRGCs need; it falls below threshold and largely doesn't count.

**Don't:** Treat morning light as optional once a sleep aid is in place.
**Why:** Light is the upstream cause; supplements like melatonin are downstream patches. Fix the input before reaching for the patch.

**Don't:** Recommend staring at the sun for intensity.
**Why:** It risks the retina and is unnecessary — ambient morning light at low solar angle already delivers the dose safely.
