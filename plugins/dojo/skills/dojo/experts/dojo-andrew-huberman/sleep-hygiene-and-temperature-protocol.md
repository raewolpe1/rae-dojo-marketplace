# Sleep Hygiene & Temperature Protocol

## When to Use
- When the user reports poor, short, or fragmented sleep.
- When someone struggles to fall asleep or wakes at 3am.
- When designing or repairing a sleep routine.
- When sleep is the upstream lever for energy, focus, mood, or hormones.
- When the user wants the concrete toolkit, not generic "sleep hygiene."

## Fails When
- **There are signs of a disorder.** Loud snoring with gasping, true chronic insomnia, or excessive daytime sleepiness despite adequate time in bed warrant clinical evaluation, not just protocol tweaks.
- **Light and caffeine are ignored.** Sleep is set by daytime inputs; an evening routine alone won't fix a 5pm-coffee, no-morning-light pattern.
- **The temperature mechanism is dropped.** "Keep your room cool" without explaining the required core-temperature drop loses the why and the specific levers (warm shower, socks, cool room).

## Core Concept
Sleep is the single most important pillar of mental and physical health — it consolidates learning, clears metabolic waste from the brain, regulates hormones and appetite, supports immune function, and sets the ceiling on the next day's focus, mood, and resilience. And crucially, sleep is *engineered, not willed*. You do not fall asleep better by trying harder; you fall asleep better by arranging the inputs the body uses to initiate and maintain sleep. The two master inputs are light and temperature, with timing, caffeine, alcohol, and a set of behavioural tools layered on top.

Temperature is the mechanism most people underuse. To fall asleep and stay asleep, your core body temperature must drop by roughly one to three degrees Fahrenheit. To wake up, it rises again. This single fact drives much of the protocol. A cool sleeping environment helps the body shed heat — many people sleep best in a room around 18°C / 65°F or a little cooler. Counter-intuitively, a warm bath or shower before bed *helps* sleep, because the warming brings blood to the surface of the skin and, once you get out, you offload heat and your core temperature drops faster than it otherwise would — the warming triggers the cooling. Warm hands and feet (socks, or sticking feet out to dilate the vessels) help dump core heat through the extremities. The practical reading: warm the surface to cool the core, and keep the room cool.

Light is the other master input, and it bookends the day. Morning sunlight (covered in its own protocol) sets the ~16-hour timer to melatonin; equally important is keeping evening light dim, low, and warm. Bright overhead light, and screen light, in the hours before bed suppress melatonin and delay the clock through the same melanopsin cells you want active in the morning. So: dim the lights after sunset, use low and warm light sources, reduce screens or dim them, and avoid bright overhead light especially in the 10pm–4am window, which is particularly disruptive.

Consistency matters as much as any single input. Going to bed and — especially — waking at roughly the same time every day, including weekends, stabilises the clock and improves both sleep onset and quality. An hour of regularity is worth more than chasing a perfect night. Caffeine and alcohol are the two biggest chemical disruptors: caffeine has a long enough half-life that an afternoon dose fragments deep sleep even when you fall asleep fine (cut it off roughly 8–10 hours before bed), and alcohol, although sedating at first, fragments sleep architecture and suppresses REM, so it degrades sleep quality even when it speeds onset.

Then there is the behavioural toolkit for the actual night. If you can't fall asleep or you wake and can't get back, NSDR or yoga nidra is a powerful tool — you may not sleep, but the deep rest restores you and often eases you back to sleep without the anxiety spiral of clock-watching. Slow breathing with long exhales lowers arousal. Avoid lying awake frustrated for long stretches; getting up briefly into dim light and doing something calm can reset the attempt. Don't rely on melatonin supplements as a default — the common doses are far above physiological levels, it's a hormone, and it helps onset but not maintenance; fixing light, temperature, and timing is the better route. Magnesium (in forms like glycinate or threonate), theanine, and apigenin are among the supplements some people use for sleep, but they sit downstream of the behavioural foundation. For an older adult, whose sleep naturally becomes lighter and more fragmented with age, the temperature, light, consistency, and caffeine-timing levers become more important, not less — and protecting sleep is one of the highest-value things a person past fifty can do for cognition, mood, metabolism, and hormonal health.

## How to Apply
1. **Cool the room** (~18°C / 65°F) and use a **warm shower/bath before bed** to trigger the core-temperature drop; warm the extremities (socks) to dump heat.
2. **Dim, warm, low evening light;** minimise screens and bright overhead light, especially 10pm–4am.
3. **Get morning sunlight** to set the melatonin timer (the upstream half of the protocol).
4. **Keep wake time consistent,** even on weekends.
5. **Cut caffeine ~8–10 hours before bed** and treat alcohol as a sleep-quality disruptor.
6. **Use NSDR / long-exhale breathing** to fall asleep or return to sleep; don't clock-watch.
7. **Don't default to melatonin;** if using sleep supplements (magnesium glycinate/threonate, theanine, apigenin), layer them on top of the behavioural foundation, ideally after discussing with a doctor.

## Examples
**Situation:** A 55-year-old falls asleep fine but wakes at 3am and can't get back.
**Application:** Cool the room, warm shower before bed, no caffeine after early afternoon, and an NSDR session when he wakes rather than clock-watching; reinforce morning light.
**Result:** Easier return to sleep and, over weeks, fewer awakenings as the clock and inputs stabilise.

**Situation:** Someone relies on melatonin nightly and still sleeps poorly.
**Application:** Phase out melatonin, fix light and temperature and caffeine timing, add consistency.
**Result:** Better-quality, better-maintained sleep from addressing the actual inputs.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Tell people to just "try to relax and sleep."
**Why:** Sleep is engineered through inputs (temperature, light, timing, chemistry), not willed; effort to sleep often backfires.

**Don't:** Recommend melatonin as a first-line nightly sleep aid.
**Why:** Doses are typically supraphysiological, it's a hormone, and it aids onset but not maintenance — fix light, temperature, and timing first.
