## When to Use

Use when a team or partnership has reached an impasse, when two people or groups with different perspectives need to collaborate, or when "compromise" keeps producing mediocre results. Also use when someone wants to understand how genuine collaboration produces outcomes better than any individual could achieve alone.

## Fails When

Fails in low-trust relationships where people are protecting territory rather than genuinely opening to each other's perspective. Also fails when one party has already decided the outcome and is performing collaboration. Synergy requires genuine openness to being changed by the encounter.

## Core Concept

Habit 6 — Synergize — is the capstone of the Public Victory. It is the habit of creative cooperation, of combining perspectives, strengths, and energies to produce something better than any party could achieve independently.

**The Mathematics of Synergy:**
Covey's central claim is that 1 + 1 can equal 3, 10, or 100 — depending on the level of trust and openness in the relationship. Compromise produces 1 + 1 = 1.5 — both parties give something up. Synergy produces 1 + 1 = 3 or more — both parties walk away with something neither could have created alone.

This is not mysticism. It is what happens in nature constantly: soil plus rain plus seed plus sunlight plus air produce a tree that none of those elements alone could create. Synergy is the creative miracle that emerges when diverse elements combine in the right conditions.

**Valuing Differences:**
The key to synergy is learning to value differences rather than merely tolerate them. Most people tolerate differences — they put up with someone's contrasting perspective while waiting for a chance to assert their own view. Fewer people appreciate differences — they recognize that differing views have some merit. Synergists celebrate differences — they actively seek out viewpoints that contrast with their own, because they know that only through the encounter with genuine difference can something new be created.

Covey's insight: the person who sees things differently from you is not your adversary. They hold a piece of the puzzle you don't have. Your job is not to persuade them but to genuinely understand them — and then to allow the encounter between your two perspectives to produce something neither of you imagined at the outset.

**The Relationship Between Habits 4, 5, and 6:**
These three habits form an interdependent triad. Habit 4 (Win-Win) provides the mental framework: you approach the interaction committed to an outcome that works for both parties. Habit 5 (Seek First to Understand) provides the communication behavior: you invest in deeply understanding the other person before presenting your own view. Habit 6 (Synergize) is what becomes possible when you have done Habits 4 and 5 well: genuine creative cooperation that transcends the positions both parties brought to the table.

Without the foundation of Habits 4 and 5, what looks like "synergy" is actually negotiation at best or manipulation at worst.

**The Third Alternative:**
Synergy always involves finding what Covey later called "the third alternative" — not my way, not your way, but a third way that is better than either original position. This requires that both parties be genuinely open to having their minds changed. It requires intellectual humility, creativity, and the courage to say: "I don't know what the answer is yet, but I'm willing to find out together."

**Conditions for Synergy:**
Synergy requires:
- High trust between parties (built through Habits 1–5)
- Genuine openness to other perspectives
- Willingness to be influenced as well as influential
- Patience with the creative process
- Curiosity about difference rather than defensiveness about it

**The 1/10 Opportunity:**
Covey estimates that most people and organizations are operating at a small fraction of their synergistic potential. The habits of defensiveness, compromise, "my way or the highway," and adversarial negotiation leave enormous value on the table. Every relationship carries a 1/10 opportunity — the chance to achieve ten times more through genuine creative cooperation than through individual effort or lowest-common-denominator compromise.

## How to Apply

1. **Audit your current collaborations** — in which relationships are you settling for compromise when synergy is possible? What would need to change — in trust, in openness — to access a third alternative?

2. **Practice the synergy question** — in the next significant disagreement, after genuinely understanding the other person's position, ask: "Is there a solution that is better than what either of us has proposed?" Hold that question open genuinely.

3. **Celebrate, don't tolerate, differences** — when someone in a meeting has a contrasting view, practice asking them to elaborate rather than countering. Try to understand the piece of the puzzle they hold that you don't.

4. **Build the trust foundation** — synergy is downstream of trust. Identify the relationships where the Emotional Bank Account is in deficit. What deposits could you make this week?

5. **Protect the creative process** — synergy often requires a messy, uncertain period where the answer isn't yet clear. Practice tolerance for ambiguity and resist the urge to prematurely close on a familiar solution.

6. **Model synergistic behavior in groups** — be the person who says "I think there's a third alternative we haven't considered yet." This invitation, offered consistently, changes team culture.

## Examples

**Example 1: The management team stuck in compromise**
A leadership team repeatedly compromises on strategic direction — each meeting produces decisions that partially satisfy everyone and fully satisfy no one. Covey would diagnose this as a trust deficit: people are protecting their domains rather than genuinely opening their perspectives. The intervention: spend a meeting doing nothing but understanding each other's views (Habit 5) before anyone advocates for a position. The output is often a third alternative no one had walked in with.

**Example 2: The marriage stuck in "your way vs. my way"**
A couple consistently resolves disagreements by alternating — sometimes her way, sometimes his. The relationship is fair but never creative. Covey would point to what is being left on the table: their differences in perspective are not a problem to manage but a resource to mine. What would it look like to ask: "What do we both want, and is there a way to get there that uses both our perspectives?" The question alone opens a different quality of conversation.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Pseudo-synergy**: Going through the motions of collaboration while already knowing the conclusion you want. People recognize when a "collaborative process" is actually a managed negotiation toward a predetermined outcome. This destroys trust and forecloses genuine synergy.

2. **Valuing harmony over truth**: Some teams achieve a pleasant atmosphere by avoiding real disagreement. This is the opposite of synergy — it is the suppression of the very differences that could generate creative solutions. Covey: "The strength of the relationship is not measured by its absence of conflict but by its capacity to handle conflict creatively."

3. **Stopping at compromise**: Compromise is better than "my way or the highway," but it is not synergy. Compromise produces the average of two positions; synergy produces something better than either original position. Always ask whether a third alternative exists before settling for a split difference.
