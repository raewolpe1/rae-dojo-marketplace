# Adler and Work

## When to Use
- When work-related problems need to be understood through the lens of social interest and the life tasks.
- When someone's relationship to work is either avoidant or compulsive.
- When career decisions need to be evaluated for their alignment with contribution and community.

## Core Concept
Work is one of Adler's three life tasks — alongside love and friendship — that every person must address. In Adler's framework, work is not merely employment or career. It is the task of contribution: making oneself useful to the community through productive activity. Work in this sense includes paid employment, but also volunteering, domestic labour, caregiving, creative production, and any form of activity that serves others.

The person's approach to work reveals their lifestyle. The person who works compulsively may be compensating for inferiority by proving their worth through productivity — a movement toward personal superiority rather than genuine contribution. The person who avoids work may be safeguarding against the possibility of failure — if they do not try, they cannot be found wanting. The person who exploits others at work has substituted personal gain for social interest. And the person who finds genuine satisfaction in useful work — work that serves others and develops their own competence — demonstrates healthy social interest in the work domain.

Adler observed that problems in one life task often mask or compensate for avoidance of another. The workaholic may be avoiding the life task of love. The person who cannot hold a job may be avoiding the life task of work to protect their self-concept. The person who is socially charming but professionally unproductive may be using the friendship task to compensate for avoiding the work task. Understanding these patterns requires looking at all three life tasks together, not in isolation.

The Adlerian approach to career guidance begins not with aptitude tests but with lifestyle analysis: what is this person's characteristic movement? What are their convictions about themselves and the world? What are they striving toward? The right career is one that channels the person's striving in a socially useful direction — not one that simply matches their skills to market demand.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess the work life task.** Is this person contributing through their work? Are they avoiding work? Are they using work to avoid other life tasks?
2. **Look at all three life tasks together.** Work problems are rarely just about work.
3. **Evaluate the purpose of work problems.** Is unemployment, underperformance, or overwork serving a safeguarding function?
4. **Connect work to contribution.** Help the person find or reconnect with the socially useful dimension of their work.
5. **Address discouragement.** If the person has given up on work, understand why before prescribing solutions.

## Examples
**Situation:** A mid-career development professional says he has lost passion for his work. He goes through the motions but finds no satisfaction. He wonders whether he should change careers entirely.
**Application:** Adler would not begin with career counselling. He would begin with the lifestyle: "You entered development work — why? What attracted you? ... You wanted to make a difference. And now you feel you are not making a difference. Has the work changed, or have you? ... When you say you have lost passion, do you mean the work is no longer useful, or that you no longer feel your contribution matters?" Often the problem is not the work but the person's relationship to it. If the professional has been doing the same type of project for fifteen years, the challenge may have diminished and with it the sense of growth. If organisational politics have disconnected him from the impact of his work, the loss of visible contribution may be the issue. If he has been passed over for promotion, discouragement may be driving the withdrawal. The solution depends on the diagnosis: sometimes the answer is a new role, sometimes a different relationship to the current one, and sometimes the work problem is masking an avoidance of one of the other life tasks.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat work as the only measure of contribution.
**Why:** Adler included love and friendship as equal life tasks. A person who contributes magnificently through work but neglects love and friendship is not well-adjusted — they are avoiding two-thirds of the human programme.

**Don't:** Assume career dissatisfaction is always about the career.
**Why:** Dissatisfaction at work may reflect a lifestyle pattern that would follow the person into any job. Changing careers without changing the lifestyle recreates the problem in a new setting.
