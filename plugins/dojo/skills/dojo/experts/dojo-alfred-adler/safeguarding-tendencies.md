# Safeguarding Tendencies

## When to Use
- When symptoms, excuses, or self-defeating behaviours are protecting the person from facing a life task.
- When the purposive function of apparently irrational behaviour needs to be understood.
- When someone is creating distance between themselves and the things they need to do.

## Core Concept
Safeguarding tendencies are the strategies a discouraged person uses to protect their self-esteem from the threat of failure at the life tasks. They are Adler's answer to the question "Why would someone do something that is obviously not in their interest?" The answer is: because the behaviour serves a purpose that is more important to the person than the apparent cost — it protects the fictional final goal from being tested and found wanting.

Adler identified several common safeguarding strategies. Excuses involve creating obstacles that explain failure in advance: "I would have succeeded if only..." The person who drinks before an exam, procrastinates until the deadline makes quality impossible, or chooses partners who are obviously unsuitable is creating built-in explanations for failure that protect the self-concept from the more threatening conclusion: "I tried my best and it wasn't good enough."

Aggression involves moving against others to maintain superiority: depreciation (reducing others to feel taller), accusation (blaming others for one's own difficulties), and self-accusation (suffering conspicuously to control others through guilt). Each involves redirecting the focus from the person's avoidance of the life tasks to something external.

Distance-creating mechanisms involve putting space between oneself and the life tasks: retreating (withdrawal, isolation), standing still (refusing to move forward), hesitation (approaching the task but never quite beginning), and constructing obstacles (creating barriers that justify not proceeding).

The concept of safeguarding is Adler's most psychologically sophisticated contribution. It explains self-sabotage, procrastination, psychosomatic symptoms, and much chronic self-defeating behaviour without attributing them to masochism, unconscious conflict, or irrationality. The behaviour makes perfect sense — it just serves the lifestyle's need for self-protection rather than the life tasks' need for engagement.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the safeguarding strategy.** What is the person doing that creates distance from the life task?
2. **Identify what is being protected.** What would be threatened if the person fully engaged?
3. **Expose the purpose gently.** Not as accusation but as observation: "I notice that something happens every time you get close to..."
4. **Connect to the life tasks.** Which task is being avoided? Work? Love? Friendship?
5. **Encourage engagement.** Once the safeguarding is visible, help the person take one small step toward the task they have been avoiding.

## Examples
**Situation:** A talented evaluator consistently submits reports late. She always has a reason: the data was delayed, a team member was sick, the client changed the scope. Her work is excellent when it arrives, but her reputation suffers.
**Application:** Adler would see the lateness not as a time-management problem but as a safeguarding strategy. By submitting late, she protects herself from the full judgment of her work: "If I had had more time, it would have been even better." The lateness creates a built-in excuse that ensures she is never evaluated at her true capacity. The fictional final goal is probably perfection: "I must produce flawless work." Since no work is flawless, submitting on time would expose the gap between her ideal and her reality. Submitting late preserves the illusion: the gap is explained by time pressure, not by limitation. "You are one of the most capable evaluators I know of. And yet you are consistently late. What do you think the lateness accomplishes for you? ... It is possible that the lateness protects you from something more frightening than a missed deadline: the possibility that your best work, submitted on time, might not be perfect. If that is the case, the question is not 'How do I manage my time?' but 'What would it mean if my work were merely very good rather than perfect?'"

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Confront safeguarding aggressively.
**Why:** The person is protecting themselves because they are discouraged. Aggressive confrontation confirms the threat and intensifies the safeguarding. The approach must be warm and curious.

**Don't:** Assume conscious manipulation.
**Why:** Safeguarding usually operates outside awareness. The person genuinely believes the excuses. Making the pattern visible is the work — it cannot begin from accusation.
