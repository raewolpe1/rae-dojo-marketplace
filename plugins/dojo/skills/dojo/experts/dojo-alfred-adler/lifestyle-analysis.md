# Lifestyle Analysis

## When to Use
- When recurring patterns suggest a consistent underlying logic that needs to be made visible.
- When the person's characteristic way of approaching life needs to be understood as a unity.
- When individual problems need to be seen in the context of the whole person.

## Core Concept
The lifestyle (*Lebensstil*) is Adler's term for the person's characteristic, unified pattern of moving through the world. It is formed in the first four to five years of life as the child creatively responds to their situation — their family constellation, their position among siblings, their physical endowments, and the social world around them. The lifestyle includes the person's self-concept ("I am..."), their worldview ("The world is..."), their ethical convictions ("Therefore, I should..."), and their fictional final goal — the imagined ideal state toward which all behaviour is oriented.

The lifestyle is not a collection of traits but an organised unity. Everything the person does — their choice of career, their approach to relationships, their response to difficulty, their symptoms, their dreams, their earliest memories — expresses the same underlying pattern. Adler insisted that if you understood a person's lifestyle, you could predict how they would respond to any situation, because the lifestyle determines the angle of approach. A person whose lifestyle is organised around the conviction "I must never show weakness" will approach work, love, friendship, and illness in predictably consistent ways — all oriented toward concealing vulnerability.

The lifestyle operates largely outside awareness. The person does not experience it as a set of beliefs they hold but as the way things simply are. "I am the kind of person who..." feels like a description of reality, not a creative construction. Making the lifestyle conscious is one of the primary goals of Adlerian therapy — once the person can see their pattern, they can evaluate it: "Is this serving me? Is this serving others? Is there another way?"

Lifestyle analysis involves gathering data from multiple sources: the family constellation (birth order, sibling relationships, parenting styles), early recollections (the memories the person selects from childhood), the presenting problem (how does the symptom fit the lifestyle?), and observation of the person's behaviour in the therapeutic relationship itself (how they relate to the therapist reveals their characteristic approach to social situations).

## How to Apply
1. **Gather lifestyle data.** Family constellation, birth order, early recollections, current problems, relational patterns.
2. **Look for the unity.** What is the common thread across different domains of the person's life?
3. **Identify the private logic.** What convictions about self, world, and others are organising the pattern?
4. **Make the lifestyle visible.** Present it tentatively: "It seems like you approach most situations as if..."
5. **Evaluate its usefulness.** Does this lifestyle serve the life tasks? Where does it help? Where does it hinder?

## Examples
**Situation:** A woman describes a pattern: she starts projects with enormous energy, then abandons them when they become difficult. This pattern appears in her work, her relationships, and her hobbies.
**Application:** Adler would see this as a lifestyle pattern, not a series of unrelated failures. The lifestyle logic might be: "I am talented but only if things come easily. If I have to struggle, it means I am not talented enough, and I must withdraw before that is exposed." The fictional final goal is perfection without effort. The self-concept is "I am gifted" — but this is a fragile identity that cannot survive the evidence of struggle. The worldview is "Things should come easily to truly capable people." So she starts brilliantly (confirming the self-concept) and withdraws at the first difficulty (protecting it from disconfirmation). The lifestyle serves a purpose: it preserves the illusion of effortless talent by ensuring she never tests it against sustained challenge. Making this visible — "You seem to live as if struggling means failing. What if struggling means learning?" — opens the possibility of revision.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Confuse Adler's "lifestyle" with the colloquial meaning.
**Why:** In everyday usage, "lifestyle" means preferences and habits (an active lifestyle, a healthy lifestyle). In Adler's usage, it means the deep, unified pattern of convictions and movements that organises the entire personality.

**Don't:** Present the lifestyle as destiny.
**Why:** The lifestyle was creatively constructed by the child. What was constructed can be reconstructed. This is the basis of therapeutic optimism in Individual Psychology.
