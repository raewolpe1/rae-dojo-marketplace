# Fictional Final Goal

## When to Use
- When someone's behaviour only makes sense in light of an unstated ideal they are pursuing.
- When the teleological principle of Adler's psychology needs to be explained.
- When the guiding fiction is producing unrealistic expectations or useless striving.

## Core Concept
Adler borrowed the concept of fictions from the philosopher Hans Vaihinger, whose *Philosophy of As If* argued that human beings organise their behaviour around ideas that are not literally true but are treated as if they were. Adler applied this insight to personality: every person creates, in early childhood, a fictional final goal — an imagined ideal state of perfection, security, or superiority toward which all their behaviour is oriented. This goal is fictional in that it does not exist as an achievable destination, but it is functional in that it organises and directs all movement.

The fictional final goal is rarely articulated consciously. It operates as an implicit assumption: "If I could just be perfectly competent, then I would be safe." "If everyone admired me, then I would be worthwhile." "If I never depended on anyone, then I could not be hurt." The goal determines the lifestyle: all the person's characteristic patterns — their approach to work, love, friendship, difficulty — are movements toward this imagined destination.

In healthy development, the fictional final goal is flexible and oriented toward social interest. The person strives toward competence and contribution without rigidly insisting on a specific form of perfection. In neurotic development, the goal becomes rigid, extreme, and oriented toward personal superiority rather than social contribution. The person who must be perfectly in control at all times, the person who cannot tolerate any sign of weakness, the person who must be universally admired — each is pursuing a rigid fiction that organises enormous effort and produces enormous suffering.

Therapeutic work with the fictional final goal involves making it visible, examining its origins and its consequences, and helping the person develop a more flexible, socially oriented alternative. The goal is never to eliminate the guiding fiction — humans cannot function without one — but to replace a rigid, self-serving fiction with a flexible, community-serving one.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the fiction.** What ideal state is this person moving toward? What would "success" look like in their private logic?
2. **Make it explicit.** Often the person has never articulated the goal. Naming it makes it examinable.
3. **Examine its consequences.** What does this fiction cost? What does it prevent?
4. **Trace its origins.** How did this fiction develop? What childhood situation made it seem necessary?
5. **Develop a more flexible alternative.** Not the abandonment of goals but the relaxation of rigid, absolute goals.

## Examples
**Situation:** A woman works relentlessly toward an unattainable standard of professional achievement. Every accomplishment is immediately devalued — "That wasn't good enough" — and replaced by a new, higher target. She is exhausted, isolated, and unable to enjoy her considerable success.
**Application:** The fictional final goal is implicit: "If I achieve enough, I will finally be worthy." This fiction organises everything — the relentless work, the devaluation of accomplishments (because each one fails to produce the promised feeling of worth), the inability to rest (because resting means accepting the current, insufficiently perfect state). Adler would make the fiction visible: "You seem to be pursuing a destination where achievement finally makes you feel worthy. But you have achieved a great deal and you do not feel worthy. What if the fiction is the problem? What if no amount of achievement can produce the feeling you seek, because worth does not come from achievement? Where else might it come from?" The goal is not to stop striving — she is talented and her work has value — but to decouple worth from achievement and reconnect striving to contribution rather than self-validation.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss the fictional goal as mere fantasy.
**Why:** The fiction has real motivational power. It organises the person's entire life. Dismissing it trivialises their experience.

**Don't:** Try to eliminate goals entirely.
**Why:** Humans need direction. The issue is not having a guiding fiction but having one that is rigid, absolute, and disconnected from social interest.
