# Courage and Encouragement

## When to Use
- When someone has stopped trying because they have lost faith in their capacity.
- When discouragement is the root of the presenting problem.
- When encouragement needs to be understood as a specific technique, not mere positivity.

## Core Concept
Adler considered courage the foundational virtue and discouragement the root of most psychological dysfunction. Courage, in Adler's usage, is not the absence of fear but the willingness to face the life tasks — work, love, friendship — despite feelings of inferiority and uncertainty. The courageous person acts even when they are not sure they will succeed. The discouraged person stops acting because they have concluded that success is impossible.

Discouragement is the mechanism by which inferiority feelings become an inferiority complex. The feeling of inferiority is universal and healthy — it motivates development. But when the person loses faith in their ability to close the gap between where they are and where they need to be, the feeling becomes paralysing. The discouraged person develops symptoms, excuses, and safeguarding behaviours — all serving the purpose of avoiding the life tasks that they no longer believe they can manage.

Encouragement is Adler's primary therapeutic and educational tool — and he considered it more important than insight, interpretation, or technique. Encouragement is not the same as praise, reassurance, or cheerleading. Praise evaluates from above ("Good job!") and reinforces dependence on external validation. Reassurance denies difficulty ("Don't worry, it'll be fine") and is often disbelieved. Encouragement acknowledges difficulty honestly while affirming the person's capacity to face it: "This is hard, and you can handle it."

Genuine encouragement has specific qualities. It focuses on effort and process rather than outcome. It recognises the person's strengths without inflating them. It communicates trust in the person's ability to cope. It treats setbacks as information rather than as evidence of inadequacy. And it is grounded in honest assessment — encouragement that is obviously unearned is detected immediately and increases distrust.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the discouragement.** When did this person stop believing they could manage? What happened?
2. **Acknowledge the difficulty.** Do not minimise the challenge. Encouragement that denies reality is not credible.
3. **Affirm genuine capacity.** Point to evidence — specific instances where the person has coped, contributed, or succeeded.
4. **Focus on effort, not outcome.** The willingness to try is the courage. The result is secondary.
5. **Be honest.** False encouragement is worse than none. The person must believe that your assessment is genuine.

## Examples
**Situation:** A consultant has been asked to lead a major evaluation for the first time. She is convinced she will fail and wants to decline the assignment.
**Application:** Adler would not say "You'll be fine" (reassurance that denies difficulty) or "You're brilliant, of course you can do it" (praise that the person cannot believe). He would say: "Leading your first major evaluation is genuinely daunting. You are right that it is difficult. But let me ask you something: have you ever done field research before? ... You have, many times. Have you ever managed a team on smaller projects? ... You have. Have you ever analysed data and written a report? ... Many times. What exactly is different about this project? ... The scale? The visibility? Those are real challenges. But the skills you need are skills you already have. You are not being asked to do something you have never done — you are being asked to do what you have done, on a larger canvas. The question is whether you have the courage to try something that might not be perfect. I think you do."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Encourage without honesty.
**Why:** "You can do anything you set your mind to" is not encouragement — it is a fiction that the person will test against reality and find wanting. Genuine encouragement acknowledges real limitations while affirming real capacities.

**Don't:** Skip to encouragement without understanding the discouragement.
**Why:** The discouragement has a history and a logic. Understanding why the person lost courage is essential for offering encouragement that addresses the actual obstacle.
