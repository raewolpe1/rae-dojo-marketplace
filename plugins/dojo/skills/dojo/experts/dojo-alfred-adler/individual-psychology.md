# Individual Psychology

## When to Use
- When someone needs to understand Adler's system as a whole before exploring its parts.
- When the relationship between Adler, Freud, and Jung needs to be clarified.
- When the basic principles of Individual Psychology need to be stated.

## Core Concept
Individual Psychology is the name Alfred Adler gave to his system of psychology, founded after his break from Freud in 1911. The name is commonly misunderstood. "Individual" does not mean focused on the isolated person (quite the opposite — Adler's psychology is among the most socially oriented in the field). It comes from the Latin *individuum*, meaning "undivided." Adler's fundamental premise is that the person is an indivisible unity — not split into id, ego, and superego, not divided between conscious and unconscious, but a whole being moving purposefully through a social world.

The system rests on several interlocking principles. First, teleology: all behaviour is goal-directed. Adler rejected Freud's deterministic drive theory and replaced it with a purposive model. People are not pushed by the past but pulled by the future — by their vision of where they want to go. To understand a person, you must understand their goals, not their traumas. Second, holism: the person is an indivisible unity. Thoughts, feelings, and behaviours are not separate systems but expressions of a single lifestyle. Third, social embeddedness: the individual exists only in relation to others. All psychological problems are fundamentally social problems — failures of cooperation, contribution, or connection. Fourth, phenomenology: what matters is not objective reality but how the person perceives and interprets their situation. The same event produces different responses in different people because of their different lifestyles. Fifth, creativity: the person is not a passive product of heredity and environment but an active creator of their own lifestyle. Adler called this the "creative power of the individual."

Adler was arguably the most practically influential psychologist of the twentieth century, though he receives less credit than he deserves. His ideas anticipated or directly influenced humanistic psychology (Maslow explicitly acknowledged Adler), cognitive-behavioural therapy (the focus on beliefs and their modification), positive psychology (strengths, meaning, contribution), family therapy (birth order, family constellation), and the self-help movement (Dale Carnegie attended Adler's lectures). When someone speaks of inferiority complexes, overcompensation, lifestyle, or the importance of belonging, they are speaking Adler's language — usually without knowing it.

## How to Apply
1. **Think holistically.** Do not separate the person's problem from their whole way of living. The symptom expresses the lifestyle.
2. **Think teleologically.** Ask what the behaviour is trying to accomplish, not what caused it.
3. **Think socially.** Locate the problem in the person's relationship to the three life tasks: work, love, friendship.
4. **Think creatively.** The person chose their lifestyle — they can choose to modify it.
5. **Think encouragingly.** The goal is to restore the person's courage to face the life tasks.

## Examples
**Situation:** A colleague describes Adler as "Freud-lite" — just a lesser version of psychoanalysis.
**Application:** This is a common misunderstanding. Adler did not simply modify Freud — he replaced Freud's entire framework. Where Freud saw drives (libido, aggression), Adler saw goals (superiority striving, social interest). Where Freud saw the person divided (id/ego/superego), Adler saw the person as a unity. Where Freud located pathology in repressed unconscious conflicts, Adler located it in mistaken goals and discouraged movement. Where Freud's therapy was archaeological — digging into the past — Adler's was orienteering — determining where the person is heading and whether the direction serves the community. The systems share a historical origin but are fundamentally different in their assumptions about human nature, motivation, and change.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce Adler to "the inferiority complex guy."
**Why:** The inferiority feeling is one element of a comprehensive system that includes lifestyle, social interest, birth order, early recollections, fictional finalism, the life tasks, and a democratic philosophy of human relations.

**Don't:** Present Individual Psychology as outdated.
**Why:** Many of Adler's core ideas — the importance of belonging, the purposive nature of behaviour, the social construction of meaning, the therapeutic value of encouragement — are more mainstream now than when he proposed them.
