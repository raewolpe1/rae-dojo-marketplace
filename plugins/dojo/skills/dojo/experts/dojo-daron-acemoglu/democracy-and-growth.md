# Democracy and Growth

## When to Use
- When debating whether democracy helps or hinders growth.
- When responding to authoritarian-development arguments.
- When evaluating democratic-reform programmes.

## Fails When
- **Democracy is treated as sufficient for development.** It is contributory; outcomes depend on the broader institutional structure.

## Core Concept

The cross-country evidence on democracy and growth has been contested. Acemoglu and co-authors (notably the 2019 *Democracy Does Cause Growth* paper in JPE) made a major contribution: using more comprehensive democracy data and improved identification, they showed that democratisations cause approximately 20% higher GDP per capita over 25 years, holding other factors constant.

The mechanisms:

**Democracy increases investment in human capital.** Democratic regimes systematically invest more in education and health for the broad population. The mechanism is straightforward: voter constituencies demand and reward this.

**Democracy reduces social unrest and political instability.** Democratic resolution of conflicts is generally less destructive than authoritarian repression or revolutionary upheaval.

**Democracy improves economic-policy quality over time.** The accountability mechanism punishes catastrophic policy errors and corrects them. Authoritarian systems can sustain bad policies longer because the feedback mechanism is weaker.

**Democracy supports inclusive economic institutions.** The franchise creates a broad constituency for inclusive economic rules; the electoral mechanism constrains elite capture of economic policy.

The qualifications matter:

**The effect is gradual and long-run.** The 20% gain is over 25 years. In the short run, democratic transitions can be disruptive and growth-suppressing. Patience is required.

**Nominal democracy is not sufficient.** Many countries that hold elections have substantially extractive economic institutions. The democracy-growth link is mediated by whether democratic politics actually changes the economic-institutional structure.

**Democracy at the wrong moment can fail.** Transitions in highly unequal societies with weak state capacity are particularly fragile. The narrow-corridor analysis is relevant: democracy without sufficient state capacity is hollow; state capacity without democracy is despotic.

**The Chinese case is an apparent counterexample.** China has grown rapidly under authoritarian institutions for decades. Acemoglu's reading: this is catch-up growth from a very low base under partial economic liberalisation; the authoritarian system constrains rather than enables the move to frontier innovation; the recent slowdown and recentralisation under Xi are consistent with the prediction that the model has limits.

For development practice, the implication is that democratic-institutional support is part of the development project, not separable from it. The conditions under which democracy delivers economic outcomes — broad franchise, competitive elections, constraints on the executive, accountable bureaucracy — are exactly the conditions for inclusive economic institutions.

## How to Apply

1. **Recognise the long-run, mediated relationship** between democracy and growth.
2. **Diagnose the substantive content of democracy** — beyond elections, what is the constitutional structure?
3. **Support democratic deepening alongside economic-institutional reform.**
4. **Be patient about transition disruptions** while protecting the inclusive direction.

## Examples

**Situation:** Comparison of post-apartheid South Africa (democratic, partial economic-institutional reform) vs Singapore (semi-democratic, strong economic institutions) vs Ethiopia under EPRDF (authoritarian-developmental).

**Application:** Each illustrates a different combination. South Africa shows democratic inclusion without sufficient economic-institutional reform — disappointing growth, inequality persistence, political fragility. Singapore shows narrow democracy plus strong economic institutions — high growth, sustained, but a model with its own narrow-corridor questions. Ethiopia under EPRDF showed authoritarian-developmental growth that fragmented when the political coalition collapsed. Each combination has costs. The Acemoglu view: the South African model is the most inclusive starting point but requires the economic-institutional work to deliver.

**Result:** A diagnostic that supports democratic deepening as part of the development project, not despite it.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat democracy as instrumentally optional in development.
**Why:** The evidence supports its causal contribution; the political-philosophical case for its intrinsic value adds further weight.

**Don't:** Treat any election as proof of democracy.
**Why:** The substantive content — constraints on executive, free press, competitive politics, judicial independence — is what matters.
