# Technology and Shared Prosperity

## When to Use
- When the question is whether technological change automatically improves welfare.
- When debating innovation policy from a political-economy perspective.
- When framing the AI debate in long-run historical terms.

## Fails When
- **The argument is taken as anti-technology.** Acemoglu and Johnson are pro-technology under inclusive institutional conditions.

## Core Concept

Acemoglu and Johnson's *Power and Progress* (2023) makes the historical and theoretical case that technological change does not automatically produce shared prosperity. Whether new technologies improve ordinary people's lives depends on the political-economic structure shaping their development and deployment.

The historical argument:

**The first thousand years of agricultural innovation produced little gain for ordinary people.** Productivity gains were captured by elites — landowners, religious authorities, the state. Peasants did not get richer despite centuries of technological progress.

**The early Industrial Revolution was harsh for workers.** Wages stagnated or fell during the first decades of British industrialisation despite massive productivity gains. The improvement in working-class living standards came later — driven by political mobilisation (Chartism, trade unions, electoral reform) that shifted bargaining power and forced the gains to be shared.

**The 20th-century middle-class prosperity was not automatic.** It required strong unions, mass-production technologies amenable to broad workforces, regulated capitalism, social welfare states, and policy choices about technology deployment.

**The post-1980 era reversed the pattern.** Technological change combined with weakening labour institutions, deregulation, and ideological shifts produced productivity gains that concentrated at the top. Middle-class wages stagnated; inequality rose dramatically.

**AI is the next test.** Whether AI produces broad prosperity or further concentration depends on the institutional choices being made now: how it is developed, who owns the IP and infrastructure, how labour-market institutions adapt, how regulation shapes deployment.

**Implications:**

**Technology choice is political.** What gets developed reflects who funds the research, what they value, and what political-economic structure shapes deployment. The "natural" trajectory of technology is the trajectory shaped by current political-economic conditions.

**Productivity gains do not automatically translate to shared welfare.** The translation requires institutions — labour unions, progressive taxation, public investment, regulation, social insurance — that distribute the gains.

**Innovation policy should be evaluated on distributional grounds, not just on growth.** Subsidies that accelerate displacement-biased innovation can reduce welfare even if they raise GDP.

**The state's role in technology development matters.** Public investment in basic research, technology procurement, regulation, and labour-market policy collectively shape the trajectory. State retreat from these functions concentrates the gains.

## How to Apply

1. **Treat technological trajectory as choice-dependent, not fixed.**
2. **Evaluate technology policy by distributional impact, not just innovation rate.**
3. **Strengthen the institutions that distribute gains** — labour, public investment, regulation.
4. **Be sceptical of techno-utopian narratives** that assume diffusion-to-shared-prosperity is automatic.

## Examples

**Situation:** South African industrial policy in an era of automation and AI.

**Application:** The Acemoglu-Johnson lens: technology adoption in South Africa under current conditions will likely follow the displacement-biased pattern of the past decades — capital intensifies, labour share declines, inequality widens. Industrial policy can shape the trajectory: subsidies conditioned on employment outcomes; SME support that adopts labour-complementing rather than labour-substituting technology; sector-specific deployment guidelines (e.g., in retail, agriculture, manufacturing); strong labour-market institutions that hold up wages as productivity rises. Without these, technology will deepen the existing inequality.

**Result:** A technology-policy framing that explicitly addresses distribution alongside innovation.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat technological progress as automatically good.
**Why:** Distributional effects can be severe even when aggregate productivity rises.

**Don't:** Treat the argument as Luddite.
**Why:** The case is for shaping technology toward shared prosperity, not for opposing innovation.
