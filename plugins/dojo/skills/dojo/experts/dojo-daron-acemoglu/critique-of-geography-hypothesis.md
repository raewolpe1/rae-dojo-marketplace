# Critique of the Geography Hypothesis

## When to Use
- When geographic factors (climate, disease, distance from coast) are invoked as the primary cause of underdevelopment.
- When responding to Sachs's tropical-disease argument or Diamond's geographic determinism.
- When the implicit policy is fatalism.

## Fails When
- **Geography is taken to matter not at all.** Geography matters; the claim is that it matters mostly through its effects on institutions.

## Core Concept

The geography hypothesis (advanced most prominently by Jeffrey Sachs and others) argues that tropical climate, disease environments, distance from coastlines and trade routes, and natural-resource endowments are primary determinants of long-run development. The Acemoglu critique:

**The reversal of fortune is decisive evidence.** The areas of European colonisation that were most prosperous in 1500 (densely settled, urbanised, sophisticated agriculture — Mexico, Peru, India) became relatively poor by 1900; the areas that were least prosperous (sparsely settled North America, Australia, southern South America) became relatively rich. Geography hasn't changed; institutions changed dramatically. The reversal contradicts geographic determinism. (AJR, *Reversal of Fortune*, QJE 2002).

**Within-region variation refutes pure geography.** Geographically similar areas have vastly different development outcomes (North vs South Korea; Botswana vs Zimbabwe; East vs West Germany before reunification). The variation is institutional.

**Geography operates through institutions.** Geographic factors did shape what kind of colonial institutions emerged — tropical disease made settler-colonial institutions less feasible, so extractive institutions emerged instead in those regions. Once the institutions emerged, they were the proximate driver. Geography's effect runs through this channel, not directly through climate-on-productivity.

**The tropical-disease argument is overstated.** Sachs argues that malaria and tropical diseases directly reduce productivity. Acemoglu and co-authors argue that the disease environment matters mostly through historical effects on institutional formation, with current effects much smaller than the institutional channel.

**The "natural resource curse" is also institutional.** Resource-rich countries with extractive institutions become more extractive when resources are discovered (rent-seeking dominates production); resource-rich countries with inclusive institutions (Norway, Botswana) manage the resources productively. The institutional structure determines whether resources are a blessing or curse.

The disagreement is not absolute. Geography matters — it's just not the fundamental cause that geographic determinism makes it. Institutions are the fundamental cause; geography is one of several factors shaping which institutions emerged.

The policy implications are very different. Geographic determinism implies development is largely outside policy reach — climate cannot be changed. Institutional theory implies development is reachable through institutional reform, however slow and difficult.

## How to Apply

1. **When geographic determinism appears, point to the reversal of fortune.**
2. **Distinguish geography's historical effect on institutional formation from its current direct effects.**
3. **Treat geography as a constraint to be worked with, not destiny.**
4. **Focus policy attention on institutions** as the addressable cause.

## Examples

**Situation:** A South African policy debate invokes geographic disadvantages (distance from major markets, semi-arid climate, water stress) as primary constraints.

**Application:** Geography matters but doesn't explain why South Africa's per-capita income has stagnated relative to peers since the 1970s, why inequality is among the world's highest, or why state capability has degraded over the past decade. These are institutional matters. Geography sets some constraints (water availability shapes agricultural policy) but the binding constraints on development are institutional. The reform task lies in institutions, not in lamenting geography.

**Result:** A policy framing that addresses the actually-addressable constraints.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Dismiss geography entirely.
**Why:** It shapes the institutional terrain. The argument is about primary causality, not zero causality.

**Don't:** Treat the institutional argument as deterministic in the opposite direction.
**Why:** Institutions matter most but other factors (human capital, technology, social norms) also contribute.
