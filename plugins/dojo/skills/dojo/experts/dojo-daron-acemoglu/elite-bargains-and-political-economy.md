# Elite Bargains and Political Economy

## When to Use
- When designing reform programmes.
- When analysing why reforms succeed or fail.
- When evaluating the feasibility of policy change.

## Fails When
- **The framework is reduced to crude elite-vs-mass dichotomies.** Real elite bargains are complex, fragmented, and shifting.

## Core Concept

Politics is the analysis of who has power, what they want, and how they get and keep it. Acemoglu's framework places elite bargains at the centre of understanding institutional change and persistence:

**Elites are not monolithic.** Any country has multiple elite groupings — economic (industrial, agrarian, financial, resource), political (party, executive, judicial, security), professional (technocratic, intellectual, religious), regional (urban-rural, capital-province). The political settlement is a bargain among these groupings.

**The settlement determines the institutional structure.** What economic institutions exist, what political institutions exist, and what policies get implemented all reflect the elite bargain. A change in the bargain produces a change in the institutions; a stable bargain produces stable institutions.

**Reforms succeed when they are compatible with the elite bargain or when they shift it.** A reform that threatens the existing bargain without a counter-coalition will be blocked or reversed. A reform that creates winners who become a new coalition can shift the bargain.

**Three types of reform dynamics:**

1. **Inclusive coalition reform.** The reform broadens the winning coalition (e.g., extending the franchise, securing smallholder property rights). The winners become a defending constituency.

2. **Elite-bargain reform.** The reform reshuffles the existing elite (e.g., displacing rent-extracting elites with productive elites). The new elite coalition supports the reform.

3. **Reform without coalition.** The reform is imposed by external pressure or technocratic process without altering the underlying coalition. These reforms typically reverse when the external pressure abates.

**The political-economy diagnostic asks:**

- Who are the major elite groupings?
- What does each gain or lose from the proposed reform?
- Which groupings are politically organised? Which can mobilise resources?
- What is the credible threat from those who lose?
- What is the credible benefit to those who gain?
- Is there a sequencing that builds the coalition step by step?

**Implications for development advice.** Generic policy advice — "improve governance", "reduce corruption", "liberalise trade", "build state capability" — is hollow without the political-economy analysis. The relevant question is always: in this specific elite-bargain context, how does this reform become feasible?

## How to Apply

1. **Map the elite groupings** relevant to the reform.
2. **Identify winners and losers** in detail.
3. **Evaluate the political organisation** of each group.
4. **Design reforms that build winning coalitions** rather than relying on technocratic argument.
5. **Sequence reforms to compound coalition strength.**

## Examples

**Situation:** South African anti-corruption reform — apparently popular, technically straightforward, persistently difficult to implement.

**Application:** The political-economy diagnostic: the elite bargain that emerged after 1994 included substantial rent extraction by politically connected elites across multiple parties and sectors. Anti-corruption reform threatens this directly. The winners (broad public, business sector competing for legitimate contracts, professional civil servants) are diffuse and politically fragmented. The losers (politically connected extractive elites) are concentrated and politically organised. Without sequencing that builds the winners' political coalition first (e.g., procurement reform that creates a constituency of SMEs benefiting from competitive contracting; professionalisation of public service that creates a coalition of capable officials; civil-society capacity that mobilises beneficiaries), the reform is technically right but politically unwinnable.

**Result:** A reform strategy that explicitly builds the political coalition, not just designs the technical content.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the political-economy analysis as fatalistic.
**Why:** It identifies what has to be done, not why nothing can be done.

**Don't:** Reduce political economy to crude "follow the money".
**Why:** Real elite bargains include ideological, status, and identity dimensions alongside material interest.
