# Narrow Corridor Theory

## When to Use
- When evaluating state capability vs civil liberties trade-offs.
- When analysing failed states, despotic states, or fragile democracies.
- When designing state-building or democratisation programmes.

## Fails When
- **The framework is treated as binary.** States move along the strong-weak spectrum continuously.

## Core Concept

Acemoglu and Robinson's *The Narrow Corridor* (2019) develops a framework for thinking about the state-society relationship. Two threats to liberty exist:

**The Absent Leviathan.** A state too weak to provide order, enforce contracts, or deliver public goods. Citizens are at the mercy of armed factions, traditional structures, or private violence. Examples: Somalia in much of the post-1991 period; parts of the DRC; sections of failed-state ranges.

**The Despotic Leviathan.** A state strong enough to deliver order but unconstrained by society — the elite uses state power against the citizenry. Examples: imperial China, contemporary North Korea, much of historical absolutism.

**The Shackled Leviathan.** A state strong enough to deliver public goods AND constrained by a strong, mobilised society that holds it accountable. This is the configuration that produces liberty. The state delivers services; society constrains its abuse. Examples: contemporary Sweden, Denmark, post-1688 Britain (over time).

The corridor is narrow because both state strength and societal strength must grow together. A society that demands constraints without a strong state cannot get the public goods it needs; a state that grows in capability without societal constraint becomes despotic. The two have to develop in parallel — the "Red Queen" dynamic, where both must keep running to stay in the same position relative to each other.

**Implications for development:**

**Pure state-building is dangerous.** Building state capacity without simultaneously building societal capacity to constrain it produces despotism, not liberty.

**Pure society-building is hollow.** Civil society without an effective state operates in a context where its demands cannot be implemented.

**The post-conflict and post-transition contexts are particularly tricky.** They often involve weak states (legacy of conflict) and weak societies (legacy of repression). Building both simultaneously is the challenge.

**Mobilised societies matter, not just formal institutions.** The actual practice of holding the state accountable — through media, protest, civic associations, electoral competition — is what keeps the state in the corridor. The formal constitution is necessary but not sufficient.

**Path dependence is severe.** Societies in the Despotic or Absent Leviathan zones rarely move to the corridor without major shocks. Most societies stay where they are for centuries.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose the state-society balance.** Where on the framework is the country?
2. **Identify both state-capability gaps and societal-capability gaps.**
3. **Design reform that builds both** rather than focusing on only one.
4. **Look for the Red Queen dynamic.** Are state and society both strengthening?

## Examples

**Situation:** Mapping South Africa's post-1994 trajectory.

**Application:** South Africa inherited a substantial state (apartheid-era bureaucracy and security apparatus) and a substantial society (anti-apartheid civic, labour, and religious organisations). The early post-1994 years saw both dimensions strong — substantial state delivery, active civil society. Over the next decades, the balance shifted: state capability degraded (state capture, hollowing of departments); society remained mobilised but in some sectors weakened (decline of organised labour density, fragmentation of civic organisations). The country sits in an unstable position — not in the Despotic Leviathan zone (constitutional democracy holds), not in the Absent Leviathan zone (state still functions), but with both dimensions weakening. Rebuilding requires both state-capability work and civic re-strengthening, in parallel.

**Result:** A diagnostic that orients reform toward both state and society, not just one.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Focus on either state-building or civil-society support exclusively.
**Why:** Both are needed and they have to develop together.

**Don't:** Treat the corridor as easy to reach.
**Why:** Most countries never get there. The framework is realistic about the difficulty.
