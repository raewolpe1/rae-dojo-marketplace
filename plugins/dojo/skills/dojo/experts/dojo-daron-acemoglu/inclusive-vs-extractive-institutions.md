# Inclusive vs Extractive Institutions

## When to Use
- When the question is why one country develops and another doesn't.
- When debating the role of institutions vs other factors (geography, culture, human capital).
- When designing institutional reform.

## Fails When
- **The distinction is taken as a binary** rather than a continuum. Most countries are mixed.
- **The framework is applied without historical specificity.** Generic "inclusive institutions" advice is useless.

## Core Concept

The central conceptual distinction in Acemoglu's framework is between inclusive and extractive institutions, operating in both economic and political domains.

**Inclusive economic institutions:**
- Secure property rights for the broad population (not just elites).
- Enforce contracts impartially.
- Allow free entry into economic activity — anyone can start a firm, choose a profession, invest.
- Provide public goods (infrastructure, education, basic services).
- Distribute economic rewards broadly enough to motivate investment by many.

**Extractive economic institutions:**
- Concentrate property rights and economic opportunity in the hands of a narrow elite.
- Use the legal-administrative system to extract resources from the broader population for the benefit of the elite.
- Restrict entry to protect incumbent interests.
- Underprovide public goods because the elite captures revenues for itself.

**Inclusive political institutions:**
- Distribute political power broadly (broad franchise, competitive elections, freedom of association).
- Constrain the exercise of power (rule of law, judicial independence, separation of powers).
- Make political participation accessible.

**Extractive political institutions:**
- Concentrate political power in the hands of a narrow elite.
- Provide few constraints on the elite's exercise of that power.
- Make political participation costly or impossible for most.

The argument is that **inclusive economic and political institutions reinforce each other** in a virtuous circle, as do their extractive counterparts in a vicious circle. The combinations matter: extractive politics with partially inclusive economics (China today) is unstable; inclusive politics with partially extractive economics (some Latin American democracies) is fragile.

The implication for development: the central question is not "what policies should this country adopt?" but "what is the institutional structure, and what reforms would shift it toward inclusiveness?"

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose the existing institutional structure.** Where on the inclusive-extractive spectrum is the country, in both economic and political dimensions?
2. **Identify the reinforcing mechanisms.** What feedback loops sustain the current equilibrium?
3. **Locate potential leverage points.** Where might institutional reform be feasible given the political settlement?
4. **Be honest about timescales.** Institutional change is measured in decades or centuries.

## Examples

**Situation:** A donor asks why their investment in agricultural productivity in a country with extractive land institutions hasn't translated into broad-based rural development.

**Application:** The investment increased productivity but the gains were captured by landowners and political patrons because the land-rights system, the rural credit system, and the political institutions all extract from smallholders. Without institutional reform — secure smallholder titles, competitive credit markets, accountable local government — productivity gains will not translate to development.

**Result:** The donor reorients toward institutional reform as the binding constraint, accepting that returns will materialise over decades.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat "inclusive institutions" as a universal policy template.
**Why:** The specific institutional design that works depends on history, capability, and political settlement.

**Don't:** Conflate inclusive institutions with democracy alone.
**Why:** Many nominal democracies have extractive institutional structures beneath the electoral surface.
