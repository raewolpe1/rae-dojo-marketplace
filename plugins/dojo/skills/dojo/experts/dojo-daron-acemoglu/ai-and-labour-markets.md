# AI and Labour Markets

## When to Use
- When the question is about automation, AI, and employment.
- When debating whether technological change benefits workers.
- When designing labour-market policy under technological change.

## Fails When
- **The future is treated as predetermined.** Acemoglu's central claim is that the path depends on choices.

## Core Concept

The Acemoglu-Restrepo task-based framework (multiple AEA and JEP papers from 2018 onwards) is the most influential recent economic analysis of automation. The structure:

**Tasks, not jobs, are automated.** A job is a bundle of tasks; new technology automates some tasks but leaves others. The labour-market effect depends on which tasks are automated and what happens to the rest.

**Two competing effects on labour.** Automation has a displacement effect (substituting capital for labour in automated tasks, reducing labour demand) and a productivity effect (lowering production costs, expanding output, increasing labour demand for non-automated tasks). The net effect depends on which dominates.

**The reinstatement effect.** Historically, technological change has also created new tasks that did not exist before (telephone operators, computer programmers, app developers). When reinstatement is strong, labour demand rises even when automation displaces. When reinstatement is weak, automation dominates and labour suffers.

**Recent technological change has been displacement-biased.** Empirical work (notably the 2020 *Robots and Jobs* paper) shows that since the late 1980s, automation has displaced more labour than it has reinstated, contributing to the labour-share decline and wage stagnation for middle-skill workers.

**Generative AI raises the stakes.** Generative AI is capable of automating tasks previously thought protected — cognitive, creative, professional. The Acemoglu position: the labour-market impact depends on whether the technology is developed and deployed in ways that augment human work (creating reinstatement effects) or simply substitute for it. The choice is not technological but political-economic.

**Productivity claims are exaggerated.** Acemoglu has argued in recent work (2024) that the macro productivity gains from generative AI are likely smaller than the boosters claim — perhaps 0.5-1% per decade rather than the 10%+ in some projections. The reason: the tasks that generative AI does well are a subset of the economy; the deployment costs are real; and integration into productive workflows is slower than the demonstrations suggest.

**Distributional implications matter more than aggregate gains.** Even if AI produces modest productivity gains, the distribution depends on who captures the rents. Without policy intervention, the historical pattern suggests capital captures most of the gains.

## How to Apply

1. **Frame AI's labour-market impact as a choice, not destiny.**
2. **Distinguish displacement, productivity, and reinstatement effects** in any specific case.
3. **Be sceptical of dramatic productivity claims** without strong evidence.
4. **Focus on policy choices** that shape whether technology augments or substitutes for labour.

## Examples

**Situation:** South African policy debate about AI's impact on a labour market with high unemployment.

**Application:** The Acemoglu lens: in a labour-surplus economy, displacement-biased automation is particularly costly. The policy question is how to shape technological deployment so it augments rather than replaces labour. Concrete options: tax structures that don't favour capital over labour, public investment in skills and workplace complementary technologies, sector-specific deployment guidelines, support for SME adoption of augmenting (rather than substituting) AI. The current default — passive acceptance of technology trajectories driven by global capital — is the worst option.

**Result:** A policy framing that treats AI as a political-economic terrain, not a technological inevitability.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Predict mass unemployment confidently.
**Why:** The historical record on such predictions is poor. Past automation waves produced reinstatement effects.

**Don't:** Dismiss AI's labour-market impact as overblown.
**Why:** Recent automation has had genuine displacement effects on middle-skill workers. The wage stagnation is real.
