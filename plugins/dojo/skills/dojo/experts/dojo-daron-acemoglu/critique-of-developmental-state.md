# Critique of the Developmental State

## When to Use
- When East Asian industrial policy is being advanced as a universal template.
- When the role of authoritarian state direction in development is being defended.
- When debating the China growth model.

## Fails When
- **The critique is taken to imply no role for state capability.** Acemoglu acknowledges effective state capacity matters; the disagreement is about its sources and limits.

## Core Concept

The developmental-state thesis (Chang, Wade, Amsden, Johnson) argues that East Asian growth was driven by capable, autonomous, interventionist states using industrial policy and selective protection. Acemoglu's critique has several layers:

**The historical preconditions were unusual.** South Korea, Taiwan, and Japan emerged from specific contexts — Cold War security threats, US backing, land reforms that destroyed feudal landlord power, meritocratic bureaucracies built on prior foundations (Japan's Meiji-era institutions, Korea's Confucian bureaucratic tradition), and political-economy moments when developmental coalitions could overcome rent-seekers. These conditions are rare. Replication elsewhere has been disappointing.

**Growth occurred despite, not because of, all the interventions.** Empirically separating the effects of industrial policy from broader investment in education, openness to technology transfer, macroeconomic stability, and inclusive economic institutions (over time) is hard. The Acemoglu reading: the inclusive economic institutions (broadly available property rights for productive activity, contract enforcement, market entry) did most of the work; the industrial policy was a layer on top.

**Authoritarian extractive politics produces catch-up growth, not frontier growth.** Korea, Taiwan, Singapore all transitioned (or partially transitioned) toward more inclusive political institutions during their development. The cases where authoritarian states sustained growth without political opening (Soviet Union historically; the question is now live for China) eventually hit the wall of frontier innovation that requires inclusive institutions to enable.

**The replication record is poor.** Many countries have tried East Asian-style industrial policy under different political conditions; most have produced rent-allocation rather than productive transformation. The developmental-state model exports poorly because the institutional preconditions don't travel.

**The political economy of failure is severe.** Industrial policy under extractive political institutions becomes a vehicle for elite enrichment. The benefits flow to connected firms; the costs are borne by consumers, taxpayers, and excluded competitors. The pattern is widespread.

The disagreement with Chang and Rodrik is real but not total. Acemoglu acknowledges that markets fail, that coordination can require state action, and that some industrial policy can be productive under the right institutions. The disagreement is about whether the "right institutions" can be assumed or have to be built first.

## How to Apply

1. **When the East Asian template is invoked, ask about preconditions.** Are the institutional foundations present in the case at hand?
2. **Distinguish growth from frontier innovation.** Authoritarian states can deliver the former; sustained development requires the latter.
3. **Be honest about replication risk.** Most attempts at developmental-state-style policy have failed.
4. **Focus on institutional foundations** rather than on the policy instruments.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa debates a more aggressive industrial-policy approach modelled on East Asia.

**Application:** The Acemoglu lens: the developmental-state model requires (a) a state bureaucracy with the capability and discipline to design and implement, (b) elite coalition that backs developmental rather than extractive use of state power, (c) accountability mechanisms that close failed programmes. South Africa has the constitutional inclusive frame but the political settlement still supports extractive use of state-allocated resources. Aggressive industrial policy in this setting risks deepening the state-capture pattern. The institutional foundations need rebuilding first; selective, accountable industrial policy can be layered on once those foundations are stronger.

**Result:** A nuanced policy position — not "no industrial policy" but "industrial policy only as fast as institutional foundations can support".

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use the critique to rule out all industrial policy.
**Why:** Selective interventions under accountable institutions can be productive. The argument is about preconditions.

**Don't:** Treat East Asia as a single model.
**Why:** Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Japan, and China differ substantially in their institutional structures and political economies.
