# Economic vs Political Institutions

## When to Use
- When the question is about sequencing (markets first vs democracy first).
- When analysing why apparently good economic reforms fail.
- When designing institutional reform programmes.

## Fails When
- **The two are treated as fully separable.** They are analytically distinct but causally entangled.

## Core Concept

Acemoglu's framework distinguishes two domains of institutions:

**Economic institutions** govern the structure of incentives in production, exchange, and investment. They include property rights, contract enforcement, market structure, public-goods provision, and labour-market rules. They directly shape who has incentives to invest in physical capital, human capital, and innovation.

**Political institutions** govern the distribution and exercise of political power. They include the franchise, the structure of political competition, the constraints on the executive, the independence of the judiciary, and the rules of political participation. They shape who has the power to set, change, or capture the economic institutions.

The relationship is reciprocal:

**Political institutions determine economic institutions.** Whoever has political power chooses the economic institutions — typically choosing those that benefit themselves and the groups they represent. An extractive political settlement produces extractive economic institutions; an inclusive political settlement tends to produce more inclusive economic institutions (though not automatically).

**Economic institutions affect political power.** Concentrated economic power buys political influence, capturing the rules. Broadly distributed economic ownership and opportunity creates constituencies that defend inclusive political institutions.

This is why purely economic reform in extractive political settings tends to fail. The reform may be technically sound but the political settlement will neutralise it — either through capture (the reform's benefits flow to elites), through reversal (the reform is rolled back when elites face threat), or through subversion (the reform's implementation is degraded). Soviet glasnost-without-perestroika and perestroika-without-glasnost are illustrative.

Similarly, purely political reform (free elections) without underlying economic-institutional change tends to disappoint. Nominal democracies with extractive economic structures (much of post-colonial Africa in some periods, parts of Latin America in others) deliver poor development outcomes because the political form does not constrain the extractive economic substance.

The implication: institutional reform has to engage both domains, and the sequencing depends on the specific case.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose both domains separately.** What is the economic-institutional structure? What is the political-institutional structure?
2. **Map the reinforcement mechanisms** between them.
3. **Identify where reform is most feasible** given current political settlement.
4. **Design reforms that move both domains together** where possible, rather than relying on one to drag the other.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's post-1994 institutional design — formally inclusive political institutions with substantially unreformed economic-ownership structure.

**Application:** The combination has been unstable. Inclusive politics under concentrated economic ownership creates expectations that the political system cannot deliver. The state-capture decade represented one (extractive) response: capturing the state to extract from the still-concentrated economy. A different (inclusive) response would have been: using state power to broaden economic ownership, build smallholder capability, restructure capital markets. The political settlement has not yet supported the latter at scale.

**Result:** A diagnostic that explains why political reform without economic-ownership change has been disappointing — and clarifies the next institutional task.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Argue for either "economics first" or "politics first" as universal.
**Why:** The specific sequencing depends on the country's institutional structure and political settlement.

**Don't:** Treat the two domains as substitutes.
**Why:** They are complements. Reform of either alone is fragile.
