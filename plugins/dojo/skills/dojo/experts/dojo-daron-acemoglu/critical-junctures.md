# Critical Junctures

## When to Use
- When analysing why a country's trajectory shifted (or didn't) at a specific moment.
- When evaluating whether a current shock represents a reform opportunity.
- When designing transition strategy.

## Fails When
- **The juncture is invented post hoc.** Critical junctures are identified by the size of the disruption to the previous equilibrium and the breadth of the choice space they opened, not by any subsequent change.
- **Path-dependence is treated as deterministic.** Junctures open choice; they don't dictate outcome.

## Core Concept

A critical juncture is a moment when a major shock disrupts the existing political-economic equilibrium and opens an unusually wide range of possible institutional paths. Small differences in how actors respond at the juncture can produce vastly different long-run outcomes.

The canonical Acemoglu-Robinson examples:

**The Black Death (14th century).** Halved the population of Europe, generating an acute labour shortage. In Western Europe, peasants gained bargaining power against landlords, the feudal labour-service system weakened, and over centuries this contributed to inclusive institutional development. In Eastern Europe, landlords successfully coerced peasants back into servitude, locking in the "second serfdom" and extractive institutional development. The juncture was the same; the responses diverged because of small pre-existing institutional differences.

**The Glorious Revolution (1688).** Britain's settlement constrained the monarchy and shifted political power to Parliament, locking in inclusive political institutions that supported the subsequent industrial revolution. France took the absolutist path; the institutional consequences echoed for centuries.

**Colonial encounters.** European colonisers facing different settler conditions produced different institutions — extractive in disease-heavy tropics, more inclusive in temperate settler colonies — with persistence over centuries.

**Post-1989 transitions.** The collapse of state socialism was a juncture for all the former-communist countries. The institutional paths varied enormously (Czech Republic vs Russia, for example), producing divergent long-run outcomes.

The mechanism is path-dependence: institutional choices at the juncture lock in coalitions, expectations, and feedback loops that make subsequent reversal increasingly costly.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the juncture.** What major shock disrupted the previous equilibrium and opened the choice space?
2. **Map the actual choices made** at the juncture and their consequences.
3. **Identify the alternatives** that were available but not taken.
4. **Trace the path-dependence** through which the chosen path became self-reinforcing.
5. **For current junctures:** identify whether the country is at one, and what the choice space looks like.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa 1994 — was it a critical juncture?

**Application:** Yes. The transition disrupted the apartheid institutional equilibrium and opened a wide choice space: revolutionary economic transformation, social-democratic restructuring, or constitutional liberalism with limited economic change. The choices made — constitutional inclusion, market-oriented macroeconomics, BBBEE as the redistribution mechanism, retention of the apartheid state apparatus — locked in a particular path. The state-capture decade and current economic stagnation are partly consequences of choices not made at the juncture (deeper restructuring of capital ownership, faster state-capability rebuilding, more aggressive land reform). The current moment is not another juncture of equivalent magnitude; the choices made then constrain what's possible now.

**Result:** A historical analysis that clarifies which constraints on current policy come from juncture-era choices and which are addressable now.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat every election or change of government as a critical juncture.
**Why:** Junctures are rare and shock-driven. Most political moments are within-equilibrium adjustment.

**Don't:** Use the framework to argue that current outcomes were predetermined.
**Why:** The framework is about path-dependence, not destiny. Different choices at the juncture would have produced different paths.
