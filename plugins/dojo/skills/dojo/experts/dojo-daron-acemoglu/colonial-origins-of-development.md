# Colonial Origins of Development

## When to Use
- When the empirical evidence for the institutional theory is being discussed.
- When the role of colonial history in current underdevelopment is debated.
- When responding to alternative explanations (geography, culture).

## Fails When
- **The paper is treated as monocausal.** The AJR finding establishes institutional causality at long horizons; it does not deny that other factors matter.

## Core Concept

The "Colonial Origins of Comparative Development" paper (AJR, AER 2001) is one of the most influential causal-identification papers in development economics. The argument:

**The identification strategy.** European colonisers established different types of institutions in different colonies. In places where European mortality was high (much of sub-Saharan Africa, parts of South America), Europeans set up extractive institutions — designed to extract resources without large settler populations. In places where mortality was low (North America, Australia, southern South America), they set up settler institutions modelled on European institutions, with broader property-rights and accountability structures. These initial institutions persisted into the post-colonial era.

**The instrument.** European settler mortality at the time of colonisation provides an instrument for the type of colonial institutions established. Settler mortality is plausibly exogenous to current economic conditions (driven by disease ecology centuries ago) but predicts the institutional type that emerged. Using this instrument, the paper finds that institutional variation explains a large share of current cross-country income variation.

**The "Reversal of Fortune" follow-up.** AJR (QJE 2002) shows that the colonised regions that were most prosperous in 1500 (urbanised, dense, with sophisticated pre-colonial societies — Aztec Mexico, Inca Peru, Mughal India) became relatively poor by 1900; the colonised regions that were least prosperous in 1500 (North America, Australia, southern South America) became relatively rich. This reversal contradicts geographic theories (which predict persistence) and supports the institutional theory: where pre-colonial societies were dense and prosperous, colonisers established extractive institutions to capture the existing surplus; where they were sparse, settler institutions emerged. The reversal is the smoking gun for institutional causality.

**The persistence mechanism.** Colonial institutions persist into the post-colonial era because the elites who benefited from extractive arrangements retained power after independence; the legal-administrative apparatus persisted; the absence of broad property-rights and political voice meant the inclusive-institution constituency didn't form.

**Critics.** Glaeser et al. have argued that what colonisers brought was human capital rather than institutions; AJR have responded that human capital and institutions are jointly determined and the institutional channel is identifiable. Other critics question the settler-mortality data or the specific instrument; the broader pattern has held up to substantial scrutiny.

## How to Apply

1. **Use the framework to read long-run development trajectories** as institutionally shaped.
2. **Avoid geographic determinism.** The reversal-of-fortune evidence rules it out.
3. **Apply the persistence logic** to understand why current institutional structures resist reform.
4. **Recognise the South African distinctive case:** a settler colony that became substantially extractive through racial-political institutions, requiring institutional rebuilding from a unique starting point.

## Examples

**Situation:** Comparing South African and Australian development trajectories — both former British settler colonies.

**Application:** The AJR lens: Australia evolved toward broad-based settler institutions (broad franchise within the settler population, strong property rights, accountable state); South Africa evolved toward racially exclusive settler institutions (institutions inclusive within a small white minority, extractive toward the African majority). The institutional difference predicted the development divergence. Post-1994, South Africa formally extended inclusive institutions to the whole population but the economic structure inherited from the extractive period persists. The institutional task is rebuilding inclusion across the whole economy, not just the formal political system.

**Result:** A diagnostic that grounds the post-1994 institutional project in a clear historical-comparative analysis.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat colonial history as deterministic destiny.
**Why:** The framework is about persistence with critical junctures. Junctures (including 1994) can change paths.

**Don't:** Use the paper to absolve current actors.
**Why:** Persistence requires ongoing agency by elites who benefit. Current outcomes are partly current choices.
