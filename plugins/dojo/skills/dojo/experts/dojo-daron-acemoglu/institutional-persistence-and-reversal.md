# Institutional Persistence and Reversal

## When to Use
- When designing institutional reform.
- When explaining why reforms have not stuck.
- When evaluating whether a current moment is open to change.

## Fails When
- **Persistence is read as inevitability.** Reversals do happen; the framework identifies when they do.

## Core Concept

Institutional structures persist for systematic reasons:

**Distributional self-reinforcement.** Extractive institutions concentrate wealth and political power in elites who use them to maintain extractive institutions. The political settlement that produced the institutions is reproduced by them.

**Coalition effects.** Inclusive institutions create a broad winning coalition that defends inclusion against revisionist threats. Extractive institutions create a narrow elite coalition that defends extraction.

**Expectations and norms.** Persistent institutions become normalised. The cognitive frame within which actors operate is shaped by what they have seen. The radically different alternative becomes difficult to imagine.

**Administrative path-dependence.** Bureaucracies, legal codes, registration systems, and budget structures embody the existing institutional logic. Changing them requires substantial work and creates short-run disruption.

**Cumulative investments.** Physical infrastructure, human-capital investments, and business strategies are made in response to existing institutions. Once made, they create constituencies that defend the institutions that justified them.

The persistence is striking empirically. Slave-trade-affected regions in West Africa still show lower trust today (Nunn). Colonial-extractive institutional patterns predict current income (AJR). The post-communist Russia of 2024 resembles the Russia of 1924 institutionally more than either resembles Western Europe.

But persistence is not eternal. Three reversal mechanisms exist:

**Critical junctures.** Major exogenous shocks (wars, revolutions, plagues, technological revolutions) can disrupt the equilibrium. The Black Death, the Glorious Revolution, the French Revolution, the 1989 transitions, the Arab Spring (incompletely) — each opened reform windows.

**Slow accumulation of countervailing power.** Sometimes the seeds of change are sown gradually — emergence of new economic groups, education of new strata, growth of civil society. The accumulation eventually tips the balance.

**Elite splits.** When the extractive elite fractures (factional disputes, generational divides, ideological splits), reform coalitions can form across the fracture lines.

The reform implication: design with persistence in mind. Reforms that don't shift the underlying coalition will be reversed. Reforms that build new constituencies that benefit from the reform are self-defending.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose the persistence mechanisms** sustaining the current institutional pattern.
2. **Identify which reforms can build self-defending coalitions** vs which depend on continuous external pressure.
3. **Watch for reversal moments** — moments when the persistence mechanisms weaken.
4. **Sequence reforms to compound** — early reforms create the constituencies that demand subsequent ones.

## Examples

**Situation:** South Africa's post-1994 institutional design has been substantially eroded over the past 15 years (state capture, capability decline). Reform is being designed for the next phase.

**Application:** The Acemoglu lens: the erosion was not random — it was the outworking of a political settlement that combined inclusive constitutional form with persisting extractive economic structure and elite consolidation. Reform that addresses only the visible erosion (anti-corruption, capability restoration) without shifting the underlying settlement will be reversed. Reform that builds constituencies for inclusive economic transformation (smallholder property rights, SME ecosystem, broad asset-ownership) creates self-defending coalitions. The current moment is not a critical juncture of 1994 magnitude but the persistence mechanisms have weakened (ANC dominance reduced, civil society reactivating). The window for compounding reform is open.

**Result:** A reform strategy that takes persistence seriously and designs for coalition-building.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the persistence framework as fatalism.
**Why:** Reversals happen. The framework is about identifying when and how.

**Don't:** Treat any policy change as institutional reform.
**Why:** Real institutional reform shifts underlying coalitions; surface policy change does not.
