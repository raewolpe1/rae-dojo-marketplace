# Basic Structure

## When to Use
- When determining what institutions are subject to the principles of justice.
- When evaluating whether the overall system of institutions — not individual policies — satisfies justice.
- When designing or reforming constitutions, legal frameworks, and economic institutions.

## Fails When
- **You limit the basic structure to the state.** The basic structure includes the economic system, the family, and the system of property and contract — not just government institutions. A society with a just constitution but an unjust economic system fails the test.
- **You apply justice principles to every individual action.** The basic structure is the primary subject of justice. Within a just basic structure, individuals are free to pursue their own plans without each transaction being evaluated for justice.

## Core Concept
The basic structure of society is Rawls's name for the way the major social institutions distribute fundamental rights and duties and determine the division of advantages from social cooperation. It includes the political constitution, the legal system, the economic system (including the system of property), the family, and other institutions that together shape the life prospects of persons born into different social positions.

The basic structure is the primary subject of justice because its effects are so pervasive, present from birth, and unavoidable. No one chooses the basic structure into which they are born, yet it profoundly shapes their life prospects. A person born under one basic structure may have dramatically different life prospects than an equally talented person born under another. Because the basic structure shapes prospects so fundamentally and unchosen, it is the first thing that must be evaluated for justice.

Rawls distinguishes the basic structure from the rules governing individual conduct within it. Once the basic structure is just — once the constitutional, legal, economic, and social framework satisfies the two principles — individuals are free to act within that framework according to their own conceptions of the good. Justice as fairness does not require that every market transaction, every charitable decision, or every family arrangement be separately evaluated for justice. The framework is responsible for ensuring just background conditions; individuals are free to act within those conditions.

This has practical implications. Evaluating justice requires systemic analysis — examining how the constitution, the tax system, the education system, the labour market, the healthcare system, and the social protection system interact to produce the life prospects of persons in different social positions. Individual policies cannot be evaluated in isolation because their effects depend on the rest of the basic structure.

## How to Apply
1. **Evaluate institutions as a system, not individually.** A regressive tax may be part of a just basic structure if it is combined with progressive transfers, universal public services, and fair equality of opportunity. A progressive tax may be part of an unjust basic structure if public services are inadequate and opportunity is unequal.
2. **Identify the major institutions that constitute the basic structure in the society under analysis.** In practice: the constitution, the legal system, the tax and transfer system, the education system, the healthcare system, the labour market framework, the system of property rights, and the family.
3. **Assess the cumulative effects of these institutions on the life prospects of persons in different social positions, with particular attention to the least advantaged.**

## Examples
**Situation:** Evaluating South Africa's post-apartheid institutional framework as a basic structure.
**Application:** The Rawlsian assessment examines the system. The 1996 Constitution secures basic liberties and socio-economic rights (first principle substantially satisfied in aspiration). The education system fails fair equality of opportunity (second principle, first condition violated). The tax and transfer system is moderately progressive — South Africa's fiscal system reduces the Gini coefficient more than most developing countries — but the pre-fiscal distribution is so unequal that post-fiscal inequality remains extreme. The labour market framework (minimum wage, bargaining council extensions, employment equity) shapes opportunity and distribution but may exclude the least advantaged from employment. The system of property rights — particularly land — reflects the apartheid inheritance more than any post-1994 reform. The family, as a transmitter of advantage and disadvantage, operates within a context of extreme inequality where the social inheritance of disadvantage is profound. The Rawlsian verdict: South Africa's basic structure fails the second principle comprehensively. The cumulative effect of these institutions does not maximise the expectations of the least advantaged, primarily because the education system fails to create fair equality of opportunity and the labour market framework may exclude those most in need. Reform requires not individual policy adjustments but systemic restructuring — addressing the interconnections between education, labour markets, spatial planning, and property that together determine the life prospects of those the basic structure treats least well.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Evaluate individual policies without reference to the rest of the basic structure.
**Why:** A policy that appears unjust in isolation may be part of a just system, and vice versa. The basic structure is the unit of assessment.

**Don't:** Assume that a just basic structure eliminates all inequality.
**Why:** A just basic structure permits inequality that satisfies the difference principle. The goal is just background conditions, not equal outcomes from every individual interaction within those conditions.
