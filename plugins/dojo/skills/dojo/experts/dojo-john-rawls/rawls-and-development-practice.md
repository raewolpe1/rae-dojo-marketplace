# Rawls and Development Practice

## When to Use
- When designing development programmes with explicit justice foundations.
- When constructing M&E frameworks that assess justice rather than merely measuring outputs.
- When translating philosophical principles of justice into operational development practice.

## Fails When
- **You dismiss Rawls as too abstract for development work.** The two principles generate concrete, operationally testable questions: does this programme improve the life prospects of the least advantaged? Does it strengthen or weaken fair equality of opportunity? Does it protect or erode basic liberties?
- **You treat Rawlsian development practice as simply measuring income distribution.** The framework demands assessment across all primary goods — liberties, opportunities, powers, income/wealth, and the social bases of self-respect — and requires institutional analysis of the basic structure, not merely distributional measurement.

## Core Concept
Rawls's framework offers development practitioners something that utilitarian cost-benefit analysis and capabilities approaches do not: a principled basis for prioritising among competing claims. The lexical ordering of the two principles generates a decision procedure: basic liberties first, fair equality of opportunity second, the difference principle third. This ordering resolves dilemmas that paralyse development practice — the trade-off between economic growth and political freedom, between efficiency and equity, between immediate poverty reduction and structural transformation.

The operational translation works at three levels. At the programme design level, Rawls's framework asks: which primary goods does this programme address? Does it operate at the level of the basic structure (institutional reform) or at the level of individual transfers? Does it address the situation of the least advantaged specifically, or does it target average outcomes? At the evaluation level, the framework generates specific evaluation questions: has the programme improved the expectations of the least advantaged compared to the feasible alternative of not implementing it? Has it strengthened or weakened fair equality of opportunity? Has it respected basic liberties and their fair value? At the policy level, the framework evaluates whether the basic structure — the overall institutional arrangement — satisfies the two principles, not whether individual programmes are well-designed in isolation.

The difference principle provides a specific decision rule that development practitioners can operationalise. When choosing between programme designs, ask: which design maximises the expectations of the least advantaged? This is not the same as maximising total welfare (which might sacrifice the least advantaged for aggregate gains), maximising the number of beneficiaries (which might spread resources too thin to help anyone significantly), or targeting the median (which might ignore those at the bottom). It is a maximin criterion applied to institutional design: among feasible alternatives, choose the arrangement whose worst outcome is best.

Fair equality of opportunity generates the most operationally demanding requirements for development practice. It requires not merely formal access to services (schools exist, clinics are built) but substantive equality of life prospects for persons of similar talent and motivation regardless of birth circumstances. This means that development programmes focused on education, skills, healthcare, and early childhood development are not merely poverty-reduction tools but justice requirements — necessary components of a basic structure that satisfies fair equality of opportunity.

The relationship between Rawls and the capability approach in development practice is complementary rather than competitive. Rawlsian primary goods analysis directs attention to institutional responsibilities — what the basic structure must provide. Capability analysis directs attention to conversion factors — whether what the basic structure provides translates into genuine freedom. The best M&E practice measures both: primary goods provision (inputs and institutional arrangements) and capability outcomes (what persons are actually able to do and be).

## How to Apply
1. **When designing programmes, use lexical priority to resolve trade-offs: never sacrifice basic liberties for economic gains; never sacrifice fair equality of opportunity for distributional improvements.**
2. **When evaluating programmes, apply the difference principle as a comparative test: would the least advantaged be better served by a feasible alternative programme design?**
3. **When constructing M&E frameworks, measure across all five categories of primary goods — not just income — and assess both institutional arrangements and lived outcomes.**
4. **When assessing development strategies, evaluate the basic structure as a whole — the combined effect of institutions — not individual programmes in isolation.**

## Examples
**Situation:** A Southern African regional development programme is designing its M&E framework for a multi-country initiative covering education, health, and economic inclusion across Mozambique, Malawi, Zambia, and Zimbabwe.
**Application:** A Rawlsian M&E framework would structure evaluation around the two principles in lexical order. First, assess basic liberties: does programme implementation respect political freedoms, physical security, and rule of law? In Zimbabwe, this question is prior to all others — if the basic structure denies political liberties, programme-level improvements in education or health cannot constitute progress toward justice. Second, assess fair equality of opportunity: disaggregate all outcome indicators by birth circumstances (location, parental income, gender, disability) to determine whether the programme is reducing or reproducing inequality of life prospects. In Mozambique, this means tracking whether education quality improvements reach Cabo Delgado and Nampula provinces (where conflict and poverty constrain opportunity most severely) as effectively as Maputo. In Malawi, it means assessing whether health interventions reach rural communities facing the most severe conversion factor barriers. Third, apply the difference principle: are programme resources allocated to maximise the expectations of the least advantaged? This generates a specific testable hypothesis — that the programme design chosen produces better outcomes for the worst-off than feasible alternatives. The framework would include counterfactual analysis (what would happen without the programme), disaggregated impact measurement (effects on the least advantaged specifically, not just average effects), and institutional assessment (whether the programme strengthens the basic structure's capacity to deliver justice over time, or creates parallel systems that undermine institutional development). The critical Rawlsian addition to standard M&E practice is the insistence on lexical ordering: a programme that improves average incomes while undermining democratic accountability (as some budget support programmes have been accused of doing) fails the Rawlsian test regardless of its distributional achievements.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce Rawlsian development practice to targeting the poorest quintile.
**Why:** The difference principle is an institutional criterion, not a targeting criterion. It asks whether the basic structure — the overall arrangement of institutions — maximises the expectations of the least advantaged, not whether individual transfers reach the poorest. A society can target transfers precisely while maintaining an unjust basic structure.

**Don't:** Use Rawls to argue against economic growth on distributional grounds.
**Why:** The difference principle explicitly permits inequality — and potentially substantial inequality — when it benefits the least advantaged. Economic growth that improves the life prospects of the worst-off satisfies the difference principle even if it increases inequality. The test is comparative: would an alternative arrangement produce better outcomes for the least advantaged?
