# Rawls Applied to Southern Africa

## When to Use
- When evaluating South African or Southern African institutions against the two principles of justice.
- When assessing whether post-apartheid transformation has created a just basic structure.
- When designing development interventions that take justice seriously as a foundational commitment.

## Fails When
- **You apply Rawls mechanically as a reform checklist.** Rawls offers a framework for evaluating the basic structure as a whole, not a scoring rubric for individual policies. The question is whether the overall institutional arrangement satisfies the two principles in their lexical order.
- **You ignore the gap between ideal and non-ideal theory.** Rawls's principles describe a well-ordered society. Southern African societies are transitional — emerging from profound historical injustice. Applying Rawls requires non-ideal theory: how should institutions move toward justice from deeply unjust starting points?

## Core Concept
South Africa provides one of the most illuminating cases for Rawlsian analysis because its constitutional transition explicitly engaged questions of justice, fairness, and institutional design that parallel Rawls's theoretical concerns. The 1996 Constitution embeds commitments to equality, dignity, and socio-economic rights that resonate with the two principles. Yet the institutional reality falls far short of justice as fairness — making South Africa a test case for the distance between constitutional commitment and institutional achievement.

Applying the two principles in lexical order reveals the structure of South Africa's justice deficit. The first principle — equal basic liberties with fair value of political liberties — is formally satisfied through the Bill of Rights and democratic institutions. But the fair value of political liberties is undermined by economic concentration that translates into political influence, by the dominance of a single party for three decades that weakened accountability mechanisms, and by state capture episodes that corrupted the institutional framework. The prior principle of basic liberties is compromised when gender-based violence reaches epidemic levels, when xenophobic violence threatens the physical security of non-nationals, and when informal settlement residents lack basic security of person and property.

Fair equality of opportunity — the second principle's first component — is the most conspicuously violated. The education system reproduces apartheid-era inequality: former Model C schools and private institutions produce outcomes comparable to OECD countries while township and rural schools produce functional illiteracy and innumeracy. A child born in Sandton and a child born in Lusikisiki face radically different life prospects determined not by talent or effort but by birth circumstances. Healthcare access, spatial planning inherited from apartheid, transport costs that consume a quarter of poor households' income, and the digital divide compound educational inequality to produce a basic structure that systematically fails fair equality of opportunity.

The difference principle — the second principle's second component — demands that the economic system maximise the expectations of the least advantaged. South Africa's economy, with unemployment exceeding 30 percent on the narrow definition and income concentration among the highest globally, does not satisfy this test. The question is not whether some inequality is acceptable — the difference principle permits substantial inequality if it benefits the least advantaged — but whether this institutional arrangement is the feasible alternative that best serves those at the bottom. Given that millions are excluded from productive economic participation entirely, the answer is clearly no.

The broader Southern African region presents variations on these themes. Botswana's diamond-funded development achieved relative political stability and economic growth but faces questions about whether mineral wealth has been dispersed broadly enough to satisfy the difference principle or whether it has created a narrow urban middle class alongside persistent rural poverty. Zimbabwe's institutional collapse under authoritarian rule represents a fundamental violation of basic liberties that must be remedied before higher-order justice questions can be meaningfully addressed. Mozambique's post-conflict reconstruction and recent resource discoveries raise questions about whether emerging institutions can prevent the concentration of mineral wealth in ways that violate both fair equality of opportunity and the difference principle.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess institutions in lexical order: first basic liberties and their fair value, then fair equality of opportunity, then the difference principle.** Do not jump to economic distribution before establishing whether prior principles are satisfied.
2. **Evaluate the basic structure as a whole — the combined effect of constitutional provisions, educational institutions, economic structures, social policies, and legal frameworks — not individual programmes in isolation.**
3. **For non-ideal conditions, ask the transitional justice question: what institutional reforms move the society closest to satisfying the two principles from its current unjust starting point?**

## Examples
**Situation:** A development agency is designing a five-year country programme for South Africa and wants to ground it in a coherent justice framework rather than ad hoc project selection.
**Application:** Rawls provides the systematic framework. Begin with the lexical ordering. First priority: basic liberties. This means programmes addressing gender-based violence (physical security as a basic liberty), strengthening democratic accountability institutions (Chapter 9 institutions, independent judiciary, media freedom), and ensuring that political liberties have fair value (campaign finance reform, anti-corruption measures, citizen participation mechanisms). Second priority: fair equality of opportunity. This is where the largest investment is justified given South Africa's institutional profile — early childhood development (the period when inequality in human capital formation is most consequential), basic education quality (the institutional failure that most systematically violates FEO), skills development that creates genuine access to productive employment, and spatial transformation that reduces the transport and infrastructure barriers inherited from apartheid. Third priority: economic arrangements satisfying the difference principle — supporting broad-based asset ownership (not narrow BEE), labour market reforms that include the excluded rather than merely protecting the employed, and industrial strategy that creates employment accessible to the least advantaged. The critical Rawlsian discipline is the lexical ordering: do not fund economic growth programmes that ignore basic liberties violations, and do not prioritise income distribution without addressing the structural failures of fair equality of opportunity that make that distribution unjust in the first place.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the 1996 Constitution as sufficient evidence that South Africa satisfies Rawlsian justice.
**Why:** Constitutional commitments are necessary but not sufficient. Rawls evaluates the basic structure — the actual functioning of institutions — not the written commitments. South Africa's Constitution is among the world's most progressive; its institutional reality includes some of the world's deepest inequality, educational failure, and economic exclusion.

**Don't:** Apply Rawls only to government policy while ignoring the economic structure.
**Why:** The basic structure includes the economic system — patterns of ownership, market structures, the organisation of production. Rawls's preference for property-owning democracy over welfare-state capitalism means that evaluating South Africa requires assessing not just social policy but the ownership structure, corporate concentration, and the distribution of productive assets.
