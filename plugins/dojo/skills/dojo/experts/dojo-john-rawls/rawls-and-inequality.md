# Rawls and Inequality

## When to Use
- When assessing whether inequality in a specific context is just.
- When evaluating redistributive policies through a Rawlsian lens.
- When distinguishing between Rawlsian, utilitarian, and libertarian approaches to inequality.

## Fails When
- **You present Rawls as egalitarian in the simple sense of opposing all inequality.** The difference principle explicitly permits inequality — and potentially substantial inequality — when it benefits the least advantaged. Rawls is not an egalitarian; he is a theorist of justified inequality.
- **You treat inequality as a single dimension.** Rawls's framework addresses multiple dimensions: basic liberties (which must be equal), opportunity (which must be fair), and economic distribution (which must satisfy the difference principle). Inequality in one dimension may coexist with equality in another.

## Core Concept
Rawls's treatment of inequality is sophisticated and often misunderstood. He does not oppose inequality per se. He opposes inequality that cannot be justified to those who bear its costs — the least advantaged. The difference principle provides the test: inequality is just when the institutional arrangement that produces it maximises the expectations of the least advantaged compared to all feasible alternatives, including equality.

This permits significant inequality. If a market economy with differential rewards produces higher living standards for the least advantaged than an egalitarian economy (because incentives generate production, innovation, and tax revenue that fund public services), the inequality is just. But it imposes a demanding test: at every point, the question is whether the least advantaged would be better served by a less unequal arrangement.

Rawls distinguishes his position from three alternatives. Against utilitarianism, he insists that inequality cannot be justified by aggregate welfare gains — the improved position of some cannot compensate for the diminished position of others. Against libertarianism, he insists that market outcomes are just only within a just basic structure — property rights and contractual freedom do not generate just outcomes in the absence of fair background conditions. Against luck egalitarianism, he argues that the basic structure, not individual fortune, is the primary subject of justice.

The implications for policy are institutional rather than transactional. A Rawlsian approach does not regulate individual incomes or wages directly but designs the basic structure — tax policy, education, healthcare, inheritance, property — so that the overall system satisfies the difference principle. This may be compatible with significant market freedom in individual transactions, provided the basic structure ensures that market outcomes benefit the least advantaged.

## How to Apply
1. **When assessing inequality, ask the difference principle question: would the least advantaged be better off under a feasible alternative arrangement?**
2. **Distinguish between inequality that reflects genuine productive contributions and inequality that reflects rent-seeking, market power, or inherited privilege.**
3. **Assess inequality across all dimensions of primary goods — not just income — including liberties, opportunities, and the social bases of self-respect.**

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa has one of the highest Gini coefficients in the world (approximately 0.63), with extreme concentration of income and wealth alongside mass poverty and unemployment.
**Application:** Rawls would ask: does this level of inequality satisfy the difference principle? Is the basic structure that produces it the arrangement that best serves the least advantaged? The answer in South Africa is almost certainly no. The inequality is not primarily the result of incentive structures that generate production benefiting the least advantaged. It is substantially the result of apartheid-era wealth concentration, monopolistic market structures, an education system that fails to create fair equality of opportunity, a labour market that excludes a third of the working-age population, and an economic structure that generates returns primarily for capital owners and skilled workers. The difference principle demands structural reform: progressive taxation and wealth dispersion; educational transformation that creates genuine equality of opportunity; labour market reforms that include the excluded; land reform that addresses the property concentration inherited from apartheid; and industrial strategy that creates employment accessible to the least advantaged. The critical Rawlsian insight is that the question is not whether South Africa should tolerate some inequality — it should, if that inequality generates the production and services the least advantaged need — but whether this level and structure of inequality is the arrangement that best serves the least advantaged. Given the depth of poverty alongside extreme wealth, the answer is clearly no.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use the difference principle to justify any inequality by asserting trickle-down effects.
**Why:** The difference principle requires demonstration, not assertion. If inequality is not actually generating improved prospects for the least advantaged, it is unjust regardless of theoretical claims about incentive effects.

**Don't:** Apply the difference principle to individual transactions.
**Why:** The difference principle applies to the basic structure. Individual salaries, profits, and wealth accumulations are evaluated by whether the system that produces them satisfies the difference principle, not by whether each transaction independently benefits the least advantaged.
