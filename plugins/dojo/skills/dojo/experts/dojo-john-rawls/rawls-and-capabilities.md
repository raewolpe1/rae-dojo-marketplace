# Rawls and Capabilities

## When to Use
- When comparing Rawlsian primary goods and Sen's capabilities as metrics for justice.
- When evaluating whether M&E frameworks should measure resources or functionings.
- When navigating the theoretical debate between Rawls and Sen in development contexts.

## Fails When
- **You present Rawls and Sen as simply opposed.** The debate is more nuanced: both reject utilitarianism, both focus on what persons can do and be rather than on subjective satisfaction, and both are concerned with the life prospects of the least advantaged. The disagreement is about the metric of justice, not its fundamental orientation.
- **You accept Sen's critique without engaging Rawls's response.** Rawls argued that primary goods, properly understood, account for the concerns Sen raises, because they are defined in relation to the two moral powers — the capacities that define persons as free and equal.

## Core Concept
Sen's capability critique of Rawls argues that primary goods are an inadequate metric because they measure the means of wellbeing rather than persons' actual ability to convert those means into valuable functionings. A wheelchair user and an able-bodied person may hold identical bundles of primary goods but have very different capabilities. Sen argues that justice should be assessed by what persons are able to do and be (capabilities and functionings), not by the resources they hold.

Rawls's response, developed in Political Liberalism and Justice as Fairness: A Restatement, takes several forms. First, he argues that the primary goods approach already accounts for variation in needs through the distinction between citizens and persons: justice as fairness addresses the needs of citizens — persons with the two moral powers — and provides for special needs (disability, illness) through a prior stage of the basic structure that ensures healthcare and support. Second, he argues that the capability approach is either equivalent to primary goods (if capabilities are defined in terms of the conditions that enable the exercise of the moral powers) or too dependent on controversial judgments about which capabilities matter (if the list of capabilities reflects a particular comprehensive doctrine rather than a political conception).

The practical relevance of the debate for development practitioners is that both approaches are useful at different stages of analysis. Primary goods are more readily measurable and less dependent on contested value judgments — income, wealth, educational attainment, legal rights can be measured without specifying what constitutes a good life. Capabilities direct attention to the actual functioning of persons and to the conversion factors (disability, geography, gender, age) that determine how resources translate into lived experience.

## How to Apply
1. **Use primary goods as the metric for institutional assessment and capabilities as a diagnostic for identifying where institutions fail to convert resources into genuine freedom.**
2. **When designing M&E frameworks, measure both: primary goods (resources distributed) and capability indicators (what persons are actually able to do and be).**
3. **When the two approaches diverge — when resources are adequate but capabilities are constrained — investigate the conversion factors that explain the gap.**

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's social grants provide income transfers (a primary good) but the capability to convert income into wellbeing varies dramatically by location — a grant recipient in an urban area with access to transport, healthcare facilities, and markets achieves different functionings than a grant recipient in a remote rural area with no transport, distant clinics, and limited market access.
**Application:** This is precisely the type of case that illuminates the Rawls-Sen debate. A pure primary goods assessment might judge the grant system adequate: income is transferred, the primary good is provided. A capabilities assessment would disaggregate by conversion factors: does the grant recipient have the capability to access nutrition (requiring markets and transport), healthcare (requiring facilities and transport), education for children (requiring schools and transport), and economic participation (requiring infrastructure and opportunity)? The Rawlsian response would be that these conversion factors are themselves failures of the basic structure — inadequate transport infrastructure, healthcare facilities, and market access are failures of primary goods provision, not limitations of the primary goods framework. The practical resolution: measure both. Track income transfers (primary goods) and track what persons can actually do with those transfers (capabilities). Where the two diverge, investigate the institutional failures that explain the gap and address them as failures of the basic structure.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Choose one framework dogmatically and dismiss the other.
**Why:** Both approaches illuminate justice from complementary angles. Primary goods focus attention on institutional responsibilities; capabilities focus attention on lived experience. The best development practice uses both.

**Don't:** Assume that measuring capabilities resolves all measurement problems.
**Why:** The capability approach raises its own measurement challenges: which capabilities matter? How are they weighed against each other? Who decides? These questions are as difficult as the questions the primary goods approach faces.
