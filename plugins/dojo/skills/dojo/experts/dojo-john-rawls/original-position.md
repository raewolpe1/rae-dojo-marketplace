# Original Position

## When to Use
- When explaining the philosophical device Rawls uses to derive principles of justice.
- When testing whether a proposed institutional arrangement could be justified from an impartial perspective.
- When designing decision procedures that model fairness.

## Fails When
- **You present it as a description of how societies are actually formed.** The original position is a thought experiment — a device of representation — not a historical claim. No actual contract is posited.
- **You assume the parties behind the veil are altruistic.** The parties are modelled as rationally self-interested but mutually disinterested: each cares about their own prospects but is indifferent to the prospects of others. The moral force comes from the constraints of the veil, not from the character of the choosers.

## Core Concept
The original position is Rawls's device for modelling the conditions under which principles of justice can be chosen fairly. The key element is the veil of ignorance, which deprives the choosing parties of information that would allow them to bias principles in their own favour. Behind the veil, parties do not know their race, sex, natural talents, social class, wealth, comprehensive doctrine, or conception of the good. They know only general facts about human psychology, economics, and social organisation.

Under these conditions, Rawls argues, rational parties would choose his two principles of justice rather than utilitarian, libertarian, or perfectionist alternatives. The argument proceeds through the maximin reasoning strategy: because parties cannot assess probabilities and the stakes involve their fundamental life prospects, they would choose principles that maximise the minimum position — ensuring that even the worst possible outcome is as good as possible.

The original position is not a one-time choice but a test that can be applied to any institutional arrangement. The question is always: could this arrangement be justified to persons who did not know which position they would occupy within it? If an arrangement cannot survive this test — if it can only be justified by those who know they benefit from it — it fails the fairness criterion.

The veil of ignorance models a moral intuition that most people share: that accidents of birth — being born wealthy or poor, talented or ordinary, into one racial group or another — should not determine one's life prospects. The veil formalises this intuition into a procedural device that generates principles.

## How to Apply
1. **When evaluating an institutional proposal, ask: could this be chosen by persons who did not know which position they would occupy?** Would someone accept this education system not knowing whether they would attend a well-resourced school or a failing one?
2. **Use the original position to expose self-serving justifications.** Arguments that inequality is "natural," "meritocratic," or "efficient" must be tested against the veil: would the argument be accepted by someone who might be on the losing end?
3. **Apply the veil to stakeholder consultation processes.** Design deliberative processes that approximate the impartiality of the veil by ensuring that all affected parties — including those with least power — have effective voice.

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's spatial planning legacy — in which apartheid-era Group Areas legislation concentrated black populations in distant townships and rural homelands — continues to structure access to opportunity, with residents of former townships facing long commutes, poor infrastructure, and limited access to quality services.
**Application:** The original position test exposes the injustice transparently. Would any rational person, not knowing whether they would be born in Sandton or Khayelitsha, choose the current spatial arrangement? The answer is obviously no — the arrangement can only be defended by those who know they occupy the advantaged positions. This does not mean the original position prescribes a specific spatial policy, but it establishes that the current arrangement fails the basic test of justice and that reforms — in transport, in housing, in the location of economic activity and public services — are required. The original position also clarifies the standard for reform: not merely improving conditions in disadvantaged areas (which might satisfy a utilitarian calculus if the improvement is cost-effective) but ensuring that spatial location does not determine life prospects — that the basic structure does not assign advantage and disadvantage based on where one happens to be born.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use the original position to justify any specific policy directly.
**Why:** The original position generates principles for the basic structure, not specific policies. Many different policies could satisfy the principles. The original position narrows the range of just arrangements but does not determine a unique policy.

**Don't:** Dismiss the original position as unrealistic because no actual veil exists.
**Why:** The thought experiment is a tool for moral reasoning, not a description of reality. Its value lies in modelling impartiality, just as economic models of perfect competition model efficiency without claiming that perfect competition exists.
