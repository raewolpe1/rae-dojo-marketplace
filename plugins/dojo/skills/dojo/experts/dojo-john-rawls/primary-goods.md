# Primary Goods

## When to Use
- When specifying what just institutions should distribute or ensure.
- When designing indicators for measuring whether institutional arrangements satisfy justice.
- When comparing Rawlsian, utilitarian, and capability approaches to distributive justice.

## Fails When
- **You treat primary goods as a comprehensive theory of wellbeing.** Primary goods are all-purpose means — things rational persons want whatever their plan of life. They are not a theory of what makes life good; they are a theory of what institutions owe persons to pursue their own conceptions of the good.
- **You ignore the social bases of self-respect.** Rawls regards self-respect as "perhaps the most important primary good." Institutional arrangements that systematically humiliate, stigmatise, or subordinate groups fail on this dimension even if income and wealth are adequate.

## Core Concept
Primary goods are the things that rational persons want whatever else they want — the all-purpose means necessary for pursuing any conception of the good life. Rawls identifies five categories: basic liberties (freedom of thought, conscience, association, bodily integrity, political participation); freedom of movement and free choice of occupation against a background of diverse opportunities; powers and prerogatives of offices and positions of authority and responsibility; income and wealth; and the social bases of self-respect.

The list is constructed from a political standpoint: these are the goods that citizens need in their capacity as free and equal persons cooperating under fair terms, not the goods that any particular comprehensive doctrine identifies as valuable. This allows justice as fairness to remain neutral among reasonable conceptions of the good while specifying what institutions owe to all citizens.

The index of primary goods serves as the metric for the difference principle. The least advantaged are identified by their position in the distribution of primary goods, and institutional arrangements are compared by their effects on this group's expectations of primary goods. Income and wealth serve as a practical proxy for the full index in most applications, but the other dimensions — particularly the social bases of self-respect — are not reducible to income.

The primary goods approach is intermediate between welfarism (which measures subjective satisfaction and faces interpersonal comparison problems) and resourcism (which measures material holdings without regard to persons' capacity to use them). Primary goods are objective — they can be measured without reference to subjective preferences — but they are connected to persons' capacity to pursue their plans through the link between primary goods and the two moral powers (the capacity for a sense of justice and the capacity for a conception of the good).

## How to Apply
1. **When designing M&E frameworks or indicators of institutional justice, measure across all five dimensions of primary goods — not just income and wealth.**
2. **Pay particular attention to the social bases of self-respect.** Institutional arrangements that humiliate, stigmatise, or create systematic status hierarchies fail on this dimension.
3. **Use primary goods as the metric for comparing institutional alternatives.** Which arrangement gives the least advantaged the highest expectations across the full range of primary goods?

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's social grant system (Child Support Grant, Old Age Pension, Disability Grant) reaches approximately 18 million beneficiaries and represents the largest social protection programme in Africa.
**Application:** Rawlsian primary goods analysis evaluates the grant system across all five dimensions. Income and wealth: the grants address this dimension directly, transferring income to the least advantaged. The CSG (currently approximately R510 per month per child) provides a floor below which children's income support does not fall. But the amount is modest relative to the cost of a minimally adequate life. Freedom and opportunity: do the grants enhance or constrain recipients' freedom of movement and occupational choice? Evidence suggests that grants function as a platform that enables job search, migration to areas of opportunity, and investment in children's education — expanding rather than constraining freedom. The social bases of self-respect: this is where the analysis becomes most searching. Is the grant system administered in ways that respect recipients' dignity? Are means-testing processes humiliating? Does the system create stigma? Does it reinforce or challenge the status hierarchies that apartheid created? The physical conditions at some SASSA pay points, the periodic disruption of payments, and the political instrumentalisation of grants all raise concerns about whether the system adequately protects the social bases of self-respect. A Rawlsian assessment would recommend: increase grant values to levels that meaningfully support the full range of primary goods; simplify administration to reduce indignity; and design the system as a right (which it constitutionally is) rather than as discretionary welfare.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that income transfers alone satisfy the requirements of justice.
**Why:** Primary goods include liberties, opportunities, powers, and self-respect — not just income. A society that provides adequate income transfers but denies equal basic liberties, restricts occupational choice, or systematically undermines the self-respect of disadvantaged groups fails the primary goods test.

**Don't:** Dismiss primary goods as too abstract to operationalise.
**Why:** While the full index poses measurement challenges, each dimension can be operationalised for specific institutional assessments. Education quality, healthcare access, political participation rates, occupational mobility, and subjective dignity indicators all approximate dimensions of primary goods.
