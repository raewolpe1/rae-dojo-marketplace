# Justice as Fairness

## When to Use
- When introducing or explaining the overall Rawlsian framework.
- When evaluating whether institutional arrangements satisfy the requirements of justice.
- When providing the theoretical foundation for more specific analyses of liberty, opportunity, or distribution.

## Fails When
- **You treat justice as fairness as utilitarianism with extra constraints.** Rawls's theory is fundamentally anti-utilitarian: it rejects the idea that the good of some can compensate for the deprivation of others. The least advantaged cannot be sacrificed for aggregate welfare.
- **You apply the principles directly to individual transactions rather than to the basic structure.** Justice as fairness evaluates the system of institutions, not particular market exchanges, salary negotiations, or charitable decisions.

## Core Concept
Justice as fairness is the name Rawls gives to his complete theory, built on the idea that the principles governing the basic structure of society should be those that free and equal persons would agree to under fair conditions of choice. The theory produces two principles, ordered lexically.

The first principle: each person has an equal claim to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic liberties, which scheme is compatible with the same scheme for all. This principle has strict priority — basic liberties cannot be traded for economic advantages.

The second principle: social and economic inequalities are to satisfy two conditions. First, they must be attached to offices and positions open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity. Second, they must be to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged members of society (the difference principle).

The lexical ordering means that the first principle must be fully satisfied before the second applies, and within the second principle, fair equality of opportunity takes priority over the difference principle. This ordering reflects Rawls's conviction that certain goods — the basic liberties, genuine opportunity — are not commensurable with material wealth and cannot be traded against it.

The theory is procedural and institutional. It does not prescribe specific distributions of income or wealth. It specifies the principles that the basic structure — the constitution, the legal framework, the economic system — must satisfy. A society is just when its basic structure satisfies these principles, even if the resulting distribution of income and wealth is unequal.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the institutional arrangement under evaluation.** Justice as fairness applies to the basic structure — constitutions, legal systems, economic institutions, education systems — not to individual choices within those institutions.
2. **Check the first principle: are basic liberties equal and adequate?** If not, no other consideration is relevant until basic liberties are secured.
3. **Check fair equality of opportunity: do persons with similar talents and motivation have similar life prospects regardless of social origin?**
4. **Apply the difference principle: does the arrangement maximise the expectations of the least advantaged compared to feasible alternatives?**

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's Constitution (1996) establishes an extensive Bill of Rights including socio-economic rights (housing, healthcare, food, water, social security, education) alongside civil and political liberties.
**Application:** From a Rawlsian perspective, the South African Constitution is remarkably aligned with justice as fairness in its aspirational structure. The Bill of Rights secures basic liberties (first principle). The socio-economic rights provisions and the equality clause aim toward fair equality of opportunity (second principle, first part). The constitutional commitment to "improve the quality of life of all citizens and free the potential of each person" resonates with the difference principle's concern for the least advantaged. However, justice as fairness evaluates not constitutional text but institutional reality. The test is whether the basic structure — as it actually operates — satisfies these principles. The persistence of extreme inequality, the failure of the education system to provide fair equality of opportunity, the inadequacy of public healthcare, and the erosion of state capacity in many domains suggest that South Africa's basic structure falls far short of justice as fairness despite a constitutional text that embodies many of its aspirations. The gap between constitutional aspiration and institutional reality is itself a central concern for Rawlsian analysis.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat justice as fairness as demanding perfect equality.
**Why:** The difference principle explicitly permits inequality — potentially substantial inequality — provided it benefits the least advantaged. Rawls is not an egalitarian in the sense of demanding equal outcomes.

**Don't:** Apply the theory only to government action.
**Why:** The basic structure includes economic institutions, the family, and the system of property rights — not just the state. A Rawlsian analysis must assess the entire institutional framework, including market structures and social institutions.
