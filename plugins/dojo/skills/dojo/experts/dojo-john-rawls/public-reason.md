# Public Reason

## When to Use
- When evaluating whether policy justifications meet the standard of democratic legitimacy.
- When designing deliberative processes that respect diverse worldviews.
- When navigating the role of religious and cultural arguments in public policy.

## Fails When
- **You apply the public reason requirement to all public discourse.** Public reason constrains official political decisions — legislation, constitutional interpretation, judicial rulings — not the full range of public discourse. Citizens, churches, and civil society organisations may freely express comprehensive doctrines in public; the constraint applies to the justification of coercive political power.
- **You treat public reason as requiring moral neutrality.** Public reason permits substantive moral argument — about equality, dignity, fairness, the common good — provided the argument is expressible in terms accessible to all reasonable citizens, not only to adherents of a particular doctrine.

## Core Concept
Public reason is Rawls's doctrine governing the kinds of reasons that may legitimately justify the exercise of coercive political power in a pluralistic society. The basic idea is that when citizens impose laws backed by state coercion, they owe each other justifications that all reasonable citizens can accept — not justifications that depend on accepting a particular religious, philosophical, or moral comprehensive doctrine.

This does not mean that citizens cannot have religious or philosophical reasons for their political positions. It means that when offering reasons in support of legislation, judicial decisions, or constitutional provisions, they must be able to provide a sufficient justification in public reason — in terms of political values (liberty, equality, fairness, the common good, public health and safety) that all reasonable citizens share.

Rawls later introduced the "wide view" of public reason, which permits citizens to introduce their comprehensive doctrines into public political debate provided they also offer a public reason justification for their position in due course. This accommodates the reality that many citizens' political convictions are deeply rooted in their comprehensive doctrines while maintaining the requirement that coercive law be justifiable to all.

The public reason requirement is a standard of legitimacy, not a restriction on speech. No one is prohibited from speaking; the doctrine addresses the kinds of reasons that can legitimate the use of state power. Laws justified solely by reasons drawn from a particular comprehensive doctrine lack democratic legitimacy even if they are enacted by majority vote, because they impose on citizens coercive requirements that those citizens cannot accept from within their own reasonable comprehensive doctrines.

## How to Apply
1. **When evaluating policy proposals, ask: is the justification expressible in terms that all reasonable citizens can accept, regardless of their comprehensive doctrine?**
2. **In deliberative processes, encourage participants to translate their comprehensive reasons into public reasons.** This is not suppression but translation — making arguments accessible to fellow citizens who do not share the comprehensive framework.
3. **Distinguish between the right to express comprehensive doctrines in public (which is protected by basic liberties) and the requirement that coercive law be justified by public reason.**

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's debate over traditional courts legislation, which proposed giving traditional leaders judicial authority in rural communities with limited appeal rights, raises questions about the justification of legal authority in a pluralistic society.
**Application:** Public reason analysis asks: can the authority of traditional courts be justified in terms that all reasonable South African citizens can accept? Arguments grounded solely in the intrinsic authority of traditional institutions or customary law may persuade those who share a particular cultural framework but cannot legitimate the exercise of coercive judicial power over citizens who hold different comprehensive doctrines — including women and younger community members who may not endorse patriarchal authority structures. A public reason justification for traditional courts would need to demonstrate that they provide accessible dispute resolution, protect the basic rights of all parties (including the right to opt out and access formal courts), and operate within the constitutional framework of equal basic liberties. Aspects of the proposal that could not be justified in these terms — such as restrictions on women's participation, limited appeal rights, or mandatory jurisdiction over persons who do not consent — would fail the public reason test regardless of their cultural legitimacy within a particular comprehensive doctrine. Rawls's framework does not reject traditional justice systems outright but insists that their coercive authority must be justifiable to all who are subject to it.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use public reason to exclude religious citizens from democratic participation.
**Why:** The public reason requirement applies to the justification of coercive law, not to citizens' participation in public debate. Religious citizens have the same right to participate, advocate, and organise as secular citizens. The requirement is that laws ultimately be justified in public terms.

**Don't:** Assume that secular arguments are automatically public reasons.
**Why:** Some secular comprehensive doctrines (comprehensive utilitarianism, comprehensive libertarianism) are as sectarian as religious doctrines. Public reason draws on political values that can be endorsed from multiple comprehensive perspectives, whether religious or secular.
