# Fair Equality of Opportunity

## When to Use
- When evaluating whether opportunity is genuinely equal, not merely formally non-discriminatory.
- When assessing education, health, and social policy by their effects on life chances.
- When designing institutions to ensure that talent and motivation, not social origin, determine life prospects.

## Fails When
- **You accept formal non-discrimination as sufficient.** Fair equality of opportunity is far more demanding than removing legal barriers. It requires that persons with similar talents and motivation have similar prospects regardless of social class of origin. This demands investment in education, nutrition, healthcare, and the social conditions that develop talent.
- **You ignore the family as a source of inequality.** The family transmits advantage and disadvantage across generations through wealth, social capital, educational support, and cultural resources. Fair equality of opportunity requires mitigating — though not eliminating — the influence of family background on life prospects.

## Core Concept
Fair equality of opportunity (FEO) occupies the middle position in Rawls's lexical ordering: it has priority over the difference principle but is subordinate to equal basic liberties. FEO requires that offices and positions are open to all and that persons with similar native talents and similar willingness to develop and use them have similar life prospects regardless of their initial social position.

This is far more demanding than careers open to talents (formal equality of opportunity), which merely requires the absence of legal barriers. FEO requires positive institutional action to ensure that social class does not determine the development and exercise of talent. A society in which the children of wealthy families receive excellent education while the children of poor families receive inadequate education fails FEO even if no law prohibits poor children from applying to elite institutions.

The implications are substantial. FEO requires: education systems that develop talent regardless of family background; healthcare systems that ensure the physical development of all children; nutrition and family support programmes that address early childhood disadvantage; and social institutions that prevent extreme inheritance of advantage. Rawls recognised that fully achieving FEO is enormously difficult — it requires mitigating the influence of the family on life prospects without abolishing the family — but insisted that it is a requirement of justice, not merely a policy aspiration.

FEO has priority over the difference principle. This means that institutional arrangements that maximise the expectations of the least advantaged at the cost of fair equality of opportunity are unjust. A society cannot justly compensate the least advantaged through income transfers while denying them genuine opportunity to develop their talents and compete for offices and positions.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess opportunity by outcomes, not by formal access.** If children from disadvantaged backgrounds consistently achieve less than equally talented children from advantaged backgrounds, the system fails FEO.
2. **Evaluate education systems by their capacity to develop talent regardless of social origin.** School quality, teacher allocation, curricular resources, and extracurricular opportunities must not be determined by family wealth.
3. **Address early childhood as the critical period.** The influence of family background on life prospects is established primarily in early childhood. FEO requires substantial public investment in early childhood development, nutrition, and family support.

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's education system remains profoundly unequal, with school quality strongly correlated with the socioeconomic status of the community the school serves, perpetuating apartheid-era patterns of advantage and disadvantage.
**Application:** South Africa's education system is perhaps the clearest violation of fair equality of opportunity in the contemporary world. A child born in a former Model C suburb attends a school with qualified teachers, adequate resources, small classes, and extracurricular enrichment. A child born with identical native talent in a rural former homeland or urban township attends a school where teacher subject knowledge is often inadequate, textbooks arrive late or not at all, infrastructure is decaying, and the language of instruction may be poorly mastered by both teachers and students. The predictable result: matric pass rates, university entrance qualification, and subsequent life prospects are overwhelmingly determined by where one is born, not by one's talents or effort. Rawls would identify this as a fundamental failure of the basic structure. The remedy is not merely increased education spending (though that is necessary) but structural reform: ensure that the most effective teachers work in the schools with the greatest need; equalise physical and curricular resources; invest massively in foundation phase literacy and numeracy; and address the non-school factors (nutrition, safety, family support, transport) that undermine learning in disadvantaged communities. Until South Africa's education system gives a talented child in Cofimvaba prospects comparable to a talented child in Constantia, the basic structure fails fair equality of opportunity.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat meritocracy as inherently just.
**Why:** Meritocracy rewards developed talent, but developed talent reflects social advantage as much as natural ability. A meritocracy built on an unfair educational foundation rewards the already advantaged and calls it merit.

**Don't:** Assume that fair equality of opportunity requires identical outcomes.
**Why:** FEO permits differential outcomes based on talent and effort. What it prohibits is differential outcomes based on social origin among persons with similar talent and effort.
