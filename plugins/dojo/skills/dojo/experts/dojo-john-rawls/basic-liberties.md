# Basic Liberties

## When to Use
- When determining which liberties take priority over economic distribution.
- When evaluating whether restrictions on freedom are justified.
- When assessing whether liberties exist in substance, not merely in form.

## Fails When
- **You include unlimited economic liberties in the list of basic liberties.** Rawls's list is specific: freedom of thought, liberty of conscience, political liberties, freedom of association, freedoms specified by the liberty and integrity of the person, and the rights and liberties covered by the rule of law. Unregulated property rights and freedom of contract are not basic liberties and can be regulated by the second principle.
- **You treat formal legal equality as sufficient.** The fair value of political liberties requires that all citizens have roughly equal opportunity to influence political outcomes, regardless of wealth. Formal political equality combined with unlimited political spending is insufficient.

## Core Concept
Rawls identifies a specific list of basic liberties that have lexical priority over economic distribution. These liberties protect the two moral powers — the capacity for a sense of justice and the capacity for a conception of the good — that define persons as free and equal.

The basic liberties are: freedom of thought and liberty of conscience; political liberties (the right to vote and be eligible for public office) together with freedom of speech and of assembly; freedom of association; the freedoms specified by the liberty and integrity of the person (freedom from physical assault, freedom of movement, and a right to hold personal property); and the rights and liberties covered by the rule of law.

The lexical priority of liberty means that basic liberties cannot be restricted for economic gain, social utility, or perfectionist values. Restrictions on basic liberties can be justified only when they protect other basic liberties or when, under conditions of genuine scarcity, the material prerequisites for the meaningful exercise of liberty cannot be met.

Critically, Rawls insists on the fair value of political liberties. It is not enough that all citizens have the formal right to vote and run for office. The worth of these liberties must be approximately equal — meaning that wealth cannot be permitted to translate into disproportionate political influence. This has implications for campaign finance, media ownership, lobbying regulation, and the broader relationship between economic and political power.

## How to Apply
1. **When liberty and economic efficiency conflict, liberty prevails.** A policy that restricts basic liberties to increase economic output violates the first principle regardless of the magnitude of economic gain.
2. **Assess the fair value of political liberties, not just their formal existence.** Do all citizens have roughly equal capacity to influence political outcomes?
3. **Distinguish basic liberties from non-basic liberties.** Freedom of contract and property rights beyond personal property are important but not basic — they can be regulated by the second principle.

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's Constitution protects an extensive set of rights, but the relationship between economic inequality and political influence raises questions about the fair value of political liberties.
**Application:** Rawls would examine whether South Africa's political system gives approximately equal political influence to all citizens regardless of wealth. The formal political liberties are robust: universal suffrage, freedom of expression, freedom of association, a functioning multi-party system. But the fair value of these liberties is compromised by several features of the basic structure. Party funding has historically been opaque, allowing wealthy donors and corporations to exercise disproportionate influence over party positions and candidate selection. Media ownership concentration means that wealthy individuals and corporations shape public discourse. The revolving door between political office and corporate boards creates alignment between political and economic elites. The practical capacity of poor citizens to participate in politics — attending public meetings, accessing information, engaging in civil society — is constrained by poverty, spatial distance, and the time demands of survival. Rawls would argue that securing the fair value of political liberties requires: transparent and limited party funding (the Political Party Funding Act is a step); media diversity requirements; conflict of interest regulations; and investment in the conditions that enable poor citizens to participate meaningfully in political life.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat liberty and equality as fundamentally in tension.
**Why:** Rawls integrates liberty and equality through the lexical ordering of the two principles. Equal basic liberties are not in tension with the difference principle — they are its prerequisite. The question is not liberty versus equality but which institutional arrangement best secures both.

**Don't:** Extend the priority of liberty to all economic freedoms.
**Why:** The priority of liberty applies to a specific list of basic liberties connected to the moral powers. Economic freedoms (property rights, contract rights, occupational licensing) are important but regulable. Treating all economic freedoms as basic liberties would make the difference principle inapplicable.
