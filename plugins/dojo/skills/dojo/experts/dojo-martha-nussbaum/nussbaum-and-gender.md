# Nussbaum and Gender

## When to Use
- When gender inequality needs to be analysed through the capability framework — identifying which specific capabilities are denied to women and what institutional arrangements deny them.
- When arguing that gender justice is not a peripheral concern but central to the capability approach because women are disproportionately denied central capabilities in every society.
- When *Women and Human Development* (2000) and *Sex and Social Justice* (1999) provide the framework.

## Core Concept
Nussbaum developed the capability approach partly in response to the systematic denial of women's capabilities across all societies. In *Women and Human Development* (2000), she argues that women are disproportionately denied central capabilities — bodily integrity (sexual and domestic violence), bodily health (maternal mortality, malnutrition), senses/imagination/thought (denial of education), practical reason (denial of autonomous decision-making), affiliation (denial of public participation), and control over environment (denial of property rights, employment discrimination).

The capability approach is particularly powerful for gender analysis because it moves beyond formal equality (equal rights on paper) to substantive capability (what women can actually do and be). A woman may have the legal right to own property but lack the capability — because social norms prevent her from exercising the right, because family structures deny her control, or because institutional practices discriminate against her. The capability approach sees past the formal right to the substantive reality.

Nussbaum insists that gender analysis is constitutive of the capability approach — not an add-on. Every capability assessment must disaggregate by gender; every programme design must consider women's capabilities specifically; every evaluation must ask whether the programme has secured women's capabilities or merely improved aggregate outcomes that may conceal gender inequality.

## How to Apply
1. **Disaggregate by gender on every capability.** "For each central capability, assess men's and women's status separately. Aggregate measures conceal gender gaps. The household that is 'food secure' may contain a woman who eats last and least."
2. **Identify the denial mechanisms.** "Which specific mechanisms deny women's capabilities? Legal barriers? Social norms? Institutional practices? Intra-household dynamics? Each mechanism requires a different intervention."
3. **Listen to women's own articulations.** "Women in different contexts articulate their capability deprivations differently. The programme should begin with women's own accounts of what they cannot do and be — not with the programme designer's assumptions about women's needs."
4. **Resist the victim narrative.** "Women are not only victims of capability denial — they are agents who navigate, resist, and transform the conditions that constrain them. The programme should support women's agency, not merely address their deprivation."

## Examples
**Situation:** A livelihood programme measures success by household income. Household income has increased by 25%. But a gender-disaggregated analysis reveals that the income increase flows primarily to male household members, women's workload has increased because of the programme's labour demands, and women's decision-making authority over household expenditure has not changed.
**Application:** "The programme has improved the household's economic capability while failing to address — and possibly worsening — women's capabilities specifically. The household income increase is real but distributionally blind. The capability approach demands individual-level analysis: what is this woman able to do and be? Her capability of control over material environment (the ability to hold property and work on equal terms) has not improved — the income increase flows to her husband. Her capability of bodily health may have deteriorated — increased workload without increased nutrition or rest. Her capability of practical reason — the ability to form and pursue her own conception of the good — remains constrained by her lack of decision-making authority. The programme has succeeded at the household level and failed at the individual level. Gender-disaggregated capability assessment would reveal this failure; household-level income measurement conceals it. The remedy: design for women's capabilities specifically — women's control over programme income, women's decision-making authority, women's workload management, and women's own assessment of their capability gains."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat gender as supplementary.
**Why:** Gender analysis is constitutive of the capability approach. A capability assessment that does not disaggregate by gender is incomplete by definition.

**Don't:** Impose external feminist frameworks.
**Why:** Women in different contexts understand their capability deprivations through their own moral and cultural vocabularies. The programme should begin with women's own articulations, not with imported frameworks.
