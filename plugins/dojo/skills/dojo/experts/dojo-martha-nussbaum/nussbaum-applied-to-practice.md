# Nussbaum Applied to Practice

## When to Use
- When the practitioner understands the capability approach in principle but needs a structured protocol for applying it.
- When designing capability-based programme assessments, evaluations, or policies.
- When translating Nussbaum's ten central capabilities into operational frameworks.

## Core Concept
Five steps constitute a capability-based assessment protocol:

First, capability mapping. For the target population, assess the status of each of the ten central capabilities. Which are above the threshold? Which are below? The mapping produces a capability profile — a picture of what persons can and cannot do and be.

Second, threshold analysis. For each capability that falls below the threshold, analyse the gap: how far below? What specific barriers — legal, institutional, social, economic, physical — produce the gap? The threshold analysis identifies the justice failures that the programme must address.

Third, conversion factor identification. What factors prevent resources from translating into capabilities? Disability, gender, geography, discrimination, social norms — these conversion factors explain why persons with adequate resources may still lack capabilities. The programme must address conversion factors, not just provide resources.

Fourth, institutional design. What institutional arrangements — constitutional protections, legal guarantees, social services, economic policies — would secure the denied capabilities? The capability approach demands institutional expression: philosophy without institutions is empty.

Fifth, dignity audit. Does the programme's design — its registration processes, its delivery mechanisms, its monitoring systems — respect the dignity of those it serves? A programme that secures capabilities through humiliating processes has failed the dignity test.

## How to Apply
1. **Begin with capability mapping.** "Before designing the programme, map the capability profile of the target population. Which of the ten capabilities are secured? Which are denied? The denied capabilities set the programme's priorities."
2. **Conduct threshold analysis.** "For each denied capability, how far below the threshold is the population? What barriers produce the gap? The threshold analysis transforms a philosophical framework into an operational diagnostic."
3. **Address conversion factors.** "Do not assume that providing resources will produce capabilities. Identify the conversion factors — personal, social, environmental — that prevent resources from translating into capabilities. Design for the conversion factors, not just the resources."
4. **Design institutions, not just programmes.** "Programmes end. Institutions persist. The programme should aim to establish institutional arrangements that will secure capabilities after the programme concludes."

## Examples
**Situation:** A development agency is designing a comprehensive programme for a district identified as severely deprived. The agency wants to apply the capability approach to programme design.
**Application:** "Apply the five-step protocol. Step one, capability mapping: assess each of the ten capabilities for the district population, disaggregated by gender, disability status, and geography. The mapping may reveal that capabilities 1–3 (life, bodily health, bodily integrity) are severely compromised in rural areas; that capability 4 (senses, imagination, thought) is constrained by poor-quality education; that capability 7 (affiliation) is undermined by ethnic divisions; and that capability 10 (control over environment) is denied by landlessness and political exclusion. Step two, threshold analysis: for each compromised capability, identify the specific barriers. Rural maternal mortality (capability 2) is produced by distance to facilities, lack of transport, and untrained birth attendants. Educational deprivation (capability 4) is produced by teacher absenteeism, irrelevant curriculum, and the exclusion of girls. Step three, conversion factors: even where resources exist, they may not translate into capabilities. The health facility exists but women cannot reach it (geography as conversion factor). The school exists but girls are not permitted to attend (gender norms as conversion factor). Step four, institutional design: what institutional arrangements would secure the denied capabilities permanently? Health: a staffed health post in every sub-district with community-based midwives. Education: locally recruited teachers, gender-responsive curriculum, girl-friendly school infrastructure. Governance: decentralised decision-making with mandatory inclusion of women and minorities. Step five, dignity audit: does the programme's design respect the dignity of those it serves? The registration process, the targeting mechanism, the delivery modality — each must be assessed for its impact on dignity."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce the protocol to a checklist.
**Why:** Each step requires genuine philosophical and analytical work — not mechanical application. The capability approach is a way of thinking, not a form to fill in.

**Don't:** Disconnect the protocol from its philosophical foundations.
**Why:** Without the philosophical foundation — dignity, threshold, choice, universalism — the protocol becomes just another assessment tool. The philosophy gives the protocol its moral force.
