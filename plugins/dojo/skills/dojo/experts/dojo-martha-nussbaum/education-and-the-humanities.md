# Education and the Humanities

## When to Use
- When educational policy is being designed or evaluated.
- When the role of the humanities is being debated.
- When democratic-citizenship education is the subject.

## Fails When
- **The argument is read as elitist.** Nussbaum's argument is for universal humanities education, not for protecting elite arts faculties.

## Core Concept

Nussbaum's *Cultivating Humanity* (1997) and *Not for Profit* (2010) argue that the capabilities for democratic citizenship require an education that goes beyond technical training. The thesis:

**Three capacities are central to democratic citizenship:**

**1. Socratic self-examination.** The capacity to examine one's own beliefs critically, to follow arguments where they lead, to recognise inconsistencies, to revise one's positions. This is the senses-imagination-thought capability in its critical dimension.

**2. Narrative imagination.** The capacity to imagine what it is like to be in another person's situation, particularly people unlike oneself — across class, race, nation, gender, ability. This is what compassion requires and what political deliberation across difference depends on.

**3. World citizenship.** The capacity to see oneself as a member of the human community, not only as a member of a local or national group. This grounds the cosmopolitan dimensions of justice.

**The role of the humanities.** The literary, historical, philosophical, and artistic traditions are particularly suited to developing these capacities. Literature trains narrative imagination by inviting readers into unfamiliar lives. Philosophy trains Socratic examination by modelling argumentative engagement. History trains world citizenship by displaying the breadth of human experience. The arts develop the senses and imagination directly.

**The threat.** Educational systems globally are narrowing. The pressure to produce technically trained, economically productive citizens leads to the displacement of humanities education. The result is citizens with technical skills but reduced capacity for the work of democracy.

**The democratic stakes.** Democracies depend on citizens capable of critical engagement, empathic understanding of fellow citizens, and cosmopolitan vision. A population with technical skill but stunted humanities education is poorly equipped for democratic citizenship. The result is democracy's erosion — visible across many contemporary democracies.

**The South African dimension.** South African educational debates have engaged with the basic-education crisis (literacy and numeracy outcomes), the higher-education crisis (Fees Must Fall, decolonisation), and the technical-vs-humanities debates. The capabilities framework supports humanities education across all levels — not as luxury but as foundation for democratic citizenship.

**The decolonisation dimension.** Nussbaum has engaged with debates about decolonising curricula, supporting the substantive engagement of non-Western traditions in humanities education while resisting the move toward cultural-relativist abandonment of universal capability claims.

**Universalism in pedagogy.** Humanities education is sometimes attacked as elitist or as cultural imposition. Nussbaum's argument is that the capacities it cultivates are universal, the traditions it draws on are global, and the practice should be universal — every citizen has a claim to the cultivation of these capacities, not just an elite.

**Implications for policy.** Universal humanities education across all levels; resistance to narrowing toward technical training only; investment in arts and culture as democratic infrastructure; teacher development that supports the demanding pedagogical practice the humanities require.

## How to Apply

1. **Defend humanities education as foundation of democratic capability**, not as luxury.
2. **Make humanities education universal**, not confined to elite institutions.
3. **Resist instrumentalisation of education** as only producing economically productive citizens.
4. **Support cross-cultural engagement** in humanities curricula, integrating local and global traditions.

## Examples

**Situation:** South African higher-education funding debates around STEM vs humanities allocation.

**Application:** The capabilities lens: prioritising STEM at the expense of humanities undermines democratic capabilities. A country with strong STEM and weakened humanities is poorly equipped for the work of democratic citizenship — particularly important for a young democracy navigating substantial social difference. The reform is not "STEM vs humanities" but adequate investment in both, with the humanities recognised as constitutive of democratic citizenship, not as ornament.

**Result:** An educational policy framing that values humanities education for its democratic and capability-building role.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat humanities as anti-economic-development.
**Why:** The framework supports both economic and democratic capabilities. The choice is false.

**Don't:** Limit humanities to elite institutions.
**Why:** Democratic citizenship requires universal cultivation of the capacities.
