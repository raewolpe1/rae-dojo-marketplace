# Nussbaum and Development

## When to Use
- When development practice needs a philosophical framework that goes beyond economic growth and service delivery to ask what persons can actually do and be.
- When designing, evaluating, or reforming programmes with capability-based objectives.
- When the connection between Nussbaum's philosophical framework and operational development practice needs to be made explicit.

## Core Concept
The capability approach provides the most philosophically grounded alternative to GDP-based and income-based development measurement. Where GDP measures aggregate economic output, and income measures individual purchasing power, capabilities measure what persons can actually do and be — the substantive freedoms they enjoy.

Nussbaum's contribution to development thinking goes beyond Sen's in three ways that matter for practice. First, the specific list of central capabilities provides a concrete framework for programme design and evaluation — not merely a general orientation but a specific checklist of what a dignified life requires. Second, the threshold principle provides a justice standard — below the threshold is a justice failure, not merely an unfortunate outcome. Third, the insistence on institutional expression — capabilities must be secured through constitutions, laws, policies, and programmes — connects the philosophy to the institutional architecture of development.

For development practice, the capability approach reframes every dimension of programme work. Programme design: not "what services will we provide?" but "what capabilities will we enable?" Targeting: not "who is income-poor?" but "whose capabilities fall below the threshold?" Evaluation: not "what outputs were delivered?" but "what capabilities were secured?" Policy: not "what will grow GDP?" but "what will enable every person to live a life worthy of human dignity?"

## How to Apply
1. **Design for capabilities.** "Begin programme design not from activities or outputs but from capabilities: which central capabilities does the programme aim to secure? For whom? To what threshold level? Work backward from capabilities to the activities required."
2. **Target by capability deprivation.** "Identify persons whose capabilities fall below the threshold — not only the income-poor but those denied capabilities by disability, discrimination, gender, geography, or social exclusion."
3. **Evaluate by capability change.** "The evaluation question is not 'what did the programme deliver?' but 'what capabilities did the programme enable?' The woman who received a service but cannot exercise the capability has not been served."
4. **Connect to institutions.** "Capabilities must be secured through institutional arrangements. The programme's sustainability depends not on continued funding but on whether the institutional arrangements that secure capabilities will persist."

## Examples
**Situation:** A development agency is revising its results framework. The current framework measures outputs (schools built, health workers trained, roads constructed) and outcomes (enrolment rates, mortality rates, travel times). The agency wants to incorporate the capability approach but is unsure how to operationalise it.
**Application:** "The current framework measures means (infrastructure, personnel) and proximate outcomes (enrolment, mortality). A capability framework would add the dimension that these means are supposed to serve: what are persons actually able to do and be as a result? For education: not only enrolment rates but the capability of senses, imagination, and thought — can the student actually think critically, imagine, create? Measure through assessment of critical thinking, creative expression, and Socratic capacity, not only through standardised test scores. For health: not only mortality rates but the capability of bodily health — can the person maintain good health throughout life, including reproductive health? Measure through access, quality of care, and individual health capability, not only through aggregate mortality statistics. For infrastructure: not only travel time but the capability of control over one's environment — can the person move freely, access services, participate in economic and political life? Measure through actual mobility, participation, and access, not only through road kilometres. The capability framework does not replace the current framework — it reorients it. The outputs and outcomes remain important as means. But the end — what persons can actually do and be — must be measured separately."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present capabilities as replacing all other metrics.
**Why:** Income, service coverage, and output measures are useful — they measure means that are necessary for capabilities. The capability approach reorients measurement, it does not eliminate existing metrics.

**Don't:** Ignore operational challenges.
**Why:** Measuring capabilities is harder than measuring income or service delivery. Some capabilities (practical reason, emotions, affiliation) resist simple measurement. Acknowledge the difficulty while insisting on the importance.
