# Nussbaum and Education

## When to Use
- When education policy or programmes are being evaluated solely by economic metrics — test scores, employment rates, returns on investment — without considering whether education is fulfilling its broader purpose.
- When arguing that education should cultivate not just skills but capacities for democratic citizenship, moral imagination, and critical thought.
- When Nussbaum's *Not for Profit* (2010) provides the framework for the discussion.

## Core Concept
In *Not for Profit: Why Democracy Needs the Humanities* (2010), Nussbaum argues that education faces a global crisis: the humanities and arts are being defunded and marginalised in favour of education for economic growth. This produces technically skilled persons who lack the capacities essential for democratic life: critical thinking, empathetic imagination, and responsible citizenship.

Nussbaum identifies three capacities that education must cultivate. First, critical thinking: the Socratic capacity to examine one's own beliefs, question authority, construct and evaluate arguments, and resist manipulation. A citizen who cannot think critically is not a citizen — they are a subject. Second, empathetic imagination: the capacity to understand the inner lives of others — to imagine what it is like to be someone different, in different circumstances, with different experiences. This capacity is cultivated through literature, art, and the humanities, which train the moral imagination by presenting lives unlike one's own. Third, responsible citizenship: the capacity to participate in democratic deliberation — to listen, to argue, to compromise, to take responsibility for collective decisions.

For development practice, this framework challenges education programmes that measure success solely through enrolment, completion, and test scores. These metrics capture inputs and outputs but not the capacities that education should produce. A student who scores well on standardised tests but cannot think critically, cannot imagine lives unlike her own, and cannot participate responsibly in democratic life has been educated for a job, not for a life.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess for the three capacities.** "Does this educational programme cultivate critical thinking, empathetic imagination, and responsible citizenship? If it cultivates only technical skills, it is training for employment, not educating for life."
2. **Protect the humanities.** "Literature, history, philosophy, and the arts are not luxuries — they are the disciplines that cultivate empathetic imagination. When budgets are cut, the humanities are usually the first to go. Resist this."
3. **Design for discussion.** "Education that cultivates critical thinking requires discussion-based teaching — not lecture-based instruction. Students must practise argument, examination, and deliberation, not merely receive information."
4. **Resist pure economic instrumentalism.** "Education that is valued only for its economic returns — job placement, income improvement — has abandoned its central purpose. Economic returns matter, but they are not the whole of education."

## Examples
**Situation:** A national education reform in a developing country is designed around "21st-century skills" — digital literacy, STEM competencies, and English-language proficiency. The reform defunds arts, literature, and civic education to redirect resources to STEM. International donors applaud the reform as "forward-looking."
**Application:** "The reform has defined education as job preparation and called it 'forward-looking.' It has eliminated the disciplines that cultivate the capacities democracy requires — critical thinking through philosophy, empathetic imagination through literature, civic responsibility through history and civic education — and replaced them with the disciplines that produce technically skilled workers. The donors applaud because the reform aligns with their economic model: education as human capital investment, measured by returns to labour market outcomes. But a generation educated without the humanities will be technically capable and democratically impoverished. They will be able to code but not to question; to compute but not to imagine; to work efficiently but not to deliberate wisely. The reform should be supplemented: STEM education with integrated humanities. Not arts and literature as elective additions but as core components of the curriculum. The student who can code and also think critically about the social implications of technology; who can do mathematics and also imagine lives unlike her own; who can speak English and also participate in democratic deliberation in her own language — this student has been educated, not merely trained."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Dismiss technical education.
**Why:** Technical and vocational education serves genuine needs — employment, economic security, practical competence. The argument is not that STEM is unnecessary but that it is insufficient. Education must include both.

**Don't:** Present the educational vision as impractical.
**Why:** Critical thinking, empathetic imagination, and civic responsibility can be cultivated at every level of education, in every budget context. Discussion-based teaching costs no more than lecture-based teaching. Local literature costs no more than textbooks. The vision is demanding but not impractical.
