# Nussbaum and the Field

## When to Use
- When situating Nussbaum within the broader landscape of development ethics, political philosophy, and social theory.
- When addressing legitimate critiques of the capability approach from structural, postcolonial, and libertarian perspectives.
- When clarifying the Nussbaum-Sen relationship and their distinct contributions.

## Core Concept
The capability approach is powerful but carries limitations that must be acknowledged.

First, the list critique. Nussbaum's specific list of ten central capabilities has been criticised as insufficiently democratic — who decides what the capabilities are? Nussbaum argues the list is a philosophical proposal, open to revision through cross-cultural dialogue. But critics (including Sen) worry that any fixed list risks imposing a particular conception of the good.

Second, the structural critique. The capability approach focuses on what persons can do and be — but does not systematically analyse the structural causes of capability denial. Bourdieu, Fanon, and the Marxist tradition argue that capability denial is produced by specific structures of domination — colonialism, capitalism, patriarchy — that the capability approach does not adequately theorise. The approach provides the diagnostic (these capabilities are denied) but not always the structural analysis (these specific power structures deny them).

Third, the measurement challenge. Some capabilities — practical reason, emotions, affiliation — resist quantification. The operational challenge of measuring capabilities has led to proxy measures that may capture the form but miss the substance. The Human Development Index, though inspired by Sen's version of the approach, reduces capabilities to three measurable dimensions (income, education, health) that do not capture the full list.

Fourth, the Nussbaum-Sen divergence. Sen deliberately avoids specifying a list of capabilities, preferring democratic deliberation about which capabilities matter in each context. Nussbaum argues that without a specified list, the approach lacks the philosophical precision needed to make justice claims. This is a genuine philosophical disagreement with practical implications.

## Pairings

**Amartya Sen** — Closest intellectual partner and most important interlocutor. Sen developed the capability approach; Nussbaum gave it a specific list, a threshold, and a dignity foundation. They agree on the basic framework but differ on whether to specify capabilities in advance.

**John Rawls** — Philosophical predecessor. Nussbaum's capability approach can be understood as answering the question Rawls raises (what does justice require?) with a different currency (capabilities rather than primary goods).

**Pierre Bourdieu** — Structural complement. Bourdieu's analysis of how fields, habitus, and capital distributions produce capability denial provides the structural analysis the capability approach lacks.

**Frantz Fanon** — Radical complement. Fanon's analysis of colonial domination reveals how capabilities are denied through specific historical structures of violence — psychological, economic, and political.

**Iris Murdoch** — Perceptual complement. Murdoch's moral attention provides the quality of individual perception that capability assessment requires — seeing the particular person whose capabilities are denied.

**Kwame Anthony Appiah** — Cosmopolitan partner. Appiah's cosmopolitanism provides the ethical framework for engaging across the cultural differences that the capability approach's universalism encounters.

**Michael Quinn Patton** — Methodological partner. Patton's developmental evaluation and utilisation-focused evaluation provide methodological frameworks for capability-based programme assessment.

## Examples
**Situation:** A colleague argues that the capability approach is "too Western" — that the ten central capabilities reflect Nussbaum's Western philosophical training and do not genuinely represent universal human requirements.
**Application:** "The critique deserves serious engagement. Nussbaum has always presented the list as a philosophical proposal, open to revision through cross-cultural dialogue. And the list does reflect her specific philosophical inheritance — Aristotelian, liberal, feminist. But examine the capabilities themselves: life, bodily health, bodily integrity, senses and thought, emotions, practical reason, affiliation, other species, play, control over environment. Are these Western? The capacity to live, to be healthy, to think, to form attachments, to play, to participate in decisions — these are not Western values. They are human requirements, expressed through every tradition. The Akan concept of personhood includes many of these capabilities, expressed differently. The Islamic tradition affirms many of these requirements. The Ubuntu tradition emphasises affiliation as constitutive of personhood. The universality claim is not that these specific capabilities were produced by Western philosophy but that they describe requirements of a dignified human life that every tradition recognises, in its own vocabulary. The cross-cultural dialogue that Nussbaum calls for should refine and potentially revise the list — but the challenge is to produce a better list, not to abandon the project of specifying what a dignified life requires."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Collapse Nussbaum into Sen.
**Why:** Their approaches differ significantly. Sen's is deliberately open-ended; Nussbaum's is deliberately specified. Both have strengths and weaknesses. The distinction matters.

**Don't:** Dismiss the structural critique.
**Why:** The capability approach identifies capability denial but does not always explain the structural causes. Supplementing with structural analysis (Bourdieu, Fanon, political economy) strengthens the approach.
