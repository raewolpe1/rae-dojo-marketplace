# Nussbaum and Virtue Ethics

## When to Use
- When connecting development practice to the Aristotelian tradition of asking what constitutes a fully human, flourishing life.
- When arguing that development's purpose is not economic growth but the enabling of conditions in which persons can flourish — exercise their capacities, develop their virtues, live well.
- When the philosophical anthropology underlying the capability approach needs to be articulated.

## Core Concept
Nussbaum's philosophical roots are Aristotelian. Her capability approach begins from an Aristotelian question: what does a flourishing human life require? Aristotle argued that human beings have a characteristic function (ergon) — the exercise of rational and social capacities — and that the good life consists in exercising these capacities excellently (virtue). Political arrangement exists to enable this flourishing.

Nussbaum adapts Aristotle for modern political philosophy in two crucial ways. First, she pluralises the good: where Aristotle had a specific conception of the best life (contemplation), Nussbaum argues that there are many valuable forms of human life, and the political community should enable a range of possibilities rather than prescribing a single ideal. This is why she insists on capabilities rather than functionings — the state should enable persons to live well, not prescribe how. Second, she universalises the claim: Aristotle restricted full flourishing to free adult Greek males; Nussbaum extends it to every human being, regardless of gender, class, disability, or nationality.

The Aristotelian inheritance gives the capability approach its distinctive character: it is not a theory of welfare (maximising satisfaction), nor a theory of rights (protecting negative liberties), nor a theory of fairness (distributing resources equally). It is a theory of flourishing: what conditions must be in place for each person to exercise their human capacities and live a fully human life?

## How to Apply
1. **Ask the flourishing question.** "Is this programme creating the conditions in which persons can flourish — exercise their capacities, develop their virtues, live well? Or is it merely sustaining survival without enabling flourishing?"
2. **Connect to human capacities.** "What human capacities does this programme enable? Rational thought? Social connection? Creative expression? Practical reason? The programme should address the full range of human capacities, not just material needs."
3. **Maintain pluralism.** "The capability approach does not prescribe a single good life. It provides the conditions for persons to choose and pursue their own conceptions of the good. Respect this pluralism in programme design."
4. **Demand institutional conditions.** "Virtue and flourishing are not merely individual achievements — they require institutional conditions: education, social security, political participation, legal protection. Without these conditions, individual flourishing is impossible."

## Examples
**Situation:** A youth development programme defines success as employment and income. Young people who secure jobs are counted as successes; those who do not are counted as failures. A programme manager wonders whether this definition captures what the programme should actually be pursuing.
**Application:** "Employment and income are important — they contribute to the capability of control over one's material environment. But they are not the whole of human flourishing. The Aristotelian question is: are these young people able to exercise their human capacities — to think critically, to form deep relationships, to participate in democratic life, to create, to play, to reflect on their own lives and choose their own paths? A young person who has a job but lacks the capacity for critical thought, who has income but no meaningful social connections, who earns a wage but cannot participate in the political decisions that affect her life — this person is employed but not flourishing. The programme should measure not only employment but the full range of capabilities: is this young person able to think for herself (senses, imagination, thought)? To form attachments (emotions)? To plan her own life (practical reason)? To engage with others as an equal (affiliation)? To play and enjoy herself (play)? Employment is a means — the end is a flourishing human life."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Impose a single conception of the good life.
**Why:** The capability approach is deliberately pluralist. It provides conditions for flourishing; it does not prescribe what flourishing looks like for each person.

**Don't:** Ignore the institutional conditions for virtue.
**Why:** Virtue is not purely an individual achievement. It requires institutional conditions — education, social security, legal protection, political participation — without which individual flourishing is not possible.
