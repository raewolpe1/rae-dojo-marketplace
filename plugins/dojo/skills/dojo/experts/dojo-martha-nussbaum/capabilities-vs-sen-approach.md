# Capabilities Approach: Nussbaum vs Sen

## When to Use
- When clarifying how Nussbaum's framework differs from Sen's.
- When debating whether a substantive list is needed.
- When applying the capabilities approach in practice.

## Fails When
- **The differences are exaggerated.** Nussbaum and Sen agree on the broad framework; they differ on specific elaborations.

## Core Concept

Amartya Sen developed the capability approach in his work from the 1970s onward — *Commodities and Capabilities* (1985), *Development as Freedom* (1999), *The Idea of Justice* (2009). Nussbaum has developed her version partly in dialogue with Sen, with significant agreements and important differences.

**Agreements:**

- Capabilities — what people are actually free to do and be — are the right metric of well-being, not utilities or resources.
- Functionings (what people actually do) are distinct from capabilities (what they are free to do).
- Multidimensionality: human well-being has multiple irreducible dimensions.
- Individual focus: aggregate measures cannot capture what matters.
- The approach is normative, not just descriptive.

**Differences:**

**1. The substantive list.** Sen has consistently declined to specify a definitive list of central capabilities, arguing that the specification should be the subject of public reasoning in each context. Nussbaum argues that a substantive list is both possible and necessary — without it, the framework cannot anchor political-philosophical conclusions and risks indeterminacy. The Nussbaum list is open to democratic refinement but offers a starting point.

**2. The political-philosophical anchoring.** Sen's framework remains more comparative and procedural — assessing relative capabilities, asking what improvements are possible, engaging in social-choice analysis. Nussbaum's framework is more substantively political-philosophical, grounding capabilities in a Kantian-Aristotelian dignity conception, with the apparatus of political liberalism (overlapping consensus with religious and philosophical traditions).

**3. The threshold.** Nussbaum is explicit about threshold concepts — each person must reach a threshold on each capability for justice to be served. Sen's framework is more comparative and continuous; the threshold concept is implicit at most.

**4. The role of dignity.** Nussbaum places human dignity at the foundation of the framework. Sen draws on dignity but anchors the framework more in the agency-and-freedom tradition.

**5. The treatment of feminist and disability claims.** Nussbaum has integrated feminist theory and disability theory deeply into the framework. Sen's framework has been more general, with these dimensions added by other writers.

**6. The treatment of emotion.** Nussbaum has integrated philosophy of emotion into the capabilities approach. Sen's framework is more cognitive in its core architecture.

**7. The cosmopolitan extension.** Both extend the framework globally, but Nussbaum (in *Frontiers of Justice*) develops the implications for global justice, animals, and disability more systematically.

**Mutual respect.** Sen and Nussbaum have been long-term collaborators and friends, with mutual respect. The differences are real but the framework is shared. In practice, "the capabilities approach" is used to refer to both, with the specific version's choice depending on what the analyst is doing.

**The choice between them:**

- For policy evaluation requiring substantive operationalisation: Nussbaum's list is more usable.
- For comparative assessment and social-choice analysis: Sen's framework is more flexible.
- For political philosophy grounding rights claims: Nussbaum's apparatus is more developed.
- For development-economics application: both are used, often in combination.

## How to Apply

1. **When operationalising, use Nussbaum's list** as a starting structure.
2. **When comparing across contexts, draw on Sen's procedural flexibility.**
3. **When grounding policy in rights claims, Nussbaum's political-philosophical apparatus is more developed.**
4. **Recognise that the differences are within a shared framework, not between rival schools.**

## Examples

**Situation:** Designing a development-evaluation framework for South Africa post-2030.

**Application:** The hybrid approach: Nussbaum's list as the substantive structure (ten capabilities); Sen's procedural emphasis on contextual specification and public reasoning to refine the operationalisation; integration of feminist and disability dimensions per Nussbaum; integration of agency emphasis per Sen. The result: a substantively grounded but procedurally flexible framework.

**Result:** A framework that uses the strengths of both versions.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the choice between Sen and Nussbaum as a binary.
**Why:** Most applied work uses elements of both.

**Don't:** Misrepresent Sen as having no normative content.
**Why:** Sen is deeply normative; the differences are about elaboration, not foundation.
