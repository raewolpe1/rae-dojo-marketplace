# Global Justice and Capabilities

## When to Use
- When the international obligations of justice are at issue.
- When framing development assistance as a justice question rather than charity.
- When debating climate, migration, or global-health policy.

## Fails When
- **The framework is treated as utopian.** Nussbaum is realistic about the political difficulty while insisting on the normative claims.

## Core Concept

The capabilities approach has substantial implications for global justice. *Frontiers of Justice* (2006) extends the framework across borders, arguing that justice is owed to each person regardless of nationality.

**The basic claim.** Each person, by virtue of their dignity, has a claim to the threshold level of the central capabilities. Borders do not change this. The claim is global.

**The political-structural reality.** The current global structure does not deliver capabilities equally. People born in poor countries face systematic deprivation of capabilities for which they bear no responsibility. The global structure — trade rules, intellectual property regimes, migration restrictions, climate policy — substantially shapes the distribution.

**The institutional response.** Justice requires global institutional reform. Specific implications: substantial development assistance (as obligation, not charity); fair trade arrangements; intellectual-property regimes that allow access to medicines and technology; climate policy that addresses historical responsibility; migration policy that recognises capability shortfalls as legitimate grounds for movement.

**The framework displaces narrowly national-political conceptions of justice.** The Rawlsian framework (in *Law of Peoples*) treats global justice as a relationship between peoples, not between persons. Nussbaum argues this is inadequate — each person, not each people, is the subject of justice.

**The duties of wealthier states are real.** The capabilities framework grounds robust duties of wealthier states toward poorer ones — substantial transfers, market access, technology transfer, climate finance. These are not generosity but justice.

**The institutional gap is severe.** The framework recognises that no current global institution adequately implements global justice. The work of justice includes building such institutions.

**The implications for development practice.** Development aid is owed, not gifted. The terms — conditionality, donor-defined priorities, project structures — should reflect the recipient's capability priorities, not the donor's strategic interests. The current aid architecture is partly justified and partly unjust.

**The climate dimension.** Climate change disproportionately affects countries that contributed least to it. The capabilities framework grounds historical-responsibility-based claims for climate finance, adaptation support, and migration accommodation. The current global climate response is grossly inadequate from a capabilities-justice standpoint.

**The migration dimension.** When capabilities are systematically denied in one's country of birth, the capability to seek their fulfilment elsewhere is a legitimate claim. The current migration regime — which substantially traps people in capabilities-poor contexts — is unjust on the framework. Reform requires expanding mobility rights and addressing the root causes of capability deprivation.

**For South Africa:** the country is positioned ambiguously — a middle-income country that is both recipient and provider of development assistance, embedded in deeply unequal global structures, central to African continental development. The capabilities-justice framework supports active engagement with global justice questions, including climate, trade, and migration policy from a Global South perspective.

## How to Apply

1. **Treat development assistance as obligation, not charity.**
2. **Apply the capabilities framework to global institutional design.**
3. **Make historical responsibility relevant** to climate and trade.
4. **Treat migration as a legitimate capability claim.**

## Examples

**Situation:** South African foreign policy positioning on global trade and climate.

**Application:** The capabilities-justice lens: trade rules that allow technology transfer for South African development serve global justice. Climate finance from historically responsible countries to climate-vulnerable countries serves global justice. The country's role in BRICS and African continental governance is partly the work of building institutional structures more aligned with capabilities-justice. The framework supports active rather than passive engagement.

**Result:** A foreign-policy framing grounded in global justice as a substantive commitment.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat global justice as a utopian aspiration disconnected from policy.
**Why:** The framework has concrete implications for actual decisions.

**Don't:** Confine justice obligations to the national community.
**Why:** The capabilities claim is grounded in dignity, which is not bounded by nation.
