# Nussbaum and Disability

## When to Use
- When persons with disabilities are being excluded from programmes, and the exclusion needs to be analysed as a justice failure rather than an unfortunate limitation.
- When the capability approach reveals that disability is not merely a medical condition but a capability gap produced by the interaction between impairment and social/institutional barriers.
- When *Frontiers of Justice* (2006) provides the framework for extending justice to persons with disabilities.

## Core Concept
In *Frontiers of Justice* (2006), Nussbaum argues that persons with disabilities represent a frontier for theories of justice — a test case that reveals the limitations of traditional approaches (particularly the social contract tradition) and the strengths of the capability approach.

The capability approach is uniquely suited to disability because it moves beyond resources (which may be adequate) to capabilities (which may not be). A person with a mobility impairment may have the same income as a person without one but radically different capabilities — because the built environment, the transport system, and the institutional arrangements were designed for persons without impairments. The capability denial is produced not by the impairment alone but by the interaction between the impairment and the social/institutional environment.

This reframes disability as a justice issue. The person with a disability who cannot access a public building is not experiencing a medical problem — she is experiencing a justice failure. The building was designed in a way that denies her capability of movement, participation, and control over her environment. The remedy is not medical treatment but institutional redesign: accessible buildings, inclusive services, assistive technology, and legal protections.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess capabilities, not impairment.** "The question is not 'what is this person's disability?' but 'what capabilities are denied, and what barriers produce the denial?' The capability gap — not the impairment — is the justice issue."
2. **Design for inclusion from the start.** "Inclusion is a design principle, not an add-on. A programme designed for 'normal' participants and then 'adapted' for persons with disabilities has embedded exclusion in its structure."
3. **Identify the conversion factors.** "What barriers prevent persons with disabilities from converting resources into capabilities? Physical barriers, attitudinal barriers, institutional barriers, information barriers — each must be identified and addressed."
4. **Include persons with disabilities in design.** "Nothing about us without us. Persons with disabilities must participate in the design of programmes that affect them — as experts on their own capability deprivation, not as objects of charitable assistance."

## Examples
**Situation:** A community health programme provides outreach services but has not adapted its services for persons with disabilities. Health workers visit households but do not have the training, equipment, or protocols to serve persons with hearing impairments, visual impairments, or mobility limitations. Persons with disabilities receive the same visit as everyone else — which means they receive services designed for persons without disabilities.
**Application:** "Equal treatment is not equal capability. The health worker who visits every household equally provides equal resources but not equal capabilities. The person with a hearing impairment who receives verbal health information without sign language or visual aids does not have the capability of bodily health — not because the health worker did not visit but because the visit was not designed to secure her capability. The person with a mobility limitation who cannot reach the clinic for follow-up does not have the capability of bodily health — not because the clinic does not exist but because it was not designed for her. The programme achieves equal coverage without equal capability. The capability approach demands redesign: health workers trained in disability-inclusive service delivery, materials in accessible formats, referral pathways that account for mobility barriers, and — crucially — the participation of persons with disabilities in designing the services they receive."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Reduce disability to medical condition.
**Why:** The capability approach locates the problem in the interaction between impairment and environment, not in the impairment alone. The environment can be changed; the medical model suggests only the person can (or should) be changed.

**Don't:** Treat inclusion as charity.
**Why:** Including persons with disabilities is a matter of justice — they are denied capabilities that are their due. Framing inclusion as charitable generosity rather than as a justice obligation diminishes their dignity.
