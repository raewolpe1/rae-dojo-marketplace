# The Central Capabilities

## When to Use
- When a specific, philosophically grounded list of what a dignified life requires is needed for programme design, assessment, or evaluation.
- When diagnosing which dimensions of human flourishing a programme addresses and which it neglects.
- When arguing that development must address all central capabilities, not merely economic or health dimensions.

## Core Concept
Nussbaum's ten central capabilities constitute the minimum requirements of a life worthy of human dignity. Each is qualitatively distinct — you cannot compensate for the denial of one by providing more of another. They are:

1. **Life.** Being able to live to the end of a human life of normal length; not dying prematurely or before one's life is so reduced as to be not worth living.
2. **Bodily health.** Being able to have good health, including reproductive health; to be adequately nourished; to have adequate shelter.
3. **Bodily integrity.** Being able to move freely from place to place; to be secure against violent assault, including sexual assault and domestic violence; having opportunities for sexual satisfaction and choice in matters of reproduction.
4. **Senses, imagination, and thought.** Being able to use the senses, to imagine, think, and reason — in a truly human way informed by an adequate education. Being able to produce self-expressive works.
5. **Emotions.** Being able to have attachments to things and people outside ourselves; being able to love, to grieve, to experience longing, gratitude, and justified anger.
6. **Practical reason.** Being able to form a conception of the good and to engage in critical reflection about the planning of one's own life. This includes freedom of conscience and religious observance.
7. **Affiliation.** (a) Being able to live with and toward others, to recognise and show concern for other human beings, to engage in various forms of social interaction. (b) Having the social bases of self-respect and non-humiliation; being treated as a dignified being whose worth is equal to that of others.
8. **Other species.** Being able to live with concern for and in relation to animals, plants, and the world of nature.
9. **Play.** Being able to laugh, to play, to enjoy recreational activities.
10. **Control over one's environment.** (a) Political: being able to participate effectively in political choices. (b) Material: being able to hold property and to work on equal terms with others.

The list is open to revision — Nussbaum has always presented it as a working proposal, subject to cross-cultural dialogue. But its core claim is non-negotiable: every person must achieve a threshold level of every central capability for their life to be worthy of human dignity.

## How to Apply
1. **Conduct a capability audit.** "For the population the programme serves, assess each of the ten central capabilities. Which are above the threshold? Which are below? The denied capabilities identify the programme's priorities."
2. **Resist trade-offs below the threshold.** "Below the threshold, capabilities cannot be traded against each other. A programme that improves bodily health at the cost of bodily integrity (requiring degrading medical examinations, for example) has not achieved justice."
3. **Expand the programme's vision.** "Most development programmes address capabilities 1–3 (life, health, bodily integrity) and sometimes 10 (material environment). Capabilities 4–9 — senses and thought, emotions, practical reason, affiliation, other species, play — are systematically neglected. These are not luxuries. They are constitutive of human dignity."
4. **Specify contextually.** "The central capabilities are universal. Their institutional expression varies. 'Affiliation' requires the social bases of self-respect — but what constitutes these bases varies across communities. Specify the capability's content in dialogue with the community."

## Examples
**Situation:** A comprehensive social protection programme provides cash transfers, health coverage, education subsidies, and housing support. An evaluation assesses the programme against the ten central capabilities and finds that while capabilities 1–3 and 10(b) are reasonably well addressed, capabilities 4–9 receive no attention: recipients have no access to cultural activities, no space for play, limited emotional support, constrained practical reason (they are told what to do, not supported in choosing for themselves), and diminished affiliation (the registration process is humiliating).
**Application:** "The programme secures the material preconditions for a dignified life — income, health, education, housing — but neglects the non-material capabilities that make a life genuinely human. The recipient who has adequate nutrition but no opportunity to think, create, or express herself has not achieved a life worthy of human dignity. The recipient who has shelter but is humiliated by the programme's registration process has had her capability of affiliation — the social bases of self-respect — violated by the very programme designed to help her. The capability assessment reveals what the programme's economic indicators conceal: that material provision without attention to the full range of human capabilities is necessary but radically insufficient. The programme should: redesign the registration process to preserve recipients' dignity; include cultural and recreational activities as programme components; support recipients' practical reason by offering choices rather than mandating conditions; and address the emotional dimension — loneliness, shame, grief — that poverty produces and that economic transfers alone cannot address."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the list as a rigid checklist.
**Why:** The ten capabilities are a philosophical framework, not an audit checklist. They require interpretation, contextual specification, and philosophical engagement — not mechanical application.

**Don't:** Reduce capabilities to measurable indicators.
**Why:** Some capabilities — practical reason, emotions, affiliation — resist simple measurement. The philosophical content must be preserved even when indicators are developed. An indicator that captures the form but misses the substance of a capability is worse than no indicator at all.
