# Nussbaum and Law

## When to Use
- When capabilities need to be secured through constitutional, legal, and institutional arrangements — not merely through temporary programmes.
- When the gap between legal rights (which may exist on paper) and actual capabilities (which may not exist in practice) needs to be addressed.
- When connecting the capability approach to constitutional design, human rights law, and legal reform.

## Core Concept
Nussbaum is a professor of law as well as philosophy, and the capability approach is designed to be institutionally expressed — through constitutions, legislation, and legal frameworks. Capabilities that exist only in philosophical argument, without institutional guarantee, are aspirations rather than entitlements.

The connection between capabilities and law operates at three levels. First, constitutional: many national constitutions already contain language compatible with the capability approach — guarantees of life, health, education, dignity, and political participation. The capability approach provides the philosophical content that gives these constitutional provisions their meaning: what does "the right to health" mean in capability terms? It means the capability of bodily health — being able to have good health, including reproductive health, adequate nourishment, and adequate shelter.

Second, legislative: capabilities must be secured through specific legislation — health laws, education laws, anti-discrimination laws, environmental laws, social protection laws. Each capability requires its own institutional architecture.

Third, judicial: when capabilities are denied, persons must have access to judicial remedies. The capability approach supports justiciable social and economic rights — the claim that a court can order the state to provide education, health, or housing to persons who fall below the threshold.

For development practice, the law connection is crucial for sustainability. Programmes end; laws persist. A programme that secures capabilities for five years but does not establish the legal and institutional framework to sustain them has provided temporary relief, not justice.

## How to Apply
1. **Connect capabilities to constitutional provisions.** "Does the country's constitution guarantee capabilities? If so, the programme should be designed as the institutional expression of the constitutional guarantee — not as a discretionary donor initiative."
2. **Design for legal sustainability.** "The programme should aim to establish legal and institutional frameworks that will secure capabilities after the programme ends. The goal is not a five-year programme but a permanent institutional arrangement."
3. **Support justiciable rights.** "Where capabilities are denied, persons should have access to judicial remedies. The programme should support legal literacy, legal aid, and access to courts as components of capability security."
4. **Identify the rights-capability gap.** "Many countries have constitutional rights that are not translated into capabilities. The programme's role is to close this gap — to make the constitutional promise a lived reality."

## Examples
**Situation:** A country's constitution guarantees the right to education. But in rural areas, schools are under-resourced, teachers are absent, and girls are discouraged from attending. The constitutional right exists; the capability does not.
**Application:** "The constitutional right to education is a legal guarantee of capability 4 — senses, imagination, and thought. But the guarantee is not self-executing. The right exists on paper; the capability does not exist in practice. The programme should position itself as the institutional mechanism for closing this gap. Not as a donor initiative delivering education services but as the institutional expression of the constitutional guarantee. This framing matters: it positions the programme's objectives as legal entitlements rather than donor priorities, it engages the judiciary as a potential enforcer of the right, and it creates a sustainability pathway — when the programme ends, the constitutional obligation remains. The programme should support: legal literacy (so that communities know they have a constitutional right to education), legal aid (so that communities can enforce the right through courts), and institutional strengthening (so that the state's education system can fulfil the constitutional obligation without external programme support). The goal is not to deliver education but to make the constitutional right a lived capability."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present law as sufficient.
**Why:** Legal rights without the social, economic, and cultural conditions to exercise them are formal entitlements without substantive capabilities. Law is necessary but not sufficient.

**Don't:** Treat constitutional guarantees as automatically producing capabilities.
**Why:** Many constitutions guarantee rights that are not realised in practice. The gap between the right and the capability is the space in which development operates.
