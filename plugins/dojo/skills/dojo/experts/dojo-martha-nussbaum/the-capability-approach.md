# The Capability Approach

## When to Use
- When development measurement focuses on resources (income, assets, services) without asking whether those resources translate into actual capability — what people can actually do and be.
- When reframing the fundamental question of development from "how much does this person have?" to "what is this person able to do and to be?"
- When providing the philosophical foundation for human development as distinct from economic development.

## Core Concept
The capability approach, as Nussbaum develops it, holds that the appropriate measure of justice and development is not resources (income, GDP, assets) nor utility (preference satisfaction, happiness) but capabilities — what each person is actually able to do and to be. The approach begins from a simple but powerful observation: two persons with the same income may have radically different capabilities. A person with a disability, a person facing discrimination, a woman in a society that restricts women's movement — each may have adequate income but lack the capability to be healthy, to participate in political life, or to move freely.

Nussbaum's version of the capability approach differs from Sen's in three important ways. First, Nussbaum provides a specific list of central capabilities (Sen deliberately avoids specifying a list). Second, Nussbaum grounds the approach in the concept of human dignity (Sen grounds it more loosely in freedom and agency). Third, Nussbaum argues for a threshold — a minimum level of each capability below which a life worthy of human dignity is not possible — whereas Sen's approach is more comparative (better or worse, more or less).

The capability approach makes a fundamental distinction between capabilities and functionings. A capability is what a person is able to do or be; a functioning is what a person actually does or is. The approach values capabilities over functionings because it values choice. A person who is able to participate in political life but chooses not to has the capability; a person who cannot participate because she is excluded does not. Justice requires the capability, not the functioning — because dignity requires the freedom to choose.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask the capability question.** "For each person the programme serves, ask: what is this person actually able to do and to be? Not what resources does she have — but what can she do with those resources, given her particular circumstances?"
2. **Identify the conversion factors.** "What factors convert resources into capabilities? Disability, discrimination, gender, geography, social norms — these conversion factors determine whether resources translate into capabilities. A programme that provides resources without addressing conversion factors has not secured capabilities."
3. **Measure capabilities, not just resources.** "The programme measures school enrolment. But does the child have the capability of senses, imagination, and thought? Is she actually able to think critically, to imagine, to experience the world? Enrolment is a resource; the capability is what the resource enables."
4. **Protect choice.** "The programme should enable capabilities, not mandate functionings. The woman who can access reproductive health services but chooses not to has a capability. The woman who cannot access them does not. The distinction matters."

## Examples
**Situation:** A rural water programme has installed boreholes in 200 villages. The programme reports 100% coverage — every village has a water point within 500 metres. But a follow-up study finds that women in some villages still walk several kilometres to alternative water sources because the boreholes are controlled by male village leaders who restrict women's access during certain hours.
**Application:** "The programme has provided the resource (boreholes) without securing the capability (access to clean water). The conversion factor — gendered control of the water point — means that the resource does not translate into capability for all persons equally. The programme measures coverage: 100% of villages have a water point. The capability approach measures differently: what is each woman actually able to do? Can she access clean water when she needs it? For the women in the villages with restricted access, the answer is no — despite the borehole being 500 metres away. The programme has achieved resource coverage without achieving capability coverage. The remedy is not more boreholes but attention to the conversion factor: the gendered power relations that determine who can use the resource and when. This may require community negotiation, women's participation in water management committees, or alternative access arrangements. The capability approach insists: the measure of success is not the borehole but the woman's actual ability to access clean water. If she cannot access it, the programme has not succeeded — regardless of what the coverage statistics say."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present the capability approach as merely a measurement tool.
**Why:** It is a philosophical framework — a theory of justice that grounds measurement in a conception of human dignity. Reducing it to a measurement technique loses the philosophical force.

**Don't:** Ignore conversion factors.
**Why:** The gap between resources and capabilities is produced by conversion factors — personal, social, and environmental conditions that determine whether resources translate into capabilities. Ignoring them produces the illusion of adequacy.
