# Emotions and Political Judgment

## When to Use
- When emotional dimensions of policy are at stake (grief, anger, fear, compassion).
- When designing political communication or public ritual.
- When evaluating responses to social trauma.

## Fails When
- **The framework is used to manipulate emotions.** Nussbaum's argument is that emotions embody cognitive content and should be engaged respectfully, not manipulated.

## Core Concept

*Upheavals of Thought* (2001) is Nussbaum's monumental work on the philosophy of emotions. The central thesis: emotions are not non-cognitive feelings opposed to reason; they are evaluative judgments about matters of importance to the agent. They have cognitive content and play an essential role in human practical reason.

**The cognitive theory of emotions.** Grief embodies the judgment that one has lost something important. Fear embodies the judgment that one faces a serious threat to what one values. Anger embodies the judgment that one has been wrongfully harmed by another. Love, gratitude, compassion all embody specific evaluative judgments.

**The implications for ethics.** Emotional responses are not separate from moral reasoning; they are part of it. A person who feels no grief at the death of a friend, no anger at injustice, no compassion at suffering is exhibiting not Stoic rationality but ethical deficiency. The emotions are part of how humans recognise and respond to value.

**The implications for politics.** Political life involves emotion necessarily — fear of threat, anger at injustice, grief at loss, hope for the future, compassion across difference. The political philosopher who imagines politics as pure rational discourse misses how it actually works and what it actually involves.

**Political emotions matter both as expressions and as cultivations.** What citizens feel about each other, about institutions, about the past and the future shapes political possibilities. The cultivation of appropriate political emotions — particularly compassion and the public emotions of democratic solidarity — is part of political work.

**The role of public art, ritual, and rhetoric.** Democratic societies cultivate political emotions through public art (memorials, music, poetry), public ritual (commemorations, ceremonies), and political rhetoric (speeches that engage emotional resources). These are not decorative; they are constitutive of democratic culture. (*Political Emotions* (2013) develops this further.)

**The dangers.** Emotions can be manipulated, narrowed, or weaponised. Fear can be mobilised against minorities. Anger can be channelled into retribution. Compassion can be confined to a narrow in-group. The work of democratic citizenship includes critical reflection on the emotions one experiences, the emotions one cultivates in others, and the emotions one's institutions evoke.

**The expansion of compassion.** Compassion requires three judgments: that the suffering is serious (not trivial), that the sufferer does not deserve the suffering, and that the sufferer is similar to oneself (the eudaimonistic judgment). The third judgment is the limiting one — it explains why compassion often extends only to in-group members. Expanding compassion requires expanding the imagination of similarity — across class, race, nation, species.

**For South Africa specifically:** the post-apartheid political emotions — anger, grief, hope, fear — have shaped the political trajectory. The TRC was an attempt to engage these emotions politically. The success has been partial; the unfinished emotional work of the transition continues to shape the country's political life.

## How to Apply

1. **Take emotional dimensions of policy seriously**, not as obstacles but as constitutive content.
2. **Design public ritual, art, and rhetoric** that cultivate democratic emotions.
3. **Be alert to emotional manipulation** and the narrowing of compassion.
4. **Expand the eudaimonistic judgment** — cultivate the imagination of similarity across difference.

## Examples

**Situation:** A community grappling with loss after major flood damage.

**Application:** The grief is rational — it embodies the judgment that something important has been lost. Policy responses should engage the grief — through acknowledgement, memorialisation, sustained presence, alongside material support. A purely technical response that ignores the grief fails the affected community on the very dimension that matters most to them. The framework demands integration.

**Result:** A policy response that takes the affective dimension as core, not peripheral.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat emotion as opposed to rationality.
**Why:** Emotions embody cognitive content; the dichotomy is false.

**Don't:** Manipulate emotions for political ends.
**Why:** The framework is about engagement and respect, not strategic manipulation.
