# Nussbaum and Cosmopolitanism

## When to Use
- When the moral basis for international development needs to be articulated: why do persons in wealthy countries have obligations to persons in poor countries?
- When arguing that the capability approach's universalism entails obligations that cross national borders.
- When situating Nussbaum's cosmopolitanism in relation to Appiah's and to the Stoic tradition.

## Core Concept
Nussbaum's cosmopolitanism draws on the Stoic tradition: every human being is a citizen of the world, and our obligations extend to all human beings regardless of nationality. If every person possesses dignity, and if dignity requires a threshold level of central capabilities, then the denial of capabilities anywhere is a concern for persons everywhere.

This has direct implications for development. The wealthy nation that could contribute to securing the capabilities of persons in poorer nations and does not is failing in a duty of justice, not merely declining an act of charity. Development assistance is not generosity — it is the institutional expression of the obligation that dignity imposes.

Nussbaum's cosmopolitanism is compatible with particular attachments — to family, community, and nation. The cosmopolitan does not love all persons equally; she recognises that particular relationships create particular obligations. But she insists that particular attachments do not exhaust moral obligation. Beyond the circle of family, community, and nation, there are obligations to every human being — obligations grounded in the dignity that every person shares.

## How to Apply
1. **Ground development in obligation.** "Development assistance is a matter of justice, not charity. The capability approach provides the foundation: if every person's dignity requires a threshold level of capabilities, and if we can contribute to securing those capabilities, we are obligated to do so."
2. **Maintain the threshold globally.** "The threshold applies to every person. A world in which some persons fall below the threshold while others have vastly more than they need has not achieved justice — regardless of national borders."
3. **Support global institutional reform.** "Individual acts of assistance are insufficient. Global justice requires institutional reform: fair trade rules, international taxation, technology transfer, and development cooperation that genuinely secures capabilities."
4. **Balance global and local obligations.** "Cosmopolitanism does not require abandoning local obligations. It requires recognising that moral obligation extends beyond the local — that the suffering of distant persons is a genuine moral claim on our resources and attention."

## Examples
**Situation:** A government debates whether to increase its overseas development assistance budget. Opponents argue that national citizens' needs should be prioritised — "charity begins at home." Proponents argue for solidarity with the global poor.
**Application:** "The debate is framed as charity — discretionary generosity that competes with domestic priorities. The capability approach reframes it as justice. If every person possesses dignity, and if dignity requires a threshold level of capabilities, then the government has an obligation to contribute to securing those capabilities — domestically and internationally. 'Charity begins at home' treats international obligation as optional. Justice is not optional. The practical question is how to fulfil both domestic and international obligations — through what institutional arrangements, at what level, with what accountability. But the moral question — whether the obligation exists — is settled by the capability approach: it does, because dignity is not bounded by nationality."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Require the abolition of national identity.
**Why:** Cosmopolitanism is compatible with particular attachments. The cosmopolitan citizen loves her country while recognising obligations beyond it.

**Don't:** Ignore institutional challenges.
**Why:** Global justice requires institutions — and current global institutions are inadequate. Acknowledging the moral obligation does not resolve the institutional challenge.
