# Globalisation and Poverty

## When to Use
- When addressing the relationship between trade openness and poverty reduction.
- When responding to claims that globalisation harms the poor.
- When advising on how to design trade policy to maximise poverty reduction.

## Fails When
- **You assert that globalisation reduces poverty automatically and unconditionally.** Bhagwati's argument is that globalisation has a strong tendency to reduce poverty, but the outcome depends on complementary domestic policies — governance, education, infrastructure, social protection.
- **You ignore within-country inequality while celebrating cross-country convergence.** Bhagwati acknowledges that globalisation can increase within-country inequality even as it reduces between-country inequality and global poverty.

## Core Concept
Bhagwati's central argument, developed most fully in "In Defense of Globalization" (2004), is that trade openness and economic integration constitute the most powerful poverty reduction mechanism available. The argument operates through several channels.

First, the growth channel. Countries that integrated into the global economy grew faster than those that did not. Growth, while not sufficient for poverty reduction, is necessary at scale. The poverty reductions in China (800 million people lifted from poverty between 1980 and 2015), India (270 million between 1990 and 2015), and Vietnam, Bangladesh, and other integrating economies are historically unprecedented and occurred during the period of deepest globalisation.

Second, the employment channel. Export-oriented growth creates jobs — particularly in labour-intensive manufacturing and services that absorb unskilled and semi-skilled workers. The garment factories of Bangladesh, the electronics assembly of Vietnam, the call centres of India create employment that would not exist under autarky. These are not ideal jobs by wealthy-country standards, but they represent substantial improvements over the subsistence agriculture or informal economy alternatives.

Third, the price channel. Trade reduces the prices of goods consumed by the poor. Imported food, clothing, and basic manufactured goods are cheaper under free trade than under protection. Since the poor spend a higher share of their income on these goods, they benefit disproportionately from lower prices.

Bhagwati addresses the inequality objection directly. He distinguishes between between-country inequality (which globalisation has reduced as poor countries grew faster than rich ones) and within-country inequality (which has increased in some countries). His response to within-country inequality is not to restrict trade but to improve complementary policies: education, health care, progressive taxation, and social protection. Restricting trade to reduce inequality would reduce the total income available for redistribution — a cure worse than the disease.

## How to Apply
1. **When assessing globalisation's poverty effects, examine the counterfactual.** Compare outcomes in integrating economies with outcomes in economies that remained closed. The comparison strongly favours openness.
2. **Separate the effects of globalisation from the effects of inadequate complementary policies.** If inequality has increased in an open economy, ask whether the cause is openness or insufficient redistribution, education, and social protection.
3. **Design trade policy to maximise pro-poor effects.** Reduce tariffs on goods consumed by the poor before reducing tariffs on luxury goods. Liberalise labour-intensive sectors where the poor are most likely to find employment.

## Examples
**Situation:** A South African civil society coalition argues that globalisation has increased inequality in South Africa and advocates for higher tariffs to protect domestic manufacturing jobs.
**Application:** Bhagwati would challenge the causal attribution. South Africa's inequality — a Gini coefficient among the highest in the world — predates post-apartheid trade liberalisation and has deep roots in apartheid-era spatial planning, education inequality, and labour market dualism. Attributing this inequality to globalisation confuses correlation with causation. Moreover, the proposed remedy — higher tariffs — would raise prices for the 30 million South Africans living below or near the poverty line, who spend the highest share of income on tradeable goods (food, clothing, basic household items). The tariffs would protect a relatively small number of manufacturing jobs (the beneficiaries of protection are specific firms and their workers) at the cost of higher prices for the majority. Bhagwati's alternative: maintain trade openness to keep consumer prices low, invest the fiscal savings from not subsidising inefficient industries in education (especially early childhood and primary school quality), expand the social grant system to cushion adjustment, and address the binding constraints on employment growth (rigid labour market regulation, skills gaps, infrastructure deficits). The route to reducing South African inequality runs through better domestic policy, not through closing the economy.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Claim that all criticism of globalisation is motivated by protectionist self-interest.
**Why:** Some critics raise legitimate concerns about adjustment costs, power asymmetries in trade negotiations, and inadequate social protection. Bhagwati engages these concerns seriously while maintaining that the answer is better complementary policy, not less trade.

**Don't:** Ignore cases where trade liberalisation was poorly sequenced or implemented without adequate support.
**Why:** Bhagwati supports well-designed liberalisation with complementary reforms, not shock therapy without social protection. Poorly implemented liberalisation can produce concentrated losses that are both unjust and politically destabilising.
