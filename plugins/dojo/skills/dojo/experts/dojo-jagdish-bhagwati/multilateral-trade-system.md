# The Multilateral Trade System

## When to Use
- When advising on the value of the WTO and multilateral trade rules for developing countries.
- When evaluating whether regional or bilateral agreements serve developing country interests.
- When discussing reform of the international trade architecture.

## Fails When
- **You present Bhagwati as an uncritical defender of the WTO status quo.** Bhagwati criticises specific WTO failures — the stalled Doha Round, rich-country agricultural subsidies, TRIPS provisions — while defending the principle of multilateralism.
- **You ignore the power asymmetries within multilateral negotiations that disadvantage developing countries.** Bhagwati acknowledges these but argues they are less severe than the power asymmetries in bilateral negotiations.

## Core Concept
Bhagwati's defence of the multilateral trading system rests on a fundamental insight: small and poor countries benefit most from a rules-based system. In bilateral negotiations, economic and political power determines outcomes — the larger economy dictates terms. In a multilateral system with binding rules, even small economies have legal protections, dispute settlement mechanisms, and the ability to form coalitions.

The most-favoured-nation (MFN) principle — that trade concessions granted to one partner must be extended to all WTO members — is the system's most important feature. MFN prevents discrimination, ensures that trade preferences cannot be used as political leverage, and creates a non-discriminatory global marketplace. Every deviation from MFN (through preferential trade agreements, bilateral deals, or special arrangements) weakens this foundation.

Bhagwati acknowledges the WTO's failures. The Doha Development Round, launched in 2001 to address developing country concerns — agricultural subsidies, market access, special and differential treatment — has effectively stalled. Rich countries continue to subsidise agriculture at levels that distort global markets and undermine developing country farmers. TRIPS provisions on intellectual property impose costs on developing countries (particularly in pharmaceuticals) that may exceed the benefits. These are legitimate criticisms — but Bhagwati argues they are arguments for reforming the multilateral system, not for abandoning it in favour of bilateral deals where developing countries have even less bargaining power.

The dispute settlement mechanism, despite its imperfections, provides developing countries with legal recourse against rich-country protectionism that would be unavailable in a purely bilateral world. Small countries have successfully challenged large economies at the WTO — an outcome that bilateral power politics would never produce.

## How to Apply
1. **Advise developing countries to prioritise multilateral engagement even when the process is frustrating.** The alternative — a bilateral world — is worse for small economies.
2. **Support WTO reform that addresses developing country concerns.** Agricultural subsidy reduction, TRIPS flexibility, enhanced special and differential treatment.
3. **Evaluate regional agreements against whether they strengthen or undermine multilateral disciplines.** Agreements that build toward multilateral norms are constructive; those that create preferential fortresses are harmful.

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa participates in multiple overlapping trade arrangements — SACU, SADC, the EU Economic Partnership Agreement, AfCFTA, and various bilateral investment treaties — while WTO negotiations remain stalled.
**Application:** Bhagwati would express concern about the fragmentation. Each agreement has different rules of origin, different schedules, different product coverage, creating what he calls the "spaghetti bowl" of overlapping preferences. For South African exporters — particularly small and medium enterprises — navigating this complexity imposes compliance costs that can exceed the value of the preferences. South African trade policy should pursue a three-track strategy consistent with Bhagwati's framework. First, continue to support multilateral reform at the WTO, particularly on agricultural subsidies and services liberalisation where South Africa has offensive interests. Second, design AfCFTA with liberal rules of origin and MFN-compatible disciplines that move toward, not away from, multilateral norms. Third, simplify the existing web of preferential agreements by harmonising rules of origin across SACU, SADC, and the EPA where possible. The goal is a trade architecture that approximates the simplicity and non-discrimination of the multilateral ideal while pragmatically managing the current fragmented reality.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present the WTO as irrelevant because the Doha Round has stalled.
**Why:** The WTO's existing rules, dispute settlement mechanism, and institutional framework continue to provide value to developing countries even without new negotiating rounds. The infrastructure of rules matters independently of the pace of new liberalisation.

**Don't:** Assume that regional agreements are a satisfactory substitute for multilateral progress.
**Why:** Regional agreements cover a subset of trade partners and create discrimination against non-members. Only multilateral liberalisation provides non-discriminatory access to all markets.
