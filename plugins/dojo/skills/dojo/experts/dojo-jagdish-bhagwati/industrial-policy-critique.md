# Critique of Industrial Policy

## When to Use
- When evaluating proposals for governments to promote specific industries through subsidies, tariffs, or directed credit.
- When explaining the information and political economy problems that undermine industrial policy.
- When distinguishing between horizontal policies (improving the business environment for all) and vertical policies (picking specific sectors or firms).

## Fails When
- **You reduce the critique to a simplistic "governments can't pick winners."** Bhagwati's critique is more nuanced: the information problem is real, but the political economy problem is decisive. Even if governments could identify promising industries, the political process of allocating subsidies and protection would be captured by rent-seekers.
- **You fail to distinguish between horizontal and vertical industrial policy.** Bhagwati is more sympathetic to horizontal policies (education, infrastructure, stable macroeconomic environment, rule of law) that improve conditions for all industries than to vertical policies that favour specific sectors.

## Core Concept
Bhagwati's critique of industrial policy targets the practice, not just the theory. The theoretical case for infant industry protection — that new industries face temporary disadvantages (learning curves, scale economies, capital market imperfections) that prevent them from competing with established foreign firms, and that temporary protection allows them to reach viability — is internally coherent. Bhagwati's critique is that the conditions required for successful infant industry policy are almost never met in practice.

The information problem: identifying which industries will become competitive requires knowledge of future comparative advantage, technology trajectories, and global market conditions that no government possesses. The industries that appear promising today may be obsolete by the time they reach scale. The industries that will dominate tomorrow may not yet exist or may be invisible to government planners.

The political economy problem: once a mechanism exists for dispensing trade protection or industrial subsidies, every industry will claim to deserve support. The allocation of support will be determined not by economic merit but by political influence. Well-connected industries receive protection; innovative but politically weak firms do not. The result is a pattern of support that reflects political power rather than economic potential.

The graduation problem: temporary protection becomes permanent because the protected industry has no incentive to become competitive (protection removes the penalty for inefficiency) and has every incentive to lobby for continued protection (the rents from protection fund the lobbying). Bhagwati's observation that "infants never grow up" reflects the systematic tendency of protected industries to remain dependent on protection.

The DUP problem: industrial policy creates rents that generate directly unproductive profit-seeking activities — lobbying, corruption, circumvention — that consume real resources with zero social product. The resources devoted to capturing industrial policy rents are resources not devoted to genuine productive activity.

## How to Apply
1. **Evaluate industrial policy proposals against the three problems: information, political economy, and graduation.** If the proposal cannot demonstrate mechanisms to address all three, scepticism is warranted.
2. **Favour horizontal over vertical industrial policy.** Government investment in education, infrastructure, institutions, and the overall business environment benefits all industries and is less susceptible to rent-seeking capture.
3. **If vertical support is adopted, insist on sunset clauses, performance conditionality, and declining protection schedules.** These design features address the graduation problem, though the political economy problem makes enforcement difficult.

## Examples
**Situation:** The South African government's Industrial Policy Action Plan proposes support for the automotive, clothing, agro-processing, and green industries through a combination of tariff protection, production incentives, and designated local content requirements.
**Application:** Bhagwati would assess each element against his three-problem framework. The automotive industry programme (APDP/SAAM) provides substantial support through import duty rebates conditional on local production. The question is whether South Africa has comparative advantage in automotive manufacturing or is sustaining an industry through perpetual subsidies. After two decades of support, the industry remains dependent on incentives — Bhagwati's graduation problem in action. The clothing industry tariffs protect an industry that cannot compete with Asian producers on cost; the protection raises clothing prices for the poor while sustaining jobs at a cost per job that may exceed the wages paid. The green industries support picks a technological direction that may or may not prove competitive. The alternative Bhagwati would advocate: invest the resources currently directed to vertical industrial policy into the horizontal constraints that affect all industries — reliable electricity (the binding constraint for most South African manufacturers), efficient logistics (port and rail), skills development, and regulatory simplification. These investments improve competitiveness across all sectors rather than picking specific winners.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Claim that no government has ever successfully promoted industry.
**Why:** South Korea, Taiwan, and Singapore implemented industrial policies that contributed to their development. But the conditions were specific: competent bureaucracies insulated from lobbying, performance-based support with genuine conditionality, and an export-orientation test that imposed market discipline. These conditions are rare and particularly unlikely in countries with weak governance.

**Don't:** Assume that the alternative to industrial policy is laissez-faire.
**Why:** Bhagwati supports extensive government investment in public goods — education, infrastructure, rule of law, macroeconomic stability. The critique is of selective industry promotion, not of government economic activity in general.
