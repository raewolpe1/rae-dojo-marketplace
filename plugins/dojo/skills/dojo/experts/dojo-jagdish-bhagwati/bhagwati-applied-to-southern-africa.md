# Bhagwati Applied to Southern Africa

## When to Use
- When applying Bhagwati's trade frameworks to South African or Southern African policy decisions.
- When advising on SACU, AfCFTA, the EU EPA, or South Africa's broader trade strategy.
- When analysing why South Africa's trade policy has produced mixed development outcomes.

## Fails When
- **You apply Bhagwati's prescriptions mechanically without accounting for South Africa's structural unemployment, inequality, and political economy.** The principles transfer; the application requires context.
- **You ignore that South Africa already liberalised significantly in the 1990s.** The policy debate is not about whether to liberalise from autarky but about whether the current mix of moderate tariffs, industrial policy instruments, and trade agreements is optimal.

## Core Concept
South Africa presents a complex case for Bhagwati's framework. The country liberalised substantially after 1994, reducing average tariffs from approximately 28 per cent to under 8 per cent. It joined the WTO, negotiated the EU Trade, Development and Cooperation Agreement (subsequently the EPA), and participated in SADC integration. By Bhagwati's logic, this liberalisation should have generated growth, employment, and poverty reduction through competitive pressure, export expansion, and integration into global value chains.

The results have been mixed. Trade openness contributed to some successes: the automotive industry became a genuine exporter (though dependent on incentives); the wine, fruit, and processed food sectors expanded global market share; and consumer prices fell in sectors where import competition increased. But the aggregate outcomes fell short of the East Asian model that Bhagwati celebrates. Growth has been modest (averaging 2–3 per cent since 1994), unemployment has increased (from approximately 20 per cent to over 30 per cent on the expanded definition), and the manufacturing share of GDP has declined.

A Bhagwati analysis would identify several explanations that do not indict trade liberalisation itself. First, South Africa's binding constraints are largely domestic: unreliable electricity (post-2007 load-shedding), inefficient logistics (Transnet rail and port performance), rigid labour market regulation (extension of bargaining council agreements, inflexible hiring and firing), and skills deficits (education quality crisis, particularly in maths and science). These domestic constraints would suppress economic performance under any trade regime. Second, South Africa's trade liberalisation was accompanied by a rise in the real exchange rate driven by commodity prices and capital inflows, which reduced the competitiveness of non-commodity tradeable sectors — a version of Dutch disease that is a macroeconomic problem, not a trade policy problem. Third, the industrial policy instruments retained or introduced after liberalisation (automotive incentives, clothing tariffs, localisation requirements) create rents, generate DUP activities, and protect sectors that may not have long-term comparative advantage.

Bhagwati's prescription for South Africa would focus on domestic reform: fix electricity supply, improve port and rail efficiency, reform labour market regulation to reduce the cost of formal employment, dramatically improve education quality, and simplify the regulatory environment. These horizontal reforms would benefit all sectors and would be far more effective than the vertical industrial policies currently in place. Trade policy should continue to move toward lower, more uniform tariffs, while AfCFTA should be implemented with liberal rules of origin and genuine services liberalisation.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose whether South Africa's economic underperformance is caused by trade openness or by domestic constraints.** The evidence overwhelmingly points to domestic constraints — Bhagwati's framework correctly identifies that the problem is not too much trade but too little domestic reform.
2. **Evaluate South Africa's industrial policy instruments against their costs, including DUP activities.** The automotive programme, clothing tariffs, and localisation requirements should be assessed for total cost (fiscal, consumer, DUP) per job sustained.
3. **Advise on AfCFTA design that maximises trade creation and minimises diversion.** South Africa's interest in AfCFTA is in accessing continental markets for its services and manufactured exports, which requires liberal rules of origin and genuine services liberalisation.

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's Economic Development Department proposes expanding localisation requirements to additional sectors, arguing that local procurement drives industrialisation and creates jobs.
**Application:** Bhagwati's framework identifies localisation as a form of import substitution that generates concentrated benefits for qualifying firms and dispersed costs across the economy. For each sector proposed for localisation, the analysis should estimate: the price increase for government and private purchasers; the number of jobs created or sustained in the qualifying sector; the number of jobs reduced in sectors that face higher input costs; the DUP costs (compliance, lobbying, circumvention); and the dynamic cost of shielding firms from international competition. In South Africa's experience, localisation requirements have produced modest job creation at high cost per job, have sometimes reduced the quality of procured goods, and have created rent-seeking opportunities (firms restructuring to meet localisation thresholds without genuine domestic value addition). Bhagwati's alternative: invest the resources currently spent on administering and complying with localisation requirements into the horizontal constraints that actually determine competitiveness — electricity reliability, logistics efficiency, skills, and regulatory simplification. These investments would create a broadly competitive economy rather than artificial enclaves of protected production.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Blame trade liberalisation for South Africa's unemployment crisis.
**Why:** South Africa's unemployment rate has risen despite liberalisation, but the causal factors are primarily domestic: labour market rigidity, skills mismatches, infrastructure deterioration, and the structural shift from mining and manufacturing to services. Reversing liberalisation would raise consumer prices and reduce competitiveness without creating employment.

**Don't:** Ignore the political economy of South African trade policy.
**Why:** COSATU's opposition to liberalisation, industry associations' lobbying for protection, and the ANC alliance's internal politics shape trade policy outcomes. Any feasible reform strategy must navigate these political realities, not just assert economic principles.
