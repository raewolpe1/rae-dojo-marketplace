# Trade and Environment

## When to Use
- When analysing proposals for carbon border adjustments, environmental conditionality in trade, or green trade barriers.
- When applying the theory of distortions and welfare to environmental problems.
- When advising on whether trade restrictions are appropriate instruments for environmental protection.

## Fails When
- **You present Bhagwati as opposing environmental protection.** He supports environmental protection through appropriate instruments — environmental taxes, regulations, multilateral environmental agreements — not through trade restrictions.
- **You ignore that environmental externalities can justify policy intervention even in Bhagwati's framework.** The question is whether trade policy is the right instrument, not whether the externality exists.

## Core Concept
Bhagwati's approach to trade and environment applies the same analytical framework as his approach to trade and labour standards: the theory of distortions and welfare. Environmental degradation is a real problem that deserves policy attention. But trade policy is almost never the first-best instrument for addressing environmental problems.

If the problem is a production externality — a factory that pollutes a river — the first-best instrument is a production tax or emission standard that targets the pollution directly. A tariff on the factory's output addresses the externality indirectly and creates additional distortions: it raises consumer prices, reduces output from both polluting and non-polluting domestic firms (by appreciating the real exchange rate), and creates DUP incentives. The environmental tax addresses the externality precisely; the tariff addresses it clumsily.

Bhagwati extends this analysis to the most prominent contemporary trade-environment debate: carbon border adjustment mechanisms (CBAMs). The EU's CBAM imposes a carbon price on imports from countries without equivalent carbon pricing, ostensibly to prevent "carbon leakage" (firms relocating to countries with lower carbon costs). Bhagwati's framework identifies this as a sophisticated form of trade restriction that uses environmental language. The first-best instrument for climate change is a global carbon price — negotiated multilaterally through the UNFCCC process. Unilateral border adjustments are second-best at best: they create trade disputes, impose costs disproportionately on developing countries (which have lower carbon prices because they have lower incomes and lower historical emissions), and risk retaliation that fragments the trading system.

Bhagwati also challenges the "race to the bottom" hypothesis — the claim that trade competition forces countries to lower environmental standards to remain competitive. The empirical evidence is weak: countries that trade more tend to have stronger environmental regulations (because trade generates the income that funds environmental protection), and the pollution haven effect (firms relocating to countries with lower standards) is empirically small relative to other location determinants.

## How to Apply
1. **Apply the theory of distortions and welfare to environmental trade proposals.** Is trade policy the first-best instrument? If the environmental problem is domestic, a domestic instrument is superior.
2. **Evaluate carbon border adjustments against their stated objectives.** Are they primarily motivated by environmental protection or by competitive concerns? The answer determines whether they are legitimate policy or disguised protectionism.
3. **Support multilateral environmental agreements rather than unilateral trade measures.** Global environmental problems require global solutions; unilateral trade restrictions create conflict without solving the underlying problem.

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa faces the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, which will impose a carbon price on South African exports of steel, aluminium, cement, and fertiliser to the EU. South Africa's carbon tax is lower than the EU's carbon price, creating a CBAM liability for South African exporters.
**Application:** Bhagwati's framework identifies the CBAM as a second-best instrument with distributional consequences that disproportionately burden developing countries. South Africa's carbon intensity reflects its energy mix (coal-dependent electricity), its development level, and its historical emissions (which are a fraction of the EU's cumulative emissions). Imposing a EU-level carbon price on South African exports effectively requires South Africa to adopt EU climate policy as a condition of market access — a form of conditionality that Bhagwati would critique on the same grounds as labour standards conditionality. The first-best approach: negotiate a global carbon price through the UNFCCC that reflects common but differentiated responsibilities, with transfers from wealthy to developing countries to support energy transition. The CBAM as implemented taxes developing country exports for the developed world's failure to achieve a multilateral agreement. South Africa's response should be threefold: accelerate domestic energy transition (which is warranted on economic grounds regardless of CBAM); negotiate CBAM credit for South Africa's domestic carbon tax and renewable energy investments; and challenge CBAM at the WTO if its application discriminates against developing country exporters.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Argue that environmental concerns should never influence trade policy.
**Why:** Bhagwati acknowledges that transboundary environmental externalities (climate change, ozone depletion) may justify coordinated trade measures — but through multilateral agreement, not through unilateral border adjustments.

**Don't:** Accept environmental trade measures at face value without examining the protectionist interests behind them.
**Why:** Industries in wealthy countries have learned that environmental arguments are more politically palatable than straightforward demands for protection. The same competitive interests that once lobbied for tariffs now lobby for environmental trade restrictions.
