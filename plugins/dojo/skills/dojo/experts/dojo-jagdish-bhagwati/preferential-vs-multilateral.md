# Preferential Versus Multilateral Trade

## When to Use
- When evaluating bilateral or regional trade agreements.
- When advising on whether a country should prioritise preferential or multilateral trade liberalisation.
- When analysing the proliferation of FTAs and their systemic effects.

## Fails When
- **You present all preferential agreements as equally harmful.** Bhagwati's analysis distinguishes between agreements that are building blocks toward multilateral liberalisation and those that are stumbling blocks that fragment the system.
- **You ignore the trade diversion problem.** Many analysts focus on trade creation from preferential agreements while ignoring trade diversion — the redirection of imports from efficient non-member suppliers to less efficient member suppliers.

## Core Concept
Bhagwati's critique of preferential trade agreements (PTAs) — which he provocatively labels "preferential" rather than "free" trade agreements to emphasise that they discriminate rather than liberalise — is one of his most influential and politically inconvenient arguments.

The core analytical problem is trade diversion. When Country A grants preferential access to Country B, imports previously sourced from the lowest-cost global supplier (Country C, which does not have preferential access) may shift to Country B (which is higher-cost but now pays no tariff). The consumer in Country A pays the same or higher price; Country B's less efficient producers gain a market they would not win on competitive merit; and Country C's efficient producers lose a market they deserve. Trade diversion reduces global welfare even though it increases bilateral trade between the agreement partners.

Bhagwati's "spaghetti bowl" metaphor captures the systemic problem of PTA proliferation. Each agreement has different rules of origin, different tariff schedules, different standards, and different dispute mechanisms. An exporter shipping to multiple markets must comply with a different set of rules for each — maintaining different production lines, sourcing certificates, and documentation. The compliance costs disproportionately burden small firms and developing country exporters who lack the administrative capacity to navigate the complexity.

The political economy problem compounds the analytical one. Negotiating bilateral agreements absorbs diplomatic and technical resources that might be directed toward multilateral liberalisation. Each bilateral deal creates vested interests — firms that benefit from preferential access — that oppose multilateral MFN liberalisation because it would erode their preference margin. The proliferation of PTAs can thus undermine rather than support multilateral progress.

Bhagwati's constructive position is that preferential agreements are acceptable when they meet specific criteria: liberal rules of origin that minimise trade diversion; open accession clauses that allow new members; consistency with WTO disciplines; and a demonstrated commitment by the parties to multilateral liberalisation alongside the preferential arrangement.

## How to Apply
1. **When evaluating any proposed FTA, estimate trade diversion alongside trade creation.** If trade diversion is substantial, the agreement may reduce welfare for the smaller partner.
2. **Examine the rules of origin.** Restrictive rules of origin that force firms to source from higher-cost member-country suppliers are hidden protectionism within a nominal free trade agreement.
3. **Assess whether the agreement complements or substitutes for multilateral engagement.** If diplomatic resources are being diverted from WTO to bilateral negotiations, the systemic effect may be negative.

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa is a member of SACU (with Botswana, Lesotho, Namibia, eSwatini), SADC FTA, the SADC-EU EPA, and AfCFTA. A proposal emerges for a bilateral FTA with India.
**Application:** Bhagwati would assess the proposal against several criteria. First, trade diversion risk: would preferential access for Indian goods divert South African imports from more efficient suppliers (China, the EU, ASEAN) to India? If India is not the lowest-cost supplier for the relevant products, the preference creates diversion that raises costs for South African consumers and firms. Second, spaghetti bowl complexity: adding another bilateral agreement to South Africa's already complex web of overlapping arrangements increases compliance costs for South African exporters and importers. Each new set of rules of origin, product schedules, and administrative requirements adds to the burden. Third, AfCFTA interaction: does a bilateral India deal undermine or complement AfCFTA? If India-South Africa preferences are deeper than AfCFTA preferences, South African firms may source from India rather than from continental partners, undermining the continental integration objective. Fourth, multilateral alternative: would the objectives of the India deal be better achieved through multilateral WTO liberalisation or through India's accession to broader plurilateral arrangements? Bhagwati's advice would be cautious: if the deal proceeds, insist on liberal rules of origin, consistency with AfCFTA disciplines, and an open architecture that allows other partners to accede on equivalent terms.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Oppose all preferential agreements in principle.
**Why:** Bhagwati accepts that in a world where multilateral progress is slow, pragmatic regional arrangements may be necessary. The question is whether they are designed as building blocks or stumbling blocks.

**Don't:** Evaluate preferential agreements solely on trade creation.
**Why:** The welfare effect depends on the balance of trade creation and trade diversion. An agreement that creates substantial bilateral trade while diverting trade from more efficient sources may reduce total welfare.
