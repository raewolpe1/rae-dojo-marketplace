# Deconstruction

## When to Use

- When you need to expose the hidden assumptions, hierarchies, and exclusions in a document, programme design, or theory of change
- When a framework presents itself as natural, neutral, or comprehensive and you suspect it is none of these things
- When you want to understand why a system is failing in ways that its own logic cannot explain

## Core Concept

Deconstruction is not destruction. It is not tearing things down, and it is not nihilism. Deconstruction is the patient, paradoxical work of showing how a text, a system, or a framework undermines its own foundations — how it depends upon what it excludes, how the very concepts it deploys contain the seeds of their own instability.

Every text, every programme document, every theory of change relies on certain assumptions that it presents as self-evident. Deconstruction asks: what must this text suppress in order to say what it says? What has been excluded so that the framework can appear coherent? What binary opposition — modern/traditional, expert/local, formal/informal — structures the argument, and which term has been privileged at the expense of the other?

For development practice, deconstruction is not an academic exercise. It is a reading practice that reveals the political work hidden inside apparently technical documents. When an evaluation framework claims to be "comprehensive," deconstruction asks what it leaves out. When a theory of change presents a linear causal pathway, deconstruction traces the moments where the text acknowledges — in its footnotes, its caveats, its "limitations" sections — that the linearity breaks down.

Crucially, deconstruction is affirmative. It does not expose contradictions in order to dismiss the text. It exposes contradictions in order to open space for what the text has excluded — the voices, the knowledge systems, the possibilities that the framework could not accommodate. Deconstruction says: this framework is not wrong, but it is incomplete in ways it cannot acknowledge, and that incompleteness has consequences.

The most important thing to understand about deconstruction is that it is not a method applied from the outside. The text deconstructs itself. The reader's task is to attend to the moments where it does so — where the argument stumbles, where the metaphor contradicts the thesis, where the footnote undermines the main text.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the governing framework.**
   > "Before you deconstruct, you must read generously. Understand the text on its own terms. What is it trying to do? What logic does it follow? What does it promise? Only when you understand what the text claims can you attend to what it suppresses in making that claim."

2. **Find the binary opposition.**
   > "Every text is structured by binary oppositions — pairs of concepts where one is privileged and the other subordinated. Modern/traditional. Quantitative/qualitative. Impact/process. Name the binary. Name the hierarchy. Then ask: what does the subordinated term contribute that the text cannot acknowledge?"

3. **Trace the moments of instability.**
   > "Look for the places where the text contradicts itself — not through carelessness, but necessarily. Where does the argument require an exception? Where does the footnote complicate the main claim? Where does the word 'merely' or 'just' or 'also' signal something the text is anxious about? These are the deconstructive openings."

4. **Open space for the excluded.**
   > "Deconstruction is not complete until you have articulated what the text excludes and why that exclusion matters. The excluded term is not simply absent — it is actively suppressed, and its suppression has consequences for the programme, the policy, the practice. Name those consequences. That is the affirmative move."

## Examples

**Situation:** A regional development agency has produced a strategy document that organises its work around the distinction between "evidence-based" and "non-evidence-based" approaches, committing to fund only the former.

**Application:** A deconstructive reading of this document would begin by noting that the term "evidence-based" appears natural and uncontroversial — who would argue against evidence? But the binary evidence-based/non-evidence-based conceals a hierarchy of knowledge. "Evidence" here means data produced through specific methodological protocols — randomised controlled trials, quasi-experimental designs, systematic reviews. Knowledge produced through community experience, practitioner judgement, indigenous practices, or historical memory is classified as "non-evidence" — not because it lacks empirical grounding, but because it was produced outside the recognised protocols. The strategy document depends on this classification to justify its funding decisions, but the classification is doing political work: it determines whose knowledge counts and whose does not. A deconstructive reading would trace the moments where the document itself acknowledges the limits of its evidence hierarchy — perhaps in a section on "context sensitivity" or "local adaptation" — and show that these supplements reveal the incompleteness of the governing framework. The evidence/non-evidence binary deconstructs itself the moment the document admits that evidence alone is insufficient.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Don't** use deconstruction as a synonym for criticism or fault-finding.
  - **Why:** Deconstruction is not about showing that a text is bad or wrong. It is about showing that a text cannot do what it claims to do — which is a different and more interesting observation. Reducing deconstruction to criticism domesticates it and loses its philosophical force.

- **Don't** conclude a deconstructive reading by offering a "better" framework that claims to solve the problems you have identified.
  - **Why:** A deconstructive reading that ends with a replacement framework has not understood deconstruction. The replacement framework will have its own exclusions, its own hierarchies, its own supplements. The point is not to replace one framework with another but to inhabit the tension — and to act within it, knowing that every action is compromised. Closure is not the goal.
