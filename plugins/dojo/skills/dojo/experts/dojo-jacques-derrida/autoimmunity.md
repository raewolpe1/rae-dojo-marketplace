# Autoimmunity

## When to Use

- When a system, institution, or programme is undermining itself through the very mechanisms it has deployed to protect or improve itself
- When quality assurance processes are destroying quality, accountability mechanisms are preventing accountability, or protection systems are creating the vulnerabilities they were designed to prevent
- When an institutional crisis is being worsened by the crisis response

## Core Concept

Autoimmunity is Derrida's concept for systems that destroy themselves through their own defence mechanisms — borrowing from immunology, where the immune system attacks the body it is meant to protect. In *Rogues*, Derrida applies this to democracy: democracy's efforts to defend itself against anti-democratic threats can themselves become anti-democratic. The security state that suspends civil liberties to protect democracy undermines the democracy it claims to defend.

The concept is devastatingly applicable to development practice. Consider the ubiquitous results framework. It was designed to ensure accountability, to demonstrate that programmes produce outcomes, to protect against waste and inefficiency. But the results framework can become autoimmune: the demand for measurable results can drive programmes toward easily measurable but trivial outcomes, distorting programme design away from the complex, long-term, hard-to-measure changes that actually matter. The accountability mechanism undermines the effectiveness it was designed to ensure.

Or consider the compliance apparatus of international development: procurement rules, safeguarding policies, fiduciary controls, reporting requirements. Each was created to protect against a real risk. Collectively, they can become an autoimmune disorder — consuming so much institutional energy, creating so many administrative burdens, and generating so much risk aversion that the programme cannot function. The protection mechanisms generate the institutional paralysis they were designed to prevent.

Autoimmunity is not simply "unintended consequences." It is structurally specific: the mechanism of defence is the mechanism of destruction. The system does not fail despite its protections — it fails because of them. This specificity is what makes the concept analytically powerful. It directs attention to the internal logic of the system rather than to external threats.

The autoimmune crisis is also the moment of potential transformation. When a system's defences turn against it, the system cannot continue as before. The crisis forces a confrontation with the system's own logic — a confrontation that may produce genuine change or may produce a doubling-down on the very mechanisms that caused the crisis. The autoimmune moment is therefore politically charged: it is the point where the system's future is genuinely open.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify the immune system.**
   > "Map the mechanisms that the institution or programme has deployed to protect itself: quality assurance, risk management, compliance, monitoring, evaluation, reporting. These are the immune system. Now ask: which of these mechanisms are producing effects contrary to their stated purpose? Where is quality assurance destroying quality? Where is risk management creating risk? Where is monitoring undermining the activities being monitored?"

2. **Trace the autoimmune logic.**
   > "Do not settle for 'unintended consequences.' Trace the specific mechanism through which the defence becomes the pathology. The results framework demands measurable outcomes. Measurable outcomes are simpler and more short-term than the actual changes sought. Programmes optimise for measurable outcomes at the expense of actual change. The accountability mechanism produces the distortion it was designed to prevent. Name each step in the chain."

3. **Resist the reflexive response.**
   > "When the autoimmune crisis is diagnosed, the institutional reflex is to prescribe more of the same: more oversight, tighter controls, additional reporting. But if the immune system is the pathology, strengthening the immune system will worsen the disease. Ask instead: what would it mean to reduce the defence mechanisms? What risks would need to be genuinely accepted rather than managed?"

4. **Locate the political opening.**
   > "The autoimmune crisis is a moment of genuine political possibility — the system's logic has broken down and the future is open. This is the moment to ask: what kind of system would not need to defend itself in ways that destroy it? The answer may require fundamentally rethinking the system's design rather than repairing its defences."

## Examples

**Situation:** A bilateral donor's anti-corruption measures have become so extensive that implementing partners spend 40% of their staff time on compliance, procurement processes take six months, and programme activities are delayed by years. The donor's own evaluation finds that the anti-corruption measures have not reduced corruption but have shifted it into areas that the measures do not cover — while dramatically reducing programme effectiveness.

**Application:** This is a textbook autoimmune crisis. The anti-corruption measures are the immune system — designed to protect programme resources from misuse. But the immune system has turned against the body: the compliance burden is destroying programme effectiveness more efficiently than corruption ever did. Staff who should be implementing programmes are filling in procurement forms. Activities that could respond to urgent needs are delayed by approval processes. And the corruption has not been eliminated — it has adapted, moving into the spaces the compliance framework cannot see. The system is destroying itself through its own defences. The institutional reflex will be to respond with more compliance: tighten the procurement rules, add another layer of approval, hire more compliance officers. But this is the autoimmune spiral — more immunity, more autoimmunity. A genuinely different response would require the donor to accept a level of risk — to acknowledge that perfect control is not only impossible but that the pursuit of perfect control is more damaging than the corruption it seeks to prevent. This is politically very difficult because it requires an institution to admit that its own protective mechanisms have become pathological. But the autoimmune crisis is precisely the moment where such admission becomes possible — because the alternative is to continue destroying the programme in the name of protecting it.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Don't** use autoimmunity as a blanket argument against all accountability, compliance, or quality assurance.
  - **Why:** Not every accountability mechanism is autoimmune. Some reporting requirements genuinely improve programmes. Some procurement rules genuinely prevent corruption. The concept of autoimmunity applies specifically when the defence mechanism has become the pathology — when the cure is worse than the disease. Demonstrating this requires specific analysis, not blanket critique.

- **Don't** offer a simple "solution" to the autoimmune crisis.
  - **Why:** The temptation is to say "just reduce the compliance burden." But the autoimmune crisis is structural — the system's logic generates the pathology. A simple reduction of compliance may expose the programme to the original risks that the compliance system was designed to address. The honest response is to acknowledge the aporia: the system needs defence mechanisms that will tend toward autoimmunity. The question is not how to eliminate the tendency but how to recognise and manage it — which requires ongoing vigilance, not a one-time fix.
