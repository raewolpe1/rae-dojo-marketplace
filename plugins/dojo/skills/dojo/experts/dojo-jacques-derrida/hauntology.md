# Hauntology

## When to Use

- When a new programme is being designed in a context where previous interventions have failed, and the failures are shaping community responses, institutional behaviours, or political dynamics in ways that the new programme's design does not acknowledge
- When something excluded from a framework — a population, a perspective, an approach — keeps returning to disrupt implementation
- When institutional memory (or its absence) is affecting programme effectiveness

## Core Concept

In *Specters of Marx*, Derrida coined the term "hauntology" — a play on "ontology" (the study of what is) — to name the way that what is absent, excluded, or dead continues to haunt the present. The present is never fully present to itself. It is always inhabited by specters — the ghosts of what came before, what was excluded, what failed, what was promised and not delivered.

For development practice, hauntology is perhaps Derrida's most immediately useful concept. Every new programme is haunted by the specters of previous interventions. The community that has been promised "sustainable livelihoods" four times by four different agencies does not encounter the fifth programme as a blank slate. The specters of the previous four — their promises, their failures, their abandoned infrastructure, their unfinished trainings — haunt every meeting, every needs assessment, every participation exercise. The new programme may not know about its predecessors, but the community does. The ghosts are in the room.

Institutional hauntology is equally powerful. Development organisations are haunted by their own suppressed histories — the programmes that failed but were reported as successes, the approaches that were abandoned without acknowledgement, the staff who raised concerns and were silenced. These specters do not disappear when they are suppressed. They return — as institutional cynicism, as resistance to new approaches, as the gap between official narratives and lived experience.

The specter is not simply the past. It is a figure of the untimely — something that belongs to no particular time, that disrupts the linear temporality of the programme cycle. The specter of a failed structural adjustment programme from the 1990s can appear in a community's response to a market-based intervention in 2026. The specter of a colonial-era extraction project can haunt a natural resource governance programme. Specters do not respect chronology.

Derrida insists that learning to live justly means learning to live with specters — not exorcising them (that is impossible) but acknowledging them, speaking with them, allowing them to interrupt the confident forward march of the new programme. A programme that does not know its ghosts is condemned to be haunted by them unconsciously. A programme that acknowledges its ghosts can at least begin to negotiate with the past it inherits.

## How to Apply

1. **Conduct a ghost audit.**
   > "Before designing a new intervention, ask: what has been tried here before? What was promised? What was delivered? What was abandoned? Map the specters — the previous programmes, the broken promises, the unfinished projects. These ghosts are as real as any baseline data, and they shape community responses as powerfully as any current incentive."

2. **Listen for the haunting.**
   > "In community consultations, participation exercises, and stakeholder meetings, listen not only for what people say but for the specters that inhabit their speech. When a community leader says 'we have heard this before,' they are naming a specter. When staff exhibit cynicism about a new initiative, they are channelling institutional ghosts. The haunting is diagnostic — it tells you what the present has not resolved."

3. **Acknowledge the inheritance.**
   > "Every programme inherits a context shaped by what came before. The honest move is to name the inheritance — to say, in the programme document, in the theory of change, in the inception workshop: 'we know that previous interventions in this space have failed, and we know that those failures shape how communities and institutions respond to us. Here is how we plan to engage with that inheritance rather than pretending it does not exist.'"

4. **Resist the exorcism.**
   > "The institutional reflex is to exorcise the ghosts — to draw a clean line between the failed past and the promising future. 'We are different from the previous programme.' 'We have learned from past mistakes.' 'This time will be different.' These claims are the development sector's equivalent of ghost-busting, and they never work. The specters return. The alternative is to live with them — to design programmes that acknowledge their hauntedness rather than claiming a fresh start that no programme can honestly make."

## Examples

**Situation:** A new youth employment programme is being launched in a region where three similar programmes have operated in the past decade. Each promised job creation, each trained hundreds of young people, and each ended when its funding cycle concluded, leaving trainees with certificates but no jobs. The new programme's design team has not reviewed the previous programmes and is presenting their initiative as innovative.

**Application:** The new programme is haunted by at least three specters, and its refusal to acknowledge them is its most dangerous feature. Every young person who attends the new programme's recruitment event carries the ghosts of previous programmes — the certificates gathering dust, the promised "linkages to employers" that never materialised, the excitement that turned to cynicism. The programme's claim to innovation is the most symptomatic moment: it performs a clean break with the past that the community knows is impossible. A hauntologically informed approach would begin by naming the ghosts. What did the previous programmes promise? Why did they fail? Not in the shallow sense of "lessons learned" (the specters resist domestication) but in the structural sense: what about the design of youth employment programmes in this context makes them fail? Is the failure in the programmes or in the economic conditions they cannot change? The specters are asking this question, whether the programme team hears them or not. Acknowledging the ghosts would not guarantee success — nothing can — but it would create the conditions for honesty: "we are the fourth programme to promise you jobs. Here is why we think we might be different, and here is what we will do differently when we discover that we are not as different as we hoped."

## Anti-Patterns

- **Don't** use hauntology to justify paralysis or fatalism.
  - **Why:** The fact that every present is haunted by past failures does not mean that action is futile. Derrida is not a fatalist. The point is not that new programmes will inevitably fail because they are haunted, but that programmes which refuse to engage with their ghosts are more likely to reproduce the failures they inherit. Acknowledging the specters is the condition of acting differently, not a reason to stop acting.

- **Don't** reduce the specters to "lessons learned."
  - **Why:** The "lessons learned" genre is the development sector's preferred method of exorcism — it converts the ghost into a manageable "lesson" that can be "applied" to the new programme. But specters resist this domestication. A failed programme does not leave behind a lesson — it leaves behind a haunting: disappointed communities, cynical staff, abandoned infrastructure, broken trust. These do not become "lessons" by being written into a report. They remain ghosts that must be lived with, not managed.
