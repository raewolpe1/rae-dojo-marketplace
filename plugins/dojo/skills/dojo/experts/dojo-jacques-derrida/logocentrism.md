# Logocentrism

## When to Use

- When a framework, methodology, or institutional practice presents itself as natural, self-evident, or the only rational approach — and you want to examine that claim
- When the authority of a particular form of knowledge (quantitative data, expert opinion, scientific evidence) is being used to silence other forms of knowledge
- When you want to understand the deep structure of Western knowledge systems and how they shape development practice

## Core Concept

Logocentrism is Derrida's name for the deep structure of Western thought: the privileging of presence over absence, speech over writing, identity over difference, the natural over the constructed. The "logos" — reason, word, logic — is imagined as a point of full presence: a meaning that is simply there, transparent, self-evident, requiring no interpretation.

Derrida shows that this imagined presence is an illusion. There is no concept that is simply present to itself, no meaning that requires no interpretation, no knowledge that exists independent of the systems of signs, conventions, and power relations through which it is produced. Logocentrism is the refusal to acknowledge this — the insistence that somewhere, beneath all the interpretation, there is a bedrock of pure presence, pure meaning, pure truth.

For development practice, logocentrism manifests as the unquestioned authority of certain forms of knowledge. "The data shows" is a logocentric claim — it presents data as self-evident, as speaking for itself, as requiring no interpretation. But data never speaks for itself. Data is produced through methodological choices (what to measure, how to measure, whom to ask), analytical frameworks (what counts as significant, what patterns to look for), and institutional interests (what the funder wants to know, what the evaluator is equipped to find). The claim that "the data shows" conceals all of this production behind the appearance of presence.

The privileging of quantitative over qualitative knowledge is a logocentric operation. Numbers appear more present, more self-evident, more transparent than narratives or interpretations. They seem to require less mediation — the number is just there, unambiguous. But quantitative data is the most mediated form of knowledge: every number is the product of a chain of decisions about categorisation, measurement, sampling, and analysis. The appearance of immediacy is precisely what conceals the mediation.

Similarly, the privileging of expert over local knowledge is logocentric. Expert knowledge presents itself as universal, objective, and authoritative — as logos. Local knowledge is positioned as particular, subjective, and anecdotal — as the absence of logos. But expert knowledge is always produced somewhere, by someone, within institutional structures that shape what can be known. Its universality is a claim, not a fact.

Deconstructing logocentrism does not mean rejecting reason, data, or expertise. It means exposing the hidden operations through which certain forms of knowledge claim the status of pure presence — and asking who benefits from that claim.

## How to Apply

1. **Find the claim of self-evidence.**
   > "When a document says 'it is clear that,' 'obviously,' 'the evidence demonstrates,' or 'best practice requires,' it is making a logocentric move — presenting a produced, contested, interpretive claim as if it were self-evident. Flag these moments. They are where the text's authority claims are most exposed."

2. **Trace the production.**
   > "For every claim that presents itself as self-evident, trace how it was produced. Who collected the data? What methodology was used? What questions were asked and not asked? What framework determined what counted as relevant? The production history is not a flaw — it is the reality of all knowledge. Concealing it is the logocentric operation."

3. **Name the hierarchy of knowledge.**
   > "Every programme operates with an implicit hierarchy of knowledge. RCT evidence at the top, case studies in the middle, community testimony at the bottom. Quantitative on the left, qualitative on the right. Name the hierarchy. Ask who established it. Ask whose knowledge is rendered invisible by it. The hierarchy is not a neutral methodological choice — it is a political arrangement."

4. **Restore the mediation.**
   > "When knowledge is presented as immediate — 'the facts speak for themselves,' 'the numbers are clear' — restore the layers of mediation that produced it. What decisions were made? What alternatives were excluded? What interpretive framework organises the presentation? Making the mediation visible does not invalidate the knowledge — it makes it honestly available for interrogation and debate."

## Examples

**Situation:** A development agency is preparing a country strategy based on a "rigorous evidence review" that synthesises findings from systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and randomised controlled trials. The evidence review is presented as the objective foundation for strategic decision-making — the bedrock upon which programme priorities should be built.

**Application:** The evidence review is a logocentric construction par excellence. It presents itself as pure presence — the evidence, simply there, speaking for itself, requiring only rational response. But a deconstructive reading reveals the layers of mediation concealed by the claim of rigour. The systematic reviews included certain studies and excluded others based on methodological criteria that themselves embody assumptions about what counts as valid knowledge. RCTs, which sit at the apex of the evidence hierarchy, are excellent at answering certain questions (does intervention X produce outcome Y in controlled conditions?) and incapable of answering others (why does the intervention work? for whom? in what conditions? what are the unintended consequences?). The meta-analyses aggregate findings across contexts, producing generalisations that may not apply to any particular context — including the country for which the strategy is being designed. None of this makes the evidence review useless. It makes it a particular kind of knowledge — produced by particular methods, answering particular questions, silencing particular alternatives. The logocentric move is not the evidence review itself but the claim that it provides an "objective foundation" — as if the foundation were not constructed, as if the decisions embedded in the methodology were not political, as if the hierarchy of evidence were natural rather than imposed.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Don't** use the critique of logocentrism to dismiss evidence, reason, or expertise.
  - **Why:** Derrida is not an irrationalist. Deconstructing logocentrism does not mean rejecting reason — it means rejecting reason's claim to be self-sufficient, self-evident, and free from the interpretive and institutional structures through which it is produced. The point is not to abandon evidence but to be honest about what evidence is: produced knowledge with particular strengths, particular limitations, and particular blind spots.

- **Don't** treat all knowledge claims as equally valid (or equally invalid).
  - **Why:** Deconstructing the hierarchy of knowledge does not mean flattening it into relativism. Some knowledge claims are better supported than others, more carefully produced, more honestly presented. The point is not that all claims are equivalent but that the hierarchy itself requires scrutiny — that the criteria by which we rank knowledge are themselves produced, historical, and contestable.
