# Derrida Applied to Practice

## When to Use

- When you have a concrete document in front of you — a theory of change, logframe, evaluation report, policy paper, strategy document — and want to subject it to deconstructive analysis
- When you want practical, repeatable techniques for reading development documents against the grain
- When you are preparing to review or comment on a document and want to go beyond standard quality assurance

## Core Concept

Deconstructive reading is not a vague philosophical attitude — it is a set of concrete practices that can be applied to any text. For development practitioners, the texts in question are programme documents, theories of change, evaluation reports, policy papers, logframes, and strategy documents. Each of these genres has its own conventions, its own rhetorical structures, its own mechanisms for producing authority and concealing assumptions.

The core practice is reading against the grain: reading not for what the text says but for what it must suppress, exclude, or render invisible in order to say what it says. Every text makes choices — about what to include and what to exclude, what to name and what to leave unnamed, what to present as self-evident and what to argue for. These choices are not neutral. They structure what the text can think and what it cannot, what it can see and what it renders invisible.

Practical deconstructive reading involves several concrete techniques. The binary audit identifies the oppositions that structure the text and exposes their hierarchies. The supplement scan locates what has been added as "merely additional" and asks what its supplementary status reveals about the framework's lacks. The silence analysis maps what the text does not say — the populations it does not mention, the approaches it does not consider, the failures it does not acknowledge. The authority trace follows the text's claims to authority — "the evidence shows," "best practice requires," "international standards dictate" — and asks who established the evidence, the practice, the standard, and whose knowledge was excluded in doing so.

These techniques are not destructive. They do not aim to discredit the document or its authors. They aim to make the document more honest — to surface the assumptions, hierarchies, and exclusions that every text necessarily contains. A theory of change that has been read deconstructively is not a worse theory of change — it is one whose limitations are visible, whose hierarchies are named, whose supplements are acknowledged. It is a text that knows what it does not know.

The development sector's documents are particularly rich sites for deconstructive reading because they combine several registers simultaneously: the technical (methodology, indicators, data), the political (resource allocation, priority-setting, stakeholder management), the ethical (beneficiary protection, do no harm, inclusivity), and the rhetorical (persuading donors, demonstrating rigour, performing expertise). Deconstructive reading attends to the tensions between these registers — the moments where the technical claim contradicts the ethical commitment, where the political calculation undermines the rhetorical performance, where the methodology excludes the very inclusion it claims.

## How to Apply

1. **Conduct the binary audit.**
   > "Read through the document and list every binary opposition it relies upon, whether explicit or implicit. Formal/informal. Supply/demand. Short-term/long-term. Evidence-based/opinion-based. Quantitative/qualitative. For each, mark which term is privileged. Then ask: what would the document look like if the hierarchy were reversed? The reversal is a thought experiment, not a recommendation — but it reveals the stakes of the original hierarchy."

2. **Run the supplement scan.**
   > "Identify everything positioned as supplementary, complementary, additional, or cross-cutting. Gender analysis mainstreamed across all components. Qualitative research to complement quantitative data. Local knowledge to supplement expert assessment. Community voice to enrich the evaluation findings. For each supplement, ask: if the main approach were sufficient, why does it need supplementing? What lack does the supplement reveal?"

3. **Perform the silence analysis.**
   > "Map the document's silences. What populations are not mentioned? What approaches are not considered? What risks are not named? What failures from previous interventions are not acknowledged? The silence analysis is not about what the document forgot — it is about what the document's framework makes structurally invisible. A poverty reduction programme that does not mention gender is not forgetful — it has a framework that structurally excludes gender. Name the structure, not the oversight."

4. **Follow the authority trace.**
   > "Track every claim to authority: 'research shows,' 'evidence demonstrates,' 'best practice dictates,' 'international standards require.' For each, ask: whose research? Whose evidence? Whose best practice? Whose standards? And ask the deeper question: what knowledge, what experience, what perspective was excluded from the production of the authority that the document invokes? Authority is never self-generating — it is always produced, and the production always involves exclusion."

## Examples

**Situation:** An evaluation report for a health systems strengthening programme contains the following sentence in its executive summary: "The programme successfully improved health service delivery through evidence-based capacity building of district health management teams, complemented by community engagement activities that enhanced demand for services."

**Application:** A deconstructive reading of this single sentence reveals a complex architecture of hierarchies and supplements. The binary audit identifies: evidence-based/non-evidence-based, supply (service delivery)/demand (community engagement), expert capacity (management teams)/community capacity (engagement activities). In each pair, the first term is privileged. The supplement scan reveals that community engagement is positioned as supplementary — it "complemented" the main activity and "enhanced demand" (the supply was already improved; the community's role is to demand what has been supplied). The silence analysis notes: what "evidence" informed the capacity building? Evidence about community health needs, or evidence about management practices? Where is the community's own knowledge of health in this sentence? It appears only as "demand" — the community consumes services but does not produce knowledge about health. The authority trace follows "evidence-based": evidence produced by whom, using what methods, asking what questions? The sentence performs a hierarchy of knowledge — expert evidence over community knowledge — while appearing merely to describe a programme's activities. A deconstructive reading does not rewrite the sentence — it reveals the political work the sentence performs in the act of appearing to be merely descriptive.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Don't** use deconstructive reading to ambush or humiliate document authors.
  - **Why:** Deconstructive reading reveals structural features of texts — hierarchies, exclusions, supplements — that are usually not the result of individual authorial intent. Every text has these features; they are inherent in the act of writing within institutional frameworks. The point is not to blame the author but to make the structure visible so it can be discussed and negotiated.

- **Don't** deconstruct a document without offering your reading back to its authors for dialogue.
  - **Why:** Deconstruction is not a one-way analytical weapon. Its value lies in opening conversation about what the text suppresses — which requires the participation of those who produced the text. A deconstructive reading that remains private is an intellectual exercise. A deconstructive reading shared with the document's authors is the beginning of institutional learning.
