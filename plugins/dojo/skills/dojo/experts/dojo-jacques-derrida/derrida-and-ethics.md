# Derrida and Ethics

## When to Use

- When you face an ethical situation in development practice that resists resolution by existing rules, codes of conduct, or ethical frameworks
- When you want to think about what responsibility genuinely means — not as compliance with a checklist but as an infinite demand
- When the gap between what your ethical framework requires and what the situation demands is visible and troubling

## Core Concept

For Derrida, ethics is not a system of rules to be applied. Ethics is the infinite demand of the other — a demand that exceeds every rule, every principle, every framework that might claim to satisfy it. This is not a rejection of ethical frameworks but a recognition that they are always insufficient. The moment you believe your ethical obligations are fully satisfied — that you have done everything required, checked every box, complied with every standard — you have stopped being ethical. Ethics begins precisely where the rules run out.

Drawing on Emmanuel Levinas, Derrida argues that responsibility is not a property of the self but a response to the other. The other's face — their vulnerability, their need, their demand — calls me to responsibility before I choose to be responsible. Responsibility is not something I decide; it is something that happens to me in the encounter with the other. And the demand is infinite: I can never fully discharge my responsibility to the other, because their need always exceeds my capacity to respond.

For development practice, this conception of ethics is both challenging and liberating. Challenging because it means that no "do no harm" framework, no safeguarding policy, no ethical review board can fully satisfy the ethical demand. There will always be a remainder — a dimension of harm, of exclusion, of injustice that the framework does not capture. The ethical obligation does not stop at the boundary of the compliance checklist.

But it is also liberating because it acknowledges what every honest development practitioner already knows: that the situations you face exceed the categories available to manage them. The family displaced by the infrastructure project is not merely a "safeguards case" to be processed. They are particular people with particular lives, and their demand on you is infinite — it exceeds every procedure, every compensation formula, every resettlement action plan. Acknowledging this excess is not a counsel of despair. It is the beginning of genuine ethical engagement — an engagement that does not pretend to have solved the problem but remains open to the demand.

Derrida also insists on the relationship between responsibility and the impossible. True responsibility requires a decision — but a decision worthy of the name cannot simply follow a rule. A decision that merely applies a pre-existing formula is not a decision; it is a programme. Genuine decision happens in the moment of undecidability — when the rules do not determine the outcome and you must decide without full knowledge, without certainty, without the comfort of a protocol that absolves you of responsibility.

## How to Apply

1. **Locate the excess.**
   > "When you have completed your ethical review, your safeguarding assessment, your do-no-harm analysis, ask: what remains? What ethical demand have you not addressed? What harm have you not prevented? What need have you not met? The excess is not a failure of the framework — it is the ethical demand itself. Name it, even if you cannot satisfy it."

2. **Resist ethical closure.**
   > "When an institution claims that its ethical obligations are 'fully addressed' by its policies and procedures, be sceptical. Ethical closure — the claim that the ethical demand has been fully met — is the most dangerous moment, because it licenses inattention. The claim that 'we have a robust safeguarding policy' can become the mechanism through which actual safeguarding failures are overlooked."

3. **Attend to the particular.**
   > "Ethics happens in the encounter with particular people in particular situations. General principles are necessary starting points, but the ethical moment is always the moment when the particular case exceeds the general rule. When the displaced family does not fit the compensation categories, when the beneficiary's needs exceed the programme's mandate, when the situation demands something the protocol does not cover — that is where ethics begins."

4. **Embrace undecidability.**
   > "The most important ethical decisions are the ones where the rules do not tell you what to do. Do not treat undecidability as a problem to be eliminated by better protocols. Undecidability is the condition of genuine decision. A decision that simply follows a rule is not a decision — it is a calculation. The ethical moment is the moment of decision without certainty."

## Examples

**Situation:** An evaluator conducting a programme evaluation in a conflict-affected area discovers evidence that programme resources have been diverted by a local partner to support one faction in the conflict. Reporting this finding will almost certainly result in the partner's defunding, which will end services to 5,000 beneficiaries who have no alternative provider. Not reporting it violates the evaluator's professional ethics and may enable the diversion to continue.

**Application:** This is a situation of genuine undecidability — and that is exactly what makes it an ethical situation in Derrida's sense. The evaluator cannot resolve this by applying a rule. The professional code says report; the humanitarian calculus says protect the beneficiaries; the political analysis says defunding the partner may worsen the conflict. No framework can adjudicate between these demands. The evaluator must decide, and the decision will be taken in the absence of certainty, in the awareness that whatever choice is made will involve harm. A Derridean ethics does not tell the evaluator what to do. It insists that the evaluator cannot escape responsibility by following a protocol — that the decision is theirs, with all its consequences, and that no institutional framework can absorb the weight of the choice. It also insists that the evaluator remain open to the ethical demands they cannot satisfy: the beneficiaries who will lose services, the partner staff who may face reprisals, the conflict dynamics that the decision will affect. The ethical demand is infinite, and the evaluator's capacity to respond is finite. That gap — between the infinite demand and the finite response — is not a failure. It is the structure of ethical life. The question is not whether the evaluator can make a perfect decision, but whether they can make an honest one — a decision that acknowledges what it sacrifices, that does not pretend to have resolved an irresolvable tension.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Don't** use the infinite demand of ethics as a justification for moral paralysis.
  - **Why:** The fact that no response is fully adequate does not mean that no response should be made. Derrida is not advocating inaction. He is advocating action in full awareness of its insufficiency — action that does not comfort itself with the illusion that it has fully met the ethical demand. You must decide, and you must act. The impossibility of a perfect decision does not excuse the refusal to decide at all.

- **Don't** reduce Derrida's ethics to relativism or the claim that all ethical positions are equally valid.
  - **Why:** The recognition that ethical frameworks are always insufficient is not the same as claiming that all ethical positions are equivalent. Some responses are more responsible than others, more attentive to the other, more honest about their limitations. The infinite demand does not flatten ethical distinctions — it deepens them.
