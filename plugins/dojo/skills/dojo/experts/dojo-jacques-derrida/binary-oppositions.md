# Binary Oppositions

## When to Use

- When a programme, policy, or framework is built on a binary distinction — formal/informal, modern/traditional, quantitative/qualitative, expert/local — and you want to examine what the distinction conceals
- When one side of a binary is being treated as the norm and the other as the deviation, the lack, or the problem to be solved
- When a categorical distinction is generating implementation problems because reality refuses to sort itself into two neat boxes

## Core Concept

Every binary opposition conceals a hierarchy. This is one of Derrida's most powerful and practical insights. In any binary pair — modern/traditional, formal/informal, expert/local, urban/rural, quantitative/qualitative — one term is privileged and the other subordinated. The privileged term is treated as the norm, the positive, the desirable. The subordinated term is defined by negation: it is what the privileged term is not. It is the lack, the absence, the not-yet.

But deconstruction does not stop at identifying the hierarchy. It takes three further steps. First, it reverses the hierarchy — showing that the subordinated term has its own logic, its own richness, its own necessity. The "informal" economy is not a failed version of the "formal" economy; it is a different economic logic with its own institutions, norms, and rationalities. Second, it shows mutual contamination — the privileged term depends upon the subordinated term and cannot exist without it. The "formal" economy relies on "informal" labour, networks, and flexibility. Third, it displaces the opposition altogether — showing that neither term is pure, that the boundary between them is unstable, and that the binary itself is a construction that serves particular interests.

For development practice, binary oppositions are everywhere: developed/developing, donor/recipient, supply/demand, output/outcome, participatory/extractive, top-down/bottom-up. Each of these binaries organises thinking, structures programme design, and determines what counts as success. And each conceals a hierarchy that privileges one mode of being, knowing, or acting over another.

The practical payoff of deconstructing binaries is not the elimination of all distinctions — you need categories to think and act — but the exposure of the political work that apparently neutral categories perform. When a programme distinguishes between "evidence-based" and "non-evidence-based" approaches, it is not merely describing a methodological difference. It is establishing a hierarchy of knowledge that determines whose knowledge counts, what gets funded, and who has authority. Naming the hierarchy is the first step toward negotiating it rather than being governed by it unconsciously.

## How to Apply

1. **Name the binary and the hierarchy.**
   > "Identify the binary opposition that structures the argument, the programme, or the policy. Name both terms. Then ask: which term is treated as the norm, the positive, the desirable? Which is defined by negation — as what the other is not? The hierarchy is the entry point. Without naming it, you cannot examine it."

2. **Reverse the hierarchy.**
   > "Give the subordinated term its own positive content. What does the 'informal' economy do that the 'formal' cannot? What does 'local knowledge' contain that 'expert knowledge' lacks? What does 'qualitative' research reveal that 'quantitative' misses? The reversal is not permanent — it is an analytical move that destabilises the original hierarchy."

3. **Show the mutual dependence.**
   > "Demonstrate that the privileged term depends on the subordinated term. The 'formal' economy cannot function without 'informal' labour. 'Expert' knowledge is built on 'local' knowledge it has extracted and repackaged. 'Quantitative' data depends on 'qualitative' judgements about what to count and how to categorise. The privileged term is not self-sufficient — it is supplemented by what it excludes."

4. **Displace the binary.**
   > "Having reversed the hierarchy and shown mutual dependence, ask: what would it look like to think beyond this binary? Not to collapse the distinction, but to find the concepts, practices, and institutional arrangements that the binary cannot accommodate. What falls between formal and informal? What modes of knowledge are neither expert nor local? The space between the binary is where the most interesting work happens."

## Examples

**Situation:** A livelihoods programme distinguishes between "productive" and "unproductive" economic activities. It funds productive activities (agriculture, manufacturing, trade) and discourages unproductive ones (ceremonies, social obligations, leisure). The theory of change assumes that shifting household resources from unproductive to productive activities will increase income and wellbeing.

**Application:** The productive/unproductive binary is doing remarkable political work while appearing merely descriptive. "Productive" is the privileged term — it connotes efficiency, rationality, modernity, economic growth. "Unproductive" is defined by negation — it is what does not produce economic value as the programme measures it. But a deconstructive reversal reveals that "unproductive" activities are deeply productive of something the programme's framework cannot measure: social capital, community cohesion, cultural identity, reciprocal obligation networks. These are not luxuries to be afforded after "real" production has occurred; they are the social infrastructure upon which all economic activity depends. The programme depends on the social networks it classifies as unproductive — farmer cooperation, market information sharing, mutual insurance — while undermining them by redirecting resources toward individually "productive" activities. The binary deconstructs itself: the "productive" activities cannot produce without the social substrate that the programme classifies as "unproductive." Displacing the binary would require a concept of productivity that includes the reproduction of social relations — not as a supplement to economic production, but as its condition of possibility.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Don't** treat all binary distinctions as equally problematic or equally worthy of deconstruction.
  - **Why:** Some distinctions are useful heuristics that do relatively little political damage. The question is not whether a binary exists but whether it is doing hidden hierarchical work with real consequences. Save deconstructive energy for the binaries that structure power, funding, and knowledge — not for every categorical distinction.

- **Don't** resolve the binary into a comfortable synthesis.
  - **Why:** The temptation after deconstructing a binary is to propose a "both/and" or a "continuum" that appears to transcend the opposition. But the synthesis often just reproduces the hierarchy at a higher level — the privileged term absorbs the subordinated term while appearing to include it. A genuine deconstructive move is not synthesis but the sustained inhabitation of the tension between the terms.
