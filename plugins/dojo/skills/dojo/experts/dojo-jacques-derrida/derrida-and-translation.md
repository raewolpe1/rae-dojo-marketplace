# Derrida and Translation

## When to Use

- When a programme, methodology, or framework is being transferred from one context to another — from one country to another, from headquarters to field, from policy to practice — and you want to think carefully about what happens in the transfer
- When "scaling" or "replication" is being proposed and you want to examine the assumption that something can be reproduced identically in a different context
- When the gap between design and implementation is attributed to "implementation failure" and you suspect the problem is structural rather than operational

## Core Concept

For Derrida, translation is never merely the reproduction of a meaning in another language. Translation is transformation. Something is always lost and something is always gained. The "original" text does not simply pass unchanged into the new language — it is remade, recontextualised, altered in ways that reveal aspects of the original that were invisible in its home context. And the receiving language is also changed by the act of translation — it is stretched, challenged, made to accommodate what it could not previously say.

The impossibility of perfect translation is not a failure — it is the condition of translation itself. If a perfect translation were possible, the two languages would be identical, and translation would be unnecessary. Translation exists because languages differ, and the difference is not a gap to be bridged but the space where meaning happens. The translator does not stand between two stable meanings, carrying one across to the other. The translator produces a new meaning that belongs to neither language and both.

For development practice, translation is a master concept. Every programme involves translation: from policy to practice, from headquarters to field, from one cultural context to another, from English to local languages, from international frameworks to national realities. And every act of development translation involves the same dynamics Derrida describes: loss, gain, transformation, and the impossibility of perfect fidelity.

The concept of "scaling" or "replication" is the development sector's logocentric dream — the idea that a programme can be reproduced identically in multiple contexts, that the "model" can be transferred intact from where it was tested to where it is implemented. But this is the dream of perfect translation — and perfect translation is impossible. When a conditional cash transfer programme moves from Mexico to Zambia, it is not replicated. It is translated. The institutional contexts are different, the cultural meanings of cash transfers are different, the gender dynamics are different, the political economies are different. What arrives in Zambia is not the Mexican programme — it is a new programme that bears a family resemblance to the Mexican one.

The ethical implications are significant. Translation always involves a power relation: who decides what is translatable and what is not? Whose language is the "original" and whose is the "translation"? In development, the "original" is almost always the donor's framework, the international standard, the headquarter design — and the "translation" is what happens in the field. This hierarchy is not merely linguistic — it structures accountability (the field translates the headquarters' vision, not the other way around), authority (the original is authoritative, the translation is derivative), and responsibility (when translation "fails," the fault lies with the translator, not the original).

## How to Apply

1. **Reframe implementation gaps as translation effects.**
   > "When there is a gap between programme design and programme implementation, resist the diagnosis of 'implementation failure.' Ask instead: what happened in translation? What was lost when the programme moved from design to practice, from headquarters to field, from one context to another? The gap is not a failure — it is the inevitable result of translation. The question is not how to eliminate the gap but what it reveals about the programme, the context, and the relationship between them."

2. **Identify what is untranslatable.**
   > "Every framework contains elements that resist translation — concepts, practices, institutional arrangements that are so embedded in one context that they cannot be reproduced in another without fundamental transformation. Identify these elements before the transfer, not after. What aspects of the programme depend on institutional features, cultural norms, or political conditions that are specific to the original context? These are the untranslatable elements, and they will require reinvention, not replication."

3. **Value the transformation.**
   > "What the receiving context adds to the programme is not contamination — it is the productive work of translation. Local adaptations, modifications, and innovations are not deviations from the model. They are the new meanings that emerge when a programme enters a different context. Treat them as resources, not as implementation errors."

4. **Question the direction of translation.**
   > "Why does translation flow from headquarters to field, from international to national, from English to local languages? What would it mean to reverse the direction — to translate local knowledge, local practices, local innovations into the international framework? The reversal exposes the power relation embedded in the conventional direction of translation and opens space for different kinds of knowledge to circulate."

## Examples

**Situation:** A community-driven development (CDD) model that was highly successful in Indonesia is being "replicated" in a West African country. The design team has adapted the operational manual, adjusted the budget for local costs, and translated the training materials into French and local languages. After two years, implementation results are disappointing — community participation is low, elite capture is widespread, and the model's signature feature (community-managed subprojects) is not functioning as designed.

**Application:** The diagnosis of "implementation failure" is available but misleading. What has actually happened is translation — and the translation has revealed what was untranslatable in the original model. The Indonesian CDD model depended on specific institutional features: a particular relationship between village governance and national government, a specific history of community organisation, a cultural framework for collective decision-making. These were not "context factors" that could be "adapted for" by adjusting the operational manual. They were the constitutive conditions of the model — the features without which it was not the same programme. The "translation" into West Africa necessarily produced a different programme, because the receiving context is not a neutral container into which the model can be poured but an active force that transforms what enters it. The low participation is not a failure of mobilisation — it may reflect a different political logic of community engagement. The elite capture is not a failure of governance mechanisms — it may reflect a different structure of authority that the model's design did not anticipate because it was designed for a different structure. The ethical question is about the direction of translation: the entire exercise assumes that the Indonesian model is the "original" and the West African implementation is the "translation." But what if the West African context contains knowledge about community governance that the Indonesian model lacks? What if the "failure" reveals not the inadequacy of the implementation but the inadequacy of the model's assumptions about what community-driven development requires?

## Anti-Patterns

- **Don't** use the impossibility of perfect translation as an argument against learning across contexts.
  - **Why:** The fact that replication is impossible does not mean that cross-contextual learning is worthless. It means that learning across contexts requires humility about what travels and what does not, about what is transformed and what is lost. The point is not to stop transferring programmes but to transfer them with awareness that they will be — must be — transformed in transit.

- **Don't** romanticise the local context as an authentic origin that translation corrupts.
  - **Why:** The local context is not a pure, stable original that is corrupted by the arrival of an external programme. It is itself a translated, hybrid, historically layered reality. The binary of authentic-local/imported-external reproduces the hierarchy that deconstruction interrogates. Translation goes in all directions, and every context is already a product of previous translations.
