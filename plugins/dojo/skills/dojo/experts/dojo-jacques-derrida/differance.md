# Differance

## When to Use

- When a concept central to your programme or evaluation resists stable definition — when "empowerment," "resilience," or "sustainability" means something different to every stakeholder
- When you want to understand why indicators never perfectly capture the outcomes they represent
- When the gap between what a word means and what it does is generating confusion or conflict

## Core Concept

Differance — spelled with an 'a' that can only be seen, not heard, in French — is Derrida's name for the play of meaning that makes language possible and impossible at the same time. It combines two operations: to differ (to be distinct from something else) and to defer (to postpone, to put off). Every concept means what it means only by differing from other concepts, and its meaning is always deferred — never fully present, never exhaustively captured.

This is not a deficiency of language. It is the condition of meaning itself. The word "empowerment" in a programme document does not have a meaning that sits behind it like a Platonic form. Its meaning is produced by its differences from other words — from "dependency," from "capacity," from "agency" — and by the chain of contexts in which it has been used and will be used. The meaning is never fully here, never fully now. It is always arriving and never arrived.

For development practice, differance explains why the most important concepts in our work are also the most contested. "Poverty" is not a fixed concept with a stable referent — it differs from and defers towards "inequality," "vulnerability," "deprivation," "exclusion," and its meaning shifts with each context, each measurement framework, each political moment. This is not a problem to be solved by better definitions. It is the condition of working with concepts that matter.

The practical implication is not that definitions are useless — you need working definitions to operate — but that every working definition is a provisional stabilisation of something inherently unstable. The definition does not capture the meaning; it arrests it, temporarily. And what the definition excludes will return — as contested interpretation, as implementation failure, as the gap between what was measured and what mattered.

Differance also explains the perpetual gap between a programme's stated objectives and its lived reality. The objectives are written in language, and language differs and defers. The "same" objective means something different at headquarters, in the country office, in the field, in the community. This is not a failure of communication — it is the structure of communication itself.

## How to Apply

1. **Map the chain of differences.**
   > "Take the key concept — 'resilience,' 'sustainability,' 'inclusion' — and trace the web of terms from which it derives its meaning. What is resilience not? What does it differ from? Fragility, vulnerability, dependency, rigidity. Each of these counter-terms shapes the meaning, and each is itself unstable. The concept is a node in a network, not a self-contained unit."

2. **Identify the arrested meaning.**
   > "When a programme document defines a key term, it arrests the play of differance — it fixes the meaning provisionally. Note what the definition includes and what it excludes. The exclusion is not a mistake but a necessity: you cannot operate without definitions. But the excluded meanings will return as interpretive conflict, as implementation drift, as evaluation disagreement."

3. **Attend to the gap.**
   > "The gap between the concept as defined and the concept as experienced is not a failure of alignment. It is the space of differance itself — the space where meaning happens. Instead of trying to close the gap, attend to it. What is happening in that gap? What meanings are being produced that the definition did not anticipate?"

4. **Use the instability productively.**
   > "Instead of lamenting conceptual instability, use it. If 'empowerment' means different things to different stakeholders, that multiplicity is information — it tells you something about the different subject positions, power relations, and lived experiences that the programme engages. The play of meaning is a diagnostic tool, not a problem."

## Examples

**Situation:** An M&E team is struggling because their definition of "community participation" — measured through attendance at project meetings — is producing high scores, but field staff report that the participation is hollow. Community members attend but do not speak, or speak but are not heard, or are heard but not heeded.

**Application:** Differance illuminates this problem precisely. The definition has arrested the meaning of "participation" at a single point — attendance — but the concept differs from itself across a chain of meanings: presence, voice, influence, power, ownership. Each of these is itself unstable. The indicator captures one moment in the chain and presents it as the whole. The gap between the measured participation and the experienced participation is not a measurement error — it is the play of differance at work. The concept is deferring its meaning: it promises something (genuine community influence) that is always arriving and never arrived. A deconstructively informed approach would not simply revise the indicator (though it might) but would attend to the chain of deferred meanings — asking what "participation" is always promising and never delivering, and what structures of power ensure that the deferral continues. The instability of the concept is not the problem; it is the diagnosis.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Don't** use differance as a justification for vagueness or imprecision.
  - **Why:** Differance is not an excuse for failing to define your terms. It is a philosophical observation about the structure of all definition — including precise ones. You still need working definitions. But you need them with the awareness that they are provisional arrests of meaning, not captures of truth.

- **Don't** treat differance as merely a linguistic observation irrelevant to practice.
  - **Why:** The play of meaning has material consequences. When "sustainability" is defined as financial self-sufficiency, it excludes ecological, social, and cultural meanings — and that exclusion shapes what gets funded, what gets measured, and what gets valued. Differance is not about words. It is about the politics of meaning.
