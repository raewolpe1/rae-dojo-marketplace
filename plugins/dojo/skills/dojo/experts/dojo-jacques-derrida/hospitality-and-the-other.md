# Hospitality and the Other

## When to Use

- When you are designing programme eligibility criteria and want to think carefully about who is included and who is excluded
- When a programme claims to be "inclusive" or "participatory" and you want to examine what those claims actually mean
- When you are confronting genuine difference — cultural, epistemological, political — and want to avoid reducing it to something manageable

## Core Concept

Derrida draws a sharp distinction between conditional and unconditional hospitality. Conditional hospitality says: you are welcome, provided you identify yourself, meet our criteria, follow our rules, speak our language, fit our categories. Unconditional hospitality says: you are welcome, whoever you are, without conditions, without even the requirement that you identify yourself. The first is the hospitality of the immigration office. The second is the hospitality of the ethical demand.

The tension is that unconditional hospitality is impossible in practice. Every programme, every institution, every policy must have conditions — eligibility criteria, targeting mechanisms, resource constraints. You cannot welcome everyone without limits. And yet the ethical demand of the other — the demand that arrives before any condition can be set — is unconditional. The other asks to be received as other, not as a case to be classified.

This is not a contradiction to be resolved but an aporia to be inhabited. The gap between unconditional hospitality (which is ethically demanded but practically impossible) and conditional hospitality (which is practically necessary but ethically insufficient) is the space where development practice actually happens. Every targeting decision, every eligibility criterion, every beneficiary selection process sits in this gap.

For development, this means that the question is never simply "are our criteria right?" but "what does our conditional hospitality exclude, and how do we maintain a relationship with what we exclude?" The wholly other — the person who does not fit our categories, whose needs we did not anticipate, whose knowledge we cannot classify — is precisely the one who tests whether our hospitality is genuine.

The concept of the other is equally important. The other, for Derrida (drawing on Levinas), is not simply another person. The other is what exceeds my comprehension, my categories, my frameworks. The ethical demand of the other is infinite — it cannot be satisfied by any set of actions, any checklist, any programme design. This is not a counsel of despair but a demand for humility: the recognition that our frameworks are always insufficient to the people they claim to serve.

## How to Apply

1. **Audit the conditions.**
   > "Take your programme's eligibility criteria, targeting mechanisms, or participation requirements. List every condition. For each condition, ask: who is excluded by this condition? Is the exclusion necessary, or is it an artefact of the framework's limitations? And most importantly: does the programme have any mechanism for encountering those it excludes?"

2. **Test for domestication.**
   > "When a programme claims to 'include diverse perspectives' or 'engage with local communities,' ask: on whose terms? Does the engagement require the other to translate their knowledge into our categories? Does participation require conformity to our meeting formats, our timelines, our languages? Genuine hospitality would be transformed by the encounter. If the programme remains unchanged by the people it claims to include, it has domesticated the other rather than received them."

3. **Locate the infinite demand.**
   > "The ethical demand of the other exceeds any programme's capacity to respond. This is not a failure — it is the structure of ethical life. Name the excess. What does the community need that the programme cannot provide? What knowledge does the community hold that the programme cannot accommodate? The excess is not a problem to be solved but a relationship to be maintained."

4. **Design for the unanticipated.**
   > "If your programme can only serve those it anticipated, it is not hospitable — it is a machine. Build in mechanisms for encountering the unanticipated: flexible funding, adaptive management, spaces where the programme's categories can be questioned by those they classify. This is not merely good practice — it is the minimal condition of ethical hospitality."

## Examples

**Situation:** A social protection programme has established careful targeting criteria based on a proxy means test to identify the "ultra-poor." Field implementers report that certain households in severe need are being excluded because they do not meet the proxy indicators — they own a mobile phone (gifted by a relative) or have a tin roof (installed by an NGO years ago) that pushes them above the threshold.

**Application:** This is a textbook case of the gap between conditional and unconditional hospitality. The proxy means test is a structure of conditional hospitality — it says, in effect, "you are welcome to our programme if you meet these conditions." The conditions are not arbitrary; they are the programme's best effort to identify need within resource constraints. But the conditions have become the gatekeepers, and they are excluding precisely those whose situation exceeds the categories. The households with gifted phones and donated roofs are the wholly other of this targeting system — they reveal the limits of the framework's capacity to see. A Derridean response would not simply revise the proxy indicators (though revision may be warranted). It would ask: what mechanism does the programme have for encountering those its categories exclude? Is there a space where the targeting system can be questioned by those it fails? The answer is not to abandon targeting — unconditional hospitality is impossible — but to maintain an active, uncomfortable relationship with the programme's exclusions rather than treating them as regrettable but inevitable.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Don't** use the impossibility of unconditional hospitality as an excuse for not trying.
  - **Why:** The aporia between conditional and unconditional hospitality is not a reason for complacency. It is a demand for vigilance — for constantly examining the conditions you impose and asking whether they are necessary or merely convenient. The impossibility of perfect hospitality does not excuse the refusal to improve imperfect hospitality.

- **Don't** romanticise the other as inherently virtuous or wise.
  - **Why:** The other, for Derrida, is not a noble figure to be idealised. The other is what exceeds your categories — which may be uncomfortable, challenging, or even threatening. Hospitality is not sentimental. It is the willingness to be transformed by an encounter you did not choose and cannot control. Romanticising the other is another way of domesticating them.
