# Justice and Law

## When to Use

- When you are working on rule-of-law programming and want to think about the relationship between legal reform and justice
- When a programme's compliance with its own rules seems to be producing unjust outcomes
- When you need to articulate why procedural correctness is necessary but insufficient

## Core Concept

Justice is not law. This is perhaps Derrida's most politically charged philosophical claim, and it has direct consequences for development practice. Law is a system of rules, conventions, calculations — it is constructed, historical, contingent. Law is deconstructible, which means its assumptions can be exposed, its hierarchies questioned, its exclusions named. Justice, by contrast, is not deconstructible. Justice is the infinite demand that exceeds every legal framework, every institutional arrangement, every set of rules.

The distinction is not that law is bad and justice is good. Law is necessary — you cannot have a functioning society without rules, procedures, institutions. But law is never identical to justice. There is always a gap between what the law requires and what justice demands. That gap is not a failure of the legal system — it is the condition of politics. Political action happens in the gap between law and justice: it is the effort to make law more just, knowing that the effort will never be complete, that justice will always exceed whatever law we create.

For development practice, this means that rule-of-law programming is not the same as justice programming — and confusing the two is one of the most consequential errors in governance work. You can build courts, train judges, draft constitutions, and establish legal aid systems, and the result can still be profoundly unjust. Not because the laws are bad (they may be excellent) but because law, by its nature, is a system of general rules applied to particular cases, and the particular always exceeds the general.

Every time a development programme applies its rules — its eligibility criteria, its procurement procedures, its reporting requirements — it performs an act of law. And every such act involves a gap between the rule and the case, between the general and the particular, between what the procedure requires and what justice demands. The programme manager who applies the rule correctly but produces an unjust outcome is not merely following orders — they are inhabiting the gap between law and justice, whether they know it or not.

The political implication is radical: the gap between law and justice is not a problem to be closed but a space to be kept open. A legal system that claimed to be perfectly just — that claimed there was no gap between its rules and justice — would be the most dangerous system of all, because it would have no space for critique, no room for the demand that things could be otherwise.

## How to Apply

1. **Name the gap.**
   > "In every governance intervention, every rule-of-law programme, every institutional reform, ask: where is the gap between the law (the rules, the procedures, the framework) and justice (the infinite demand of the particular case)? Do not try to close the gap — try to keep it visible."

2. **Examine the act of judgement.**
   > "Every application of a rule to a case involves a judgement — a decision that cannot be fully determined by the rule itself. The rule says 'eligible households below the poverty line,' but the judgement of who is below the line, measured how, by whom, involves decisions that exceed the rule. Attend to these moments of judgement. They are where justice and law diverge."

3. **Question the universality claim.**
   > "When a framework claims to be universally applicable — human rights, good governance principles, international best practice — ask: whose particular experience has been universalised? What was excluded from the universal in order to construct it? The universal is necessary, but it is never innocent."

4. **Protect the space of critique.**
   > "The most important feature of a just institution is not that it has good rules — it is that it has space for those rules to be questioned. A programme that cannot be challenged by those it serves is not just, regardless of how well-designed its procedures are. Build in mechanisms for the rules themselves to be contested."

## Examples

**Situation:** A governance programme has successfully established community-level grievance mechanisms. Complaints are being filed and processed according to transparent procedures. But an evaluation reveals that the mechanisms are being used primarily by elite community members, and that the most marginalised groups — women, youth, ethnic minorities — do not file complaints because the procedures require literacy, because the grievance categories do not match their experiences, or because filing a complaint against powerful community members carries risks that the mechanism does not address.

**Application:** This is a precise illustration of the gap between law and justice. The law — the grievance mechanism with its procedures, forms, categories, timelines — is functioning correctly. Complaints are filed, processed, resolved. The system is procedurally sound. But justice — the infinite demand of the particular case, the claim of the marginalised person whose experience does not fit the categories — exceeds the mechanism. The mechanism's very success as law obscures its failure as justice. A Derridean analysis would not recommend abandoning the mechanism (law is necessary) but would insist on keeping the gap visible: acknowledging that the mechanism serves those it can categorise and fails those it cannot, building in spaces where the categories themselves can be questioned, and resisting the institutional temptation to present procedural success as justice achieved. The most dangerous moment is when the programme reports that the grievance mechanism is "working well" — because that claim closes the gap between law and justice, making further critique appear unnecessary.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Don't** conclude that because law is not justice, law does not matter.
  - **Why:** Derrida is emphatic: law is necessary. The deconstructibility of law is not a reason to abandon legal frameworks but a reason to keep improving them — while knowing that improvement will never achieve identity with justice. The gap is permanent. That is what makes political action possible.

- **Don't** treat justice as a fixed standard against which law can be measured.
  - **Why:** Justice is not a blueprint. It is not a set of criteria that law could theoretically satisfy. Justice is the excess — the demand that always remains after every legal reform, every institutional improvement, every procedural refinement. If you turn justice into a checklist, you have converted it into law, and the gap reappears elsewhere.
