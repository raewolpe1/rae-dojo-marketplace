# Currency Hierarchy: Centre Currencies Enjoy Policy Space; Periphery Currencies Are Constrained

## When to Use

- When analysing why African countries face monetary constraints that centre-economy countries do not — and why this asymmetry is structural rather than a reflection of policy quality.
- When evaluating exchange-rate regime choices (fixed, floating, managed, pegged) in the context of peripheral economies where the "choice" is constrained by the hierarchy itself.
- When explaining why policy prescriptions designed for centre economies (inflation targeting, free-floating exchange rates, full capital account openness) produce perverse outcomes when applied at the periphery.

## Core Concept

The international monetary system is not a level playing field. It is organised hierarchically, with currencies ranked by their international acceptability, their role in global reserves, and the policy space their issuers enjoy. At the apex sits the US dollar — the invoicing currency for most global trade, the denomination of most international debt, and the reserve currency held by central banks worldwide. Below it, the euro, the yen, the pound sterling, and the Swiss franc occupy positions of relative privilege. At the base of the hierarchy sit the currencies of the Global South — including all African currencies — which are not accepted in international transactions, are not held as reserves, and whose issuers face constraints that centre-country issuers do not.

This hierarchy is not a natural outcome of market forces. It is maintained by institutional arrangements built in the post-war period and reinforced since: the Bretton Woods institutions, the dollar's role in oil pricing, the network effects of dollar-denominated financial markets, and the conditionality regimes that prescribe specific monetary architectures for peripheral countries. The hierarchy is self-reinforcing: because peripheral currencies are not accepted internationally, peripheral countries must earn or borrow centre currencies to participate in world trade, which creates dependence on commodity exports and external borrowing, which reinforces the subordinate position of their currencies.

For African economies, the currency hierarchy produces several concrete constraints. First, the "original sin" of international finance: because African countries cannot borrow internationally in their own currencies, they must borrow in dollars or euros, which creates currency mismatch and exchange-rate risk that centre-country borrowers do not face. Second, asymmetric adjustment: when a shock hits (commodity price collapse, global recession, pandemic), centre-country central banks can expand their balance sheets, lower interest rates, and run fiscal deficits denominated in their own currencies. Peripheral-country central banks face capital flight, currency depreciation, and pressure to tighten precisely when their economies need expansion. Third, policy importation: peripheral countries that peg to centre currencies (as CFA zone countries do to the euro) import the monetary conditions of the centre economy, regardless of whether those conditions suit the peripheral economy.

The currency hierarchy means that the "choice" of exchange-rate regime is not a free technical decision. A peripheral country that floats its currency faces volatility driven by capital flows and commodity price swings, not by domestic fundamentals. A peripheral country that fixes its currency surrenders exchange-rate adjustment and imports the centre's monetary policy. A peripheral country that manages its exchange rate faces pressure from the IMF and international capital markets to liberalise. Every option is constrained by the hierarchical position itself.

## How to Apply

1. **Locate the currency in the hierarchy.** Before analysing any monetary policy question for an African economy, establish where its currency sits in the global hierarchy. Is it a CFA franc (pegged to the euro, no independent monetary policy)? A managed float (the naira, the cedi)? A relatively deeper market (the rand)? The position determines the constraint set.

2. **Identify the asymmetric adjustment mechanism.** When analysing a shock (commodity price fall, global financial tightening, pandemic), trace how the adjustment burden distributes between centre and periphery. Centre-country central banks expand; peripheral central banks contract. Centre-country governments borrow cheaply in their own currency; peripheral governments borrow expensively in foreign currency. This asymmetry is not a policy failure but a structural feature of the hierarchy.

3. **Evaluate policy prescriptions against the hierarchy.** When confronting recommendations for inflation targeting, capital account liberalisation, or free-floating exchange rates for African economies, ask whether these prescriptions account for the hierarchical position. Inflation targeting assumes a transmission mechanism (interest rate to aggregate demand) that works differently when inflation is driven by import costs and food prices. Free floating assumes that the exchange rate will move to equilibrium when capital flows driven by global risk appetite, not domestic fundamentals, dominate.

4. **Distinguish between reforming domestic policy and reforming the hierarchy.** Some constraints on African monetary policy are domestic and can be addressed by better policy. But some are structural features of the hierarchy that no domestic policy can overcome unilaterally. The analytical task is to distinguish between the two and to be honest about which constraints require systemic reform rather than domestic adjustment.

## Examples

**Situation:** A government economic advisor in Ghana asks why the Bank of Ghana's inflation-targeting framework has not delivered sustained price stability despite following IMF-recommended best practices, and whether the framework itself is the problem.

**Application:** Ghana's position in the currency hierarchy is the key diagnostic. The cedi is a peripheral currency — not held as international reserves, not used for invoicing global trade, and subject to depreciation pressures driven by factors beyond the Bank of Ghana's control: global dollar strength, commodity price volatility (gold, cocoa, oil), and capital flow cycles driven by US Federal Reserve policy. When the Fed tightens, capital flows out of Ghana regardless of the Bank of Ghana's policy rate. When cocoa prices fall, the cedi depreciates regardless of domestic inflation dynamics. The Bank of Ghana then raises interest rates to defend the currency and contain imported inflation — contracting the domestic economy precisely when it needs support. The inflation-targeting framework, designed for centre economies where the central bank's policy rate is the primary determinant of inflation, produces perverse results in a peripheral economy where exchange-rate pass-through and import costs drive the price level. The framework is not wrong in the abstract; it is wrong for Ghana's position in the hierarchy. The advisor should consider a framework that targets the real exchange rate, manages capital flows, and distinguishes between demand-pull inflation (amenable to interest rate policy) and imported inflation (which interest rate tightening worsens by contracting the economy without addressing the source of price pressure).

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the currency hierarchy as a conspiracy theory or a purely political critique.
**Why:** The hierarchy is an observable structural feature of the international monetary system, documented by mainstream international economists (Eichengreen, Rey, Obstfeld) as well as heterodox scholars. Treating it as a conspiracy undermines the analytical precision that makes the concept useful. The hierarchy is maintained by institutional arrangements, network effects, and path dependence — not by a secret committee.

**Don't:** Assume that all peripheral currencies face identical constraints.
**Why:** The South African rand has deeper financial markets and greater international acceptability than the Malawian kwacha. The Nigerian naira faces oil-driven dynamics that differ from the Kenyan shilling's agricultural exposure. The CFA franc faces a unique constraint set defined by the franc zone architecture. Currency hierarchy is a structural framework, but it must be applied with attention to the specific position and constraint set of each currency.
