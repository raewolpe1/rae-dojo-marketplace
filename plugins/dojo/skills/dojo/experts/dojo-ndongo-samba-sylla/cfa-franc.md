# The CFA Franc as Still-Operating Colonial Monetary Instrument

## When to Use

- When a conversation involves the CFA franc, the franc zone, the BCEAO, the BEAC, or the operations account — and requires analysis of how the system actually works, not just what it is called.
- When evaluating the stalled Eco currency transition, proposed CFA reforms, or arguments about whether the franc zone serves African interests.
- When confronting the standard defence — price stability, credibility, integration — and needing the institutional mechanics to show what the defence omits.

## Core Concept

The CFA franc is not a currency in the ordinary sense. It is a monetary arrangement designed in 1945 by the French Treasury, modified at the margins since independence, but structurally continuous with its colonial origins. Fourteen African countries — eight in the West African Economic and Monetary Union (WAEMU, served by the BCEAO) and six in the Central African Economic and Monetary Community (CEMAC, served by the BEAC) — use this currency under conditions that no sovereign monetary authority would accept voluntarily.

The architecture has four pillars. First, the fixed parity: the CFA franc is pegged to the euro (previously the French franc) at a rate set by France and unilaterally adjustable by France, as the 1994 devaluation demonstrated. Second, the convertibility guarantee: France guarantees unlimited convertibility of CFA francs into euros, which sounds beneficial until you ask who benefits from capital mobility — not the subsistence farmer but the multinational firm repatriating profits. Third, the operations account: CFA zone central banks are required to deposit a minimum share (currently 50 per cent, formerly 65 per cent) of their foreign exchange reserves with the French Treasury. These reserves earn interest, but they are held in Paris, managed under French oversight, and available to the French Treasury for its own liquidity management. Fourth, French representation on the boards of the BCEAO and BEAC, providing institutional oversight that goes beyond what any other external party exercises over African monetary institutions.

The system delivers low inflation — this is empirically true and must be acknowledged. But the mechanism is an overvalued exchange rate that suppresses domestic prices by making imports artificially cheap and exports uncompetitive. The developmental consequences are severe: deindustrialisation, chronic trade deficits, suppressed agricultural producer prices, and facilitated capital flight through the convertibility guarantee. The CFA zone contains some of the poorest, least industrialised, and most commodity-dependent economies in the world — after sixty years of the "stability" the system supposedly provides.

The 2019 reform — the announced replacement of the CFA franc with the Eco in the WAEMU zone — changed the name and eliminated the French representatives on the BCEAO board, but preserved the fixed parity to the euro and the French guarantee. Sylla and others identified this as a rebranding exercise, not a reform. The fundamental architecture — external anchor, surrendered exchange-rate policy, convertibility that serves capital rather than development — remained intact.

## How to Apply

1. **Identify which pillar is operative.** When analysing a specific CFA-related problem (capital flight, trade deficit, inability to respond to commodity price shocks), trace it to the specific institutional mechanism responsible: the fixed parity, the convertibility guarantee, the reserve requirements, or the governance structure.

2. **Steelman the defence, then show the cost.** The CFA franc has genuine defenders. Acknowledge the low-inflation record, the elimination of exchange-rate risk within the zone, and the credibility anchor. Then demonstrate what these benefits cost: overvaluation, deindustrialisation, surrendered counter-cyclical capacity, facilitated capital outflows.

3. **Distinguish reform from rebranding.** Any proposed CFA reform must be evaluated against the four pillars. If the fixed parity remains, the exchange rate is still set by external conditions. If the convertibility guarantee remains, capital mobility still serves external interests. Changing names, board seats, or reserve deposit percentages while preserving the structural architecture is rebranding.

4. **Connect to specific country outcomes.** The CFA system affects different member countries differently. Cote d'Ivoire, as the zone's largest economy, captures more benefits from the arrangement than Niger or Burkina Faso. Oil exporters in CEMAC (Congo, Gabon, Equatorial Guinea) face different constraints than the landlocked Sahel economies. Specify which country and which sector when analysing CFA effects.

## Examples

**Situation:** A development agency is designing an economic resilience programme for Burkina Faso and asks why the government cannot use monetary policy to cushion the impact of falling cotton prices.

**Application:** The answer lies in the CFA franc architecture. Burkina Faso uses the CFA franc, issued by the BCEAO, which sets interest rates for the entire WAEMU zone. When global cotton prices fall, Burkina Faso cannot lower interest rates to cushion the domestic economy — the BCEAO sets a single rate for eight countries with different economic structures and different exposure to the cotton market. Nor can Burkina Faso devalue its currency to make its cotton more competitive on world markets — the CFA franc is pegged to the euro at a rate Burkina Faso does not control. Nor can the government expand fiscal spending in its own currency to absorb unemployed cotton workers — the CFA arrangement constrains fiscal deficits and the BCEAO's ability to finance government spending. The resilience programme must acknowledge that the monetary architecture itself is a source of fragility, not just the commodity dependence. Without this diagnosis, the programme will treat symptoms while the structural cause persists.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Describe the CFA franc as merely a "legacy" or "historical artifact" of colonialism.
**Why:** The CFA franc is not a legacy — it is a functioning institutional arrangement with active treaty obligations, ongoing reserve deposits, and current governance mechanisms. Treating it as a relic obscures the fact that it is maintained by contemporary political choices and institutional interests.

**Don't:** Claim that leaving the CFA franc would automatically solve development problems.
**Why:** Monetary sovereignty is a necessary condition for developmental policy space, not a sufficient one. A country that exits the CFA zone without building central bank capacity, reserve management infrastructure, capital account management tools, and a credible transition plan may face worse outcomes in the short run. The argument is for managed transition, not overnight exit.
