# Fair Trade as Marketing of Poverty

## When to Use

- When a conversation involves fair trade certification, ethical sourcing, or consumer-driven approaches to global trade justice — and requires analysis of why these approaches fail structurally even when they succeed commercially.
- When evaluating development programmes that incorporate fair trade or ethical trade components as instruments of poverty reduction.
- When the narrative that conscious consumption in the Global North can transform the structural position of commodity producers in the Global South needs to be examined critically.

## Core Concept

Fair trade is a marketing phenomenon that presents itself as a development intervention. It certifies slightly better terms of trade for a small number of commodity producers, brands those terms as "fair," and markets the resulting products to consumers in the Global North who wish to feel that their purchasing choices make a difference. The system generates significant value — but that value accrues overwhelmingly to the certifiers, the retailers, the roasters, the packagers, and the brands, not to the producers whose poverty is its essential marketing material.

The structural critique has several dimensions. First, fair trade operates within, not against, the commodity structure that produces poverty. The fundamental problem for a coffee or cocoa or cotton producer in Africa is not that their particular transaction is unfair but that the international commodity structure — price-setting power concentrated among a few multinational buyers, value addition occurring in consuming countries, terms of trade trending against primary commodities over the long run — is rigged against them. Fair trade offers a marginal price premium within this structure. It does not challenge the structure itself.

Second, the price premium is small relative to the final retail price, and the share that reaches the producer is smaller still. A fair trade coffee that retails for four dollars may include a premium of twenty cents to the cooperative, of which a fraction reaches individual farmers. The rest of the premium is captured at every stage of the supply chain north of the farm gate. The consumer pays more; the producer receives marginally more; the intermediaries capture the difference.

Third, fair trade certification is controlled by Northern organisations. The standards, the auditing processes, the branding, and the market access are managed by bodies headquartered in Europe and North America. Producers must comply with standards set by others, pay for certification administered by others, and depend on market access controlled by others. The power asymmetry that defines the commodity relationship is reproduced within the fair trade system itself.

Fourth, fair trade markets poverty. It requires the producer to remain a small-scale, artisanal, often "traditional" producer in order to qualify for the certification and to fit the marketing narrative. A Senegalese peanut farmer who mechanises, scales up, and begins processing peanuts into peanut butter for regional markets is no longer a sympathetic fair trade story. The system rewards the condition of poverty while claiming to alleviate it — and structurally discourages the transformation (value addition, industrialisation, scaling) that would actually change the producer's position.

The alternative is not better certification but structural transformation: commodity processing in producing countries, regional trade in manufactured goods, diversification away from commodity dependence, and the monetary and policy sovereignty necessary to pursue these objectives. Fair trade is a substitute for this transformation, not a step toward it.

## How to Apply

1. **Follow the value.** For any fair trade or ethical trade scheme, trace where value is created and captured along the supply chain. What share of the retail price reaches the producer? What share is captured by certification bodies, intermediaries, roasters, retailers, and brands? The distribution of value reveals the distribution of power — and it is rarely "fair."

2. **Ask what structural change the scheme produces.** Does the fair trade intervention alter the commodity structure — price-setting power, value addition, terms of trade? Or does it offer a marginal improvement within an unchanged structure? If the coffee farmer is still exporting raw beans, still price-taking, still dependent on Northern markets and Northern certifiers, the structure has not changed regardless of the premium.

3. **Examine who controls the certification.** Who sets the standards? Who audits compliance? Who grants or revokes certification? Who manages the brand? If control resides in the Global North and compliance burdens fall on producers in the Global South, the power relationship within the fair trade system mirrors the power relationship it claims to correct.

4. **Identify the development alternative the fair trade narrative displaces.** Fair trade competes for attention, funding, and policy energy with structural alternatives: commodity processing mandates, industrial policy, regional trade agreements, monetary sovereignty. By offering a feel-good market-based solution, fair trade reduces the political urgency of structural transformation. This displacement effect may be its most significant developmental impact — and it is negative.

## Examples

**Situation:** A development consultancy is designing a programme to improve livelihoods for smallholder cocoa farmers in Cote d'Ivoire. The programme concept includes a significant fair trade certification component, justified by the argument that fair trade premiums will increase farmer incomes and fund community development projects.

**Application:** The programme should be evaluated against the structural realities of the Ivorian cocoa sector. Cote d'Ivoire is the world's largest cocoa producer, but virtually all cocoa is exported as raw beans. Value addition — processing, chocolate manufacturing, branding — occurs almost entirely in Europe, where a small number of multinational firms control the market. A fair trade premium of a few hundred dollars per tonne is marginal compared to the value that processing and manufacturing add. Moreover, fair trade certification covers a tiny share of Ivorian cocoa production; scaling it to the entire sector is neither feasible (Northern fair trade demand is limited) nor desirable (it would collapse the premium). The programme would produce more lasting impact by investing in domestic cocoa processing capacity, supporting the development of regional chocolate brands for African markets, advocating for trade policies that incentivise value addition in producing countries, and addressing the monetary constraints (CFA franc overvaluation reduces export competitiveness for processed goods) that make industrial upgrading difficult. Fair trade certification can be included as one small component, but it should not be the programme's theory of change — because it does not produce structural change.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Dismiss fair trade as a scam or as having no value at all for participating producers.
**Why:** Individual fair trade cooperatives and producers do receive marginally higher prices, and fair trade premiums do fund community projects in some cases. The critique is structural — fair trade does not and cannot transform the commodity structure — not that every specific fair trade transaction is fraudulent. Dismissing all value alienates potential allies and misrepresents the argument.

**Don't:** Frame the critique as anti-consumer or anti-solidarity.
**Why:** The problem is not that Northern consumers care about producers in the Global South. The problem is that their caring is channelled into a market mechanism that markets poverty rather than addressing its structural causes. The critique should redirect solidarity toward structural demands — trade justice, monetary sovereignty, industrialisation support — not discourage solidarity itself.
