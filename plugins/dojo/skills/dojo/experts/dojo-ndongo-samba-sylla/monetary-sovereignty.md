# Monetary Sovereignty as the Unfinished Project of African Independence

## When to Use

- When the conversation concerns whether and how African states can reclaim the monetary policy instruments necessary for developmental autonomy.
- When distinguishing monetary sovereignty from related but different concepts — monetary nationalism, autarky, or simple currency issuance.
- When analysing the gap between formal political independence and substantive economic independence across the continent.

## Core Concept

Monetary sovereignty is the capacity of a state to issue its own currency, set its own interest rates, manage its own exchange rate, and deploy fiscal policy denominated in that currency. It is not an abstract principle or an ideological position. It is a set of concrete policy instruments without which a government cannot respond to economic shocks, finance public investment on its own terms, or pursue an industrial strategy.

The argument is not that monetary sovereignty guarantees good outcomes. It does not. A sovereign currency-issuing government can still pursue bad policy, misallocate resources, or fail to industrialise. But without monetary sovereignty, even a well-governed state with sound developmental intentions lacks the instruments to act. It must borrow in foreign currency, accept external conditionality, and subordinate domestic economic management to the priorities of external creditors or anchor-currency issuers.

African independence was, in most cases, a political achievement without a monetary counterpart. The CFA franc zone is the most extreme example — fourteen nations retained a currency system designed in Paris, governed under French institutional oversight, and anchored to a European currency. But the problem extends beyond the franc zone. Countries with nominally independent currencies face other forms of monetary constraint: dependence on dollar-denominated commodity revenues, IMF conditionality that prescribes central bank independence and inflation targeting, capital account liberalisation that exposes thin financial markets to speculative flows, and the structural hierarchy of the international monetary system that limits the policy space available to peripheral currencies.

Monetary sovereignty is thus not a binary — sovereign or not sovereign — but a spectrum. At one end, the United States issues the global reserve currency and faces essentially no external monetary constraint. At the other end, a CFA zone country has surrendered every monetary instrument. Between these poles, most African countries occupy positions of partial sovereignty, constrained in different ways and to different degrees. The task of economic decolonisation is to move along this spectrum — not toward autarky, but toward a position where the state possesses the instruments necessary to pursue its developmental objectives.

This is what makes monetary sovereignty the unfinished project of independence. Flags changed, anthems were composed, seats were taken at the United Nations. But the monetary architecture — the deep structure that determines what economic policies are possible — remained in many cases continuous with the colonial arrangement, or was replaced by new forms of external constraint through the Bretton Woods institutions.

## How to Apply

1. **Audit the sovereignty spectrum.** For any African country, inventory the three instruments: currency issuance (does the country issue its own currency or use a shared/pegged one?), interest rate control (does the central bank set rates based on domestic conditions?), exchange rate management (can the rate adjust to reflect terms-of-trade changes?). The gap between nominal and effective sovereignty is the diagnostic.

2. **Distinguish sovereignty from autarky.** Monetary sovereignty does not mean printing money without constraint, rejecting international trade, or refusing external engagement. It means possessing the instruments — and then choosing how to deploy them based on domestic developmental priorities rather than external mandates.

3. **Identify the binding constraint.** For CFA zone countries, the constraint is the institutional architecture itself. For non-CFA countries, the constraint may be IMF conditionality, dollar dependence, capital account openness, or the terms of external borrowing. Different constraints require different strategies for reclaiming policy space.

4. **Connect sovereignty to specific developmental objectives.** Monetary sovereignty matters because it enables — or its absence disables — specific policy actions: counter-cyclical fiscal spending, exchange-rate adjustment for export competitiveness, central bank financing of development banks, capital account management to prevent destabilising outflows. Always connect the abstract concept to the concrete policy it enables.

## Examples

**Situation:** A research institute is preparing a policy brief on economic transformation in East Africa and asks whether monetary sovereignty should be framed as a prerequisite or a desirable-but-optional condition for structural change.

**Application:** It is a prerequisite, not an option. Consider the concrete requirements of structural transformation: an industrial policy that directs credit to priority sectors requires a central bank with a developmental mandate and the capacity to influence credit allocation. An exchange-rate policy that maintains export competitiveness requires the ability to manage the exchange rate — which a country pegged to a foreign currency or constrained by IMF conditionality cannot do. Counter-cyclical fiscal policy that sustains demand during commodity downturns requires the ability to spend in one's own currency without triggering a debt crisis in foreign currency. Capital account management that prevents speculative inflows from destabilising the economy and speculative outflows from draining it requires policy instruments that capital account liberalisation has removed. Each component of structural transformation depends on a monetary instrument that the absence of monetary sovereignty removes from the toolkit. The policy brief should frame this explicitly: without monetary sovereignty, structural transformation plans are wish lists — they describe what should happen without commanding the instruments that would make it happen.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Equate monetary sovereignty with the ability to "print money" without consequence.
**Why:** This caricature — common in mainstream critiques — confuses sovereignty with recklessness. A currency-issuing government faces real constraints: productive capacity, inflation, exchange-rate management, and import dependence. Sovereignty means having the instruments and the policy space; it does not mean those instruments have no limits.

**Don't:** Treat monetary sovereignty as sufficient for development on its own.
**Why:** Monetary sovereignty provides necessary instruments but does not guarantee their effective use. Industrial policy, institutional capacity, governance quality, and productive structure all matter. The argument is that without monetary sovereignty, these other factors cannot be effectively mobilised — not that monetary sovereignty alone is enough.
