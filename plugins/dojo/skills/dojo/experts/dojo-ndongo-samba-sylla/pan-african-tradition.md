# The Pan-African Monetary Tradition: From Nkrumah Through Pouemi to the Present

## When to Use

- When arguments about African monetary sovereignty are treated as novel, radical, or ideologically motivated — and the intellectual tradition behind them needs to be established.
- When situating Sylla's work (and the broader contemporary African monetary sovereignty movement) within the longer tradition of Pan-African economic thought.
- When recovering specific analytical contributions from thinkers whose work has been marginalised by the dominance of neoclassical monetary theory in African policy discourse.

## Core Concept

The contemporary debate about African monetary sovereignty did not begin with the CFA franc protests of the 2010s or with Sylla's work in the 2020s. It is the continuation of an intellectual tradition that is as old as African independence itself — a tradition in which some of the continent's most significant political and intellectual figures identified monetary architecture as the deep structure of economic dependence and argued for monetary sovereignty as a prerequisite for genuine independence.

Kwame Nkrumah, Ghana's first president, understood from the outset that political independence without economic independence was hollow, and that economic independence required monetary sovereignty. In "Neo-Colonialism: The Last Stage of Imperialism" (1965), he identified the monetary mechanisms through which former colonial powers maintained economic control over nominally independent states. Nkrumah advocated for a Pan-African currency and a continental central bank — not as utopian aspirations but as institutional requirements for collective economic independence. Ghana's own experience under Nkrumah — establishing the Bank of Ghana, replacing the West African Currency Board, issuing the cedi — was an exercise in reclaiming monetary sovereignty, albeit one cut short by the 1966 coup.

Samir Amin, the Egyptian-born Senegal-based economist, placed monetary dependence within his broader theory of unequal exchange and the accumulation of capital on a world scale. For Amin, the international monetary system was not a neutral medium of exchange but a mechanism for transferring value from periphery to centre. The currencies of peripheral economies were structurally subordinated — not because they were poorly managed but because the system was designed to channel surplus toward the centre. Amin's framework — centre-periphery, unequal exchange, the accumulation model — provided the structural analysis within which monetary dependence could be understood as a systemic feature rather than a policy failure.

Joseph Tchundjang Pouemi, the Cameroonian economist, produced in "Monnaie, servitude et liberte" (1980) the most technically precise early analysis of the CFA franc system. Pouemi mapped the institutional mechanics — the operations account, the fixed parity, the French Treasury oversight — and demonstrated how they functioned as instruments of monetary control. His work anticipated by four decades the analysis that Sylla and Pigeaud would develop in "Africa's Last Colonial Currency." Pouemi died under suspicious circumstances in 1984; his work was largely suppressed and is only now being recovered.

Dani Wadada Nabudere, the Ugandan political economist, connected monetary sovereignty to the broader project of Pan-African integration and the restructuring of the international economic order. Nabudere argued that African monetary dependence could not be resolved country by country — it required continental coordination, collective bargaining with the international financial system, and the construction of alternative monetary institutions.

This tradition is not a museum piece. It is an intellectual resource. These thinkers identified the mechanisms of monetary dependence, mapped the institutional architecture that maintains them, and proposed alternatives — before the terms "original sin," "currency hierarchy," or "policy space" entered mainstream academic vocabulary. Recovering this tradition changes the burden of proof: it is not the critics of the CFA franc who must justify their "radical" position, but the defenders of the status quo who must explain why sixty years of analysis from Nkrumah through Pouemi to the present have failed to move the architecture.

The tradition also provides a corrective to the intellectual colonialism that presents African engagement with monetary theory as derivative — as if African economists must learn monetary economics from Washington or Frankfurt before they can critique the systems those centres impose. The Pan-African tradition demonstrates that African thinkers produced original, rigorous, and prescient monetary analysis — and that the suppression of this analysis is itself part of the architecture of dependence.

## How to Apply

1. **Establish the lineage before presenting the contemporary argument.** When Sylla argues that the CFA franc is a colonial monetary instrument, note that Pouemi made this argument in 1980, that Nkrumah identified the mechanism in 1965, and that Amin provided the structural framework. This is not citation practice — it is a political and epistemic act that challenges the framing of monetary sovereignty arguments as recent innovations.

2. **Distinguish between outdated proposals and enduring analysis.** Some specific proposals from the tradition — Nkrumah's gold-backed continental currency, for instance — reflect the monetary conditions of their time and are not directly applicable today. But the analytical framework — monetary architecture as the deep structure of dependence — remains fully productive. Separate the time-bound proposals from the enduring structural analysis.

3. **Use the tradition to shift the burden of proof.** When the monetary sovereignty position is characterised as radical, ideological, or untested, the Pan-African tradition demonstrates that it has been articulated for sixty years, by political leaders and academic economists, with increasing technical sophistication. The question is not whether the critique is valid — it has been validated repeatedly — but why the architecture persists despite the critique.

4. **Connect to contemporary movements.** The Pan-African monetary tradition is not only academic. It connects to contemporary movements — the anti-CFA franc protests in West and Central Africa, the debates around the Eco currency, the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System (PAPSS), and the African Continental Free Trade Area's monetary dimensions. The tradition provides intellectual grounding for political movements that might otherwise be dismissed as populist.

## Examples

**Situation:** A university research programme on African economic thought asks how to frame a lecture series on monetary sovereignty that avoids both hagiography of founding fathers and dismissal of their relevance to contemporary policy debates.

**Application:** The lecture series should be structured around analytical problems, not chronological biography. Begin with the contemporary problem — why do fourteen African countries still use a currency designed in Paris? — and then trace the history of analysis backward. Show that Pouemi mapped the operations account mechanism in 1980 with technical precision that anticipates current scholarship. Show that Amin's theory of unequal exchange provided the structural framework for understanding why monetary subordination is systemic, not accidental. Show that Nkrumah's advocacy for continental monetary institutions was rooted in a practical analysis of how former colonial powers maintained economic control through monetary architecture. Then bring the analysis forward: show how Sylla, Kaboub, Koddenbrock, and others have extended the tradition with new data, new institutional analysis, and new theoretical tools (including MMT). The framing avoids hagiography because it evaluates each thinker's analytical contribution critically — noting where proposals were impractical or where analysis was incomplete — while demonstrating the cumulative power of the tradition. It avoids dismissal because it shows that the tradition produced original analytical insights that anticipated mainstream discoveries by decades. The burden of proof falls where it should: on those who maintain the current architecture despite sixty years of rigorous critique.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Present the Pan-African monetary tradition as purely political or ideological rather than analytically rigorous.
**Why:** Nkrumah was a politician, but his monetary analysis was grounded in institutional reality. Pouemi was a trained economist whose work meets academic standards of technical precision. Amin was one of the most cited development economists of the twentieth century. Treating their monetary analysis as "political" — in contrast to the supposedly "technical" and "apolitical" mainstream — reproduces the epistemic hierarchy that the tradition itself critiques.

**Don't:** Romanticise the tradition as a golden age of African economic thought that was simply suppressed by external forces.
**Why:** The tradition had internal disagreements, analytical gaps, and proposals that did not account for changing global conditions. Pouemi's work was indeed suppressed, but some proposals from the tradition failed to gain traction for substantive analytical reasons as well as political ones. Honest engagement with the tradition — acknowledging its achievements and its limitations — strengthens it. Uncritical celebration weakens it by exempting it from the rigorous scrutiny its practitioners would have demanded.
