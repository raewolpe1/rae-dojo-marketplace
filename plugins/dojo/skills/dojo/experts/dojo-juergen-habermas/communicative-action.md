# Communicative Action

## When to Use
- When a decision-making process needs to be assessed for its communicative quality: is it genuinely oriented toward mutual understanding, or is it strategically oriented toward predetermined outcomes?
- When the distinction between genuine deliberation and performative consultation is at stake.
- When *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1981) provides the framework.

## Core Concept
Habermas distinguishes two fundamental orientations of human action. Strategic action is oriented toward success — the actor aims to achieve their predetermined goal by influencing others through incentives, threats, or manipulation. Communicative action is oriented toward understanding — the actors aim to reach agreement through the exchange of reasons, with each participant genuinely open to being persuaded by the better argument.

The distinction is not between good and bad intentions but between two different logics. The market operates through strategic action: the buyer and seller each pursue their own interest, and the outcome is determined by relative bargaining power. Democratic deliberation should operate through communicative action: citizens exchange reasons about the common good, and the outcome is determined by the quality of the arguments.

The pathology arises when strategic action invades domains that should be communicative. The consultation that is actually a legitimation exercise, the stakeholder engagement that is actually a public relations strategy, the participatory process that is designed to produce a predetermined outcome — all are strategic action disguised as communicative action. The diagnosis is essential for development practice because development is saturated with processes that claim to be communicative (participatory, consultative, deliberative) while operating strategically (the outcome was decided before the process began).

## How to Apply
1. **Test for genuine openness.** "Could the outcome of this process be different from what the organisers intended? If the answer is no — if the outcome is predetermined — the process is strategic regardless of its communicative appearance."
2. **Check for reason-giving.** "Are participants offering reasons for their positions? Are those reasons being heard, considered, and potentially acted upon? Or are participants merely stating positions while the powerful decide?"
3. **Assess power distribution.** "Who controls the agenda, the information, the framing, and the outcome? If one party controls all of these, the process is strategic — the communicative form conceals the strategic substance."
4. **Look for the better argument.** "Is the process structured so that the better argument can prevail — regardless of who offers it? Or is the process structured so that the argument of the more powerful prevails?"

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor organises a "joint review mission" with the government and civil society to assess a programme. The review agenda is set by the donor, the analytical framework is the donor's, the data is collected by the donor's consultants, and the review conclusions are drafted by the donor before the joint session. Civil society participants are given thirty minutes at the end to "share their perspectives."
**Application:** "The joint review is strategic action in communicative clothing. The agenda, framework, data, and conclusions are controlled by the donor — the outcome was determined before the 'joint' session. Civil society's thirty minutes is the performative gesture that allows the process to be described as 'participatory.' The communicative test: could civil society's input change the review's conclusions? If the conclusions were drafted before the session, the answer is no — and the process is strategic. A genuinely communicative joint review would: allow all parties to shape the agenda; use an analytical framework developed jointly; include data collected by government and civil society as well as by the donor; and leave the conclusions open until the joint deliberation has occurred. The thirty minutes at the end is not participation — it is the communicative minimum that allows strategic action to claim democratic legitimacy."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present communicative action as an empirical description.
**Why:** Most actual communication mixes communicative and strategic elements. Communicative action is a normative standard against which actual processes are measured — not a description of how people actually communicate.

**Don't:** Dismiss the concept as impractical.
**Why:** The concept provides the diagnostic: is this process genuine or performative? Without the distinction between communicative and strategic action, the performative process cannot be named.
