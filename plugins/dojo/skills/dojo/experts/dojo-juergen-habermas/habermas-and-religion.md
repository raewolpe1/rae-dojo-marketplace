# Habermas and Religion

## When to Use
- When religious convictions, institutions, or communities play a role in public deliberation and the question is how to include them without either privileging or excluding them.
- When the post-secular condition — the persistence and resurgence of religion in societies that expected secularisation — requires a principled response.
- When *An Awareness of What Is Missing* (2010) and Habermas's dialogue with Joseph Ratzinger provide the framework.

## Core Concept
Habermas's later work represents a significant development in his thinking about religion. In his earlier work, the secularisation thesis was largely assumed: modern societies would become progressively secular, and religion would retreat to the private sphere. By the early 2000s, Habermas acknowledged that this thesis was empirically wrong. Religion persists, not as a residue of pre-modernity but as a living force in contemporary societies — including societies that are otherwise thoroughly modern. The post-secular society is one that has adjusted to the continued existence of religious communities in an increasingly secularised environment.

This acknowledgment required a revision of his political philosophy. The revised position, articulated in *An Awareness of What Is Missing* (2010) and in his dialogue with Cardinal Ratzinger (2004), holds that religious citizens have the right and the obligation to participate in public deliberation. Their religious convictions are not to be excluded from the public sphere — such exclusion would violate the principle of inclusion that deliberative democracy requires. But religious claims, when they enter the formal political process (legislation, policy, judicial reasoning), must be translated into publicly accessible language — language that does not presuppose the truth of a particular religious tradition and that can, in principle, be evaluated by all citizens regardless of their religious commitments.

The translation requirement is not a demand that religious citizens suppress their convictions. It is a demand that the reasons offered in formal political deliberation be accessible to all. A citizen may argue against euthanasia on the basis of the sanctity of life — this is a religious claim. In public deliberation, the claim must be translated: what does the sanctity of life mean in terms that non-religious citizens can engage with? Perhaps it becomes an argument about the social consequences of normalising assisted death, or about the protection of vulnerable persons, or about the duty of care in medical ethics. The translation preserves the moral insight while rendering it publicly accessible.

Habermas acknowledges the asymmetry: the translation burden falls on religious citizens, not on secular ones. He argues that secular citizens have a complementary obligation — to remain open to the possibility that religious traditions contain moral insights that secular reason has not yet articulated. The post-secular society requires a mutual learning process: religious citizens translate; secular citizens listen with the expectation that they may learn.

For development practice, this framework is essential because development operates in contexts where religious institutions are among the most influential social actors — providing education, healthcare, social cohesion, and moral frameworks. Excluding religious voices from governance deliberation is both practically impossible and normatively unjustifiable. But allowing religious claims to determine public policy without translation violates the rights of citizens who do not share the religious commitment. The Habermasian framework navigates this tension: include religious citizens, honour their convictions, require translation into publicly accessible reasons for formal political decisions.

## How to Apply
1. **Include religious citizens.** "Religious communities and their representatives have the right to participate in public deliberation. Excluding them violates the principle of inclusion — and in most development contexts, it is practically impossible since religious institutions are deeply embedded in social life."
2. **Require translation for formal decisions.** "When religious claims enter the formal political process — legislation, policy design, programme governance — they must be translated into publicly accessible reasons. The translation does not suppress the conviction; it renders the reasons accessible to all affected citizens."
3. **Impose the complementary obligation.** "Secular actors in the deliberative process must remain genuinely open to the moral insights of religious traditions. The obligation to translate is matched by an obligation to listen — not with condescension but with the expectation of learning."
4. **Distinguish public sphere from formal politics.** "In the informal public sphere (media, civil society, public debate), religious citizens may speak in their own vocabulary without translation. The translation requirement applies to the formal institutional filter — the point at which public opinion becomes binding political decision."

## Examples
**Situation:** A health programme promotes reproductive health services in a region where religious leaders are influential. Religious leaders oppose certain services on theological grounds. The programme team is divided: some argue that the religious leaders should be excluded from programme governance because their objections are faith-based rather than evidence-based; others argue that excluding them will undermine the programme's community acceptance.
**Application:** "Both positions are inadequate. Excluding religious leaders violates inclusion and guarantees community resistance. Including them without requiring translation allows theological claims to veto public health policy without engaging in public reasoning. The Habermasian approach: include religious leaders in programme governance as legitimate participants. Their moral concerns — about dignity, family, the sanctity of life — are genuine and may contain insights that technical health programming misses. But when their objections enter formal programme decisions, they must be translated: what specific harms do the religious leaders foresee? What evidence supports those concerns? What alternative approaches might address both public health objectives and the moral concerns? The programme should also impose the complementary obligation on its own staff: listen to the religious leaders' concerns with genuine openness, not as obstacles to be managed. The religious tradition's emphasis on dignity and relational context may reveal limitations in a purely technical approach to reproductive health that the programme has not considered."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Exclude religious voices from public deliberation.
**Why:** Exclusion violates the principle of inclusion and, in most development contexts, guarantees failure. Religious institutions are deeply embedded in social life — they cannot be bypassed without losing the communicative infrastructure of the communities development serves.

**Don't:** Accept untranslated religious claims as grounds for public policy.
**Why:** A policy justified solely by appeal to religious authority cannot be accepted by citizens who do not share that authority. The translation requirement protects the rights of all citizens while honouring the moral convictions of religious citizens.
