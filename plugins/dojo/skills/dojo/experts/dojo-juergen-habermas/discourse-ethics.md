# Discourse Ethics

## When to Use
- When a norm, policy, or institutional rule needs to be assessed for its validity — not merely its legality or conventional acceptance but whether those affected could rationally agree to it.
- When distinguishing between norms imposed by power or tradition and norms that could withstand the test of rational discourse.
- When *Moral Consciousness and Communicative Action* (1990) provides the framework.

## Core Concept
Discourse ethics, as Habermas formulates it in *Moral Consciousness and Communicative Action* (1990), provides a procedural test for the validity of moral norms. It rests on two principles. The discourse principle (D): only those norms are valid that could meet with the agreement of all affected persons in a practical discourse. The universalisation principle (U): a norm is valid when the consequences and side effects that would result from its general observance for the satisfaction of each person's interests can be accepted by all affected.

The key word is *procedural*. Discourse ethics does not prescribe the content of valid norms — it does not tell you what the right answer is. It prescribes the process through which valid norms can be identified: a discourse in which all affected persons participate freely, offer reasons, consider the consequences for everyone, and reach agreement. The validity of the norm depends entirely on the quality of the discourse — not on its correspondence to an external moral standard.

This is a demanding test. It requires that all affected persons could agree — not merely that the majority agrees, not merely that the powerful agree, not merely that experts agree. A norm that benefits the majority at the expense of a minority is not valid under discourse ethics unless the minority could also accept it in a rational discourse. A development policy that improves aggregate welfare but concentrates costs on specific communities lacks validity unless those communities could rationally accept the distribution of costs and benefits.

The test is counterfactual but not hypothetical. Habermas does not ask: "What would rational persons agree to in a thought experiment?" He asks: "Could the affected persons actually agree to this if they deliberated under conditions approximating the ideal speech situation?" This means that real discourses — actual deliberative processes involving those affected — are required, not philosophical speculation about what rational agents might choose behind a veil of ignorance.

For development practice, discourse ethics provides the decisive test: could the people affected by this programme, this policy, this intervention accept the norms governing it — the targeting criteria, the benefit distribution, the implementation approach — if they participated in a genuine discourse about it? If the answer is no, the norms lack validity regardless of their technical justification.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify all affected.** "Who bears the consequences and side effects of this norm or decision? All affected persons have a right to participate in the discourse — not merely those the programme considers beneficiaries but also those who bear costs, risks, or unintended consequences."
2. **Test for rational acceptability.** "Could the affected persons accept this norm if they participated in a discourse free from domination? If the norm distributes benefits to some and costs to others, could the cost-bearers accept the distribution as justified — or does it survive only because they were not consulted?"
3. **Insist on real discourse.** "Discourse ethics requires actual discourse, not hypothetical reasoning. The programme designer imagining what communities would want is not a discourse — it is an expert substituting their judgment for the affected persons' deliberation."
4. **Distinguish validity from acceptance.** "A norm is not valid because people accept it — people may accept norms out of habit, ignorance, or coercion. A norm is valid because people could accept it after genuine deliberation. The test is rational acceptability, not empirical acceptance."

## Examples
**Situation:** A climate adaptation programme targets the most vulnerable communities for relocation. The targeting criteria were developed by technical experts using vulnerability indices. Communities were informed of the criteria but did not participate in defining them. Some communities contest the indices, arguing that the criteria miss dimensions of vulnerability that matter to them.
**Application:** "The targeting criteria lack discursive validity. They may be technically sound — the vulnerability indices may capture measurable dimensions of risk — but they were produced without the participation of those affected. The communities' contestation is not resistance to be managed; it is a validity claim. They are saying: the criteria do not capture what vulnerability means to us. Discourse ethics requires that the criteria be submitted to a discourse in which the affected communities participate — not to validate the expert criteria but to test whether the criteria could be accepted by those who will live with the consequences. The communities may identify dimensions of vulnerability the indices miss: social networks that provide informal insurance, cultural attachments to place that the indices do not measure, differential impacts within communities that aggregate indices conceal. The technically optimal criteria are not necessarily the discursively valid criteria — and only the latter have legitimacy."

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Apply discourse ethics as a thought experiment.
**Why:** Discourse ethics requires real discourses with real participants. A programme designer reasoning about what communities would want is not discourse ethics — it is paternalism with a philosophical veneer.

**Don't:** Confuse empirical consensus with discursive validity.
**Why:** People may agree to norms under coercion, out of habit, or through manipulation. Validity requires that agreement could be reached under conditions of genuine discourse — not that it has been expressed under actual conditions.
