# Enlightenment Use of Evaluation

## When to Use
- When explaining Weiss's most original contribution to the evaluation utilisation literature.
- When helping users reframe their expectations about how evaluation findings influence the world.
- When someone is discouraged because their evaluation findings did not lead to immediate policy change.

## Fails When
- **You present enlightenment as a lesser or failed form of use.** Weiss's argument is precisely the opposite: enlightenment may be the most consequential pathway of evaluation influence, precisely because it shapes the assumptions that underlie many decisions rather than influencing a single decision.
- **You describe enlightenment as uninfluenceable.** While diffuse, enlightenment is shaped by the quality, clarity, and conceptual power of evaluation findings. Evaluators can design for it.

## Core Concept
Enlightenment is Weiss's term for the process by which evaluation and research findings gradually percolate into the general climate of informed opinion and reshape the assumptions, concepts, and causal understandings that policy-makers bring to their decisions. It is not the use of a specific finding for a specific decision. It is the slow accumulation of knowledge that changes what everybody "knows" — the taken-for-granted assumptions of the policy community.

Weiss observed that when she asked policy-makers whether they used evaluation findings, many said no — they could not point to a specific evaluation that changed a specific decision. But when she examined the ideas and assumptions that shaped their decisions, she found that these had shifted over time in ways that tracked the cumulative findings of evaluation research. The concept of early childhood as a critical developmental window, the understanding that punitive drug policies produce diminishing returns, the recognition that job training without labour demand produces disappointment — these are ideas that entered policy consciousness through the slow accumulation of evidence, not through any single decisive study.

Enlightenment operates through what Weiss calls "knowledge creep" — findings seep into the thinking of journalists, advocates, programme designers, legislative staff, and eventually elected officials, often without attribution to specific studies. A policy-maker who "knows" that early intervention works is drawing on decades of accumulated research without necessarily being able to cite a single study. The specific findings have been absorbed into the background assumptions of the policy domain.

This concept has profound implications for how evaluators think about their work. If the primary pathway of influence is enlightenment rather than instrumental use, then the most important quality of an evaluation is not whether it reaches a specific decision-maker at a specific moment, but whether it contributes clear, powerful, conceptually coherent findings to the cumulative body of knowledge in its domain. The evaluation that clearly articulates why a mechanism works — even if no single decision follows — may ultimately influence more decisions than the evaluation that produces a timely finding for a single policy choice.

Enlightenment also explains why evaluation sometimes appears useless in the short term but valuable in the long term. The individual evaluation that seems to disappear without trace may be contributing to a gradual shift in understanding that eventually transforms the policy landscape. This is cold comfort for evaluators who want immediate impact, but it is an accurate description of how most social knowledge influences social action.

## How to Apply
1. **Design for conceptual clarity.** If enlightenment operates through ideas rather than specific findings, then the clarity and power of your conceptual contribution matters more than statistical precision. What new way of thinking about the problem does your evaluation offer?
2. **Communicate to multiple audiences.** Enlightenment operates through diffusion, not through single-channel delivery. Disseminate through academic publications, policy briefs, practitioner conferences, media, and informal networks.
3. **Contribute to cumulative knowledge.** Position your evaluation as part of a body of evidence. Synthesise with prior findings. Make the cumulative case explicit.
4. **Name the mechanisms clearly.** Findings that name a mechanism or explain a causal process travel further through enlightenment pathways than findings that simply report outcomes. "Mentoring works through network brokerage" is a finding that reshapes thinking; "mentoring produced a 5% improvement" is a finding that reports a number.
5. **Be patient but persistent.** Enlightenment is slow. Continue contributing to the discourse even when immediate use is not visible.

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluator has completed a series of evaluations of early childhood development (ECD) programmes in South Africa over several years, none of which produced immediate policy changes.
**Application:** Weiss's enlightenment concept reframes this apparent failure as potential long-term success. Each evaluation contributed findings about the mechanisms of early development, the critical periods for intervention, and the conditions under which ECD programmes produce lasting effects. Over time, these findings — combined with international evidence — percolated into the assumptions of the National Development Plan, the ECD policy framework, and the thinking of provincial education departments. No single evaluation "caused" the policy shift toward prioritising ECD. But the cumulative body of evaluation evidence changed what South African policy-makers took for granted about early childhood — and this shift in assumptions has shaped resource allocation decisions worth far more than any single programme decision.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use enlightenment as an excuse for poor evaluation design.
**Why:** The fact that evaluation influence is often diffuse does not mean that quality does not matter. Poorly designed evaluations contribute noise, not enlightenment. Rigorous, clearly communicated findings are what drive knowledge creep.

**Don't:** Promise commissioners enlightenment as a guaranteed outcome.
**Why:** Enlightenment is a description of how evaluation influence typically works, not a result that can be planned and delivered. The evaluator can design for it but cannot guarantee it.
