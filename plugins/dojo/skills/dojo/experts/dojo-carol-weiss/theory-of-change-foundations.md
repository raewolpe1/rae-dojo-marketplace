# Theory of Change Foundations

## When to Use
- When explaining what a theory of change is, why it matters, and how it differs from other planning and evaluation tools.
- When helping users construct, articulate, or refine a theory of change.
- When someone conflates theory of change with a logframe, results chain, or programme diagram.

## Fails When
- **You reduce theory of change to a diagram.** The diagram is a representation of the theory, not the theory itself. The theory is a set of causal hypotheses — testable claims about how and why programme activities are expected to produce intended outcomes. A theory of change without causal reasoning is a flowchart.
- **You treat theory of change as a compliance exercise.** When theory of change becomes something programmes produce because funders require it, the intellectual content drains away. What remains is a rationalised description of existing activities, not a genuine articulation of causal logic.

## Core Concept
A theory of change is a causal argument. It states: if we do X, under conditions C, then Y will occur, because of mechanism M. It makes explicit the chain of assumptions connecting programme activities to intended outcomes, specifying at each link what must happen and why.

The concept has its roots in programme theory, which Weiss developed as the analytical foundation for theory-based evaluation. The argument is straightforward: programmes embody theories about how change occurs. A skills training programme assumes that training produces skills, that skills produce employability, and that employability produces employment. Each assumption is a hypothesis. Each can be right or wrong. The evaluator's job is to make these hypotheses explicit and then test them.

A theory of change differs from a logframe or results framework in a critical respect: it specifies mechanisms. A logframe shows activities, outputs, outcomes, and impacts in a linear sequence. A theory of change asks why each connection holds — what mechanism links the activity to the output, the output to the outcome. Without mechanisms, you have a description of hoped-for sequences, not an explanation of how change is expected to occur.

The practical value is diagnostic. When a programme fails to achieve its outcomes, a well-specified theory of change tells you where the failure occurred: Was the activity delivered but the mechanism did not operate? Did the mechanism operate but the contextual conditions were wrong? Did the intermediate outcome occur but fail to produce the final outcome? Each diagnosis implies a different corrective response. Without the theory, you know only that the programme "did not work" — which tells you almost nothing about what to do next.

A good theory of change has several properties: it is explicit (stated clearly enough to be tested), it specifies mechanisms (not just sequences), it identifies assumptions (the conditions that must hold at each link), and it is falsifiable (you can specify what evidence would show the theory is wrong).

## How to Apply
1. **Start with the problem, not the activities.** What is the problem the programme addresses? What are its causes? The theory of change should explain how the programme intervenes in the causal structure of the problem.
2. **Specify the causal chain.** What sequence of changes does the programme expect to produce? Articulate each step, from activities through intermediate outcomes to final outcomes.
3. **Identify the mechanism at each link.** For each connection in the chain, ask: why should this activity produce this outcome? What is the mechanism? If you cannot specify a mechanism, you have identified a gap in your theory.
4. **State the assumptions.** At each link, what conditions must hold for the connection to work? These are the programme's assumptions, and they are potential failure points.
5. **Test for falsifiability.** Can you specify what you would observe if the theory is wrong? If not, the theory is not yet evaluable.

## Examples
**Situation:** A provincial department in the Eastern Cape asks an evaluator to help them develop a theory of change for a school nutrition programme.
**Application:** The evaluator does not start with the programme's activities. She starts with the problem: why are children in these schools undernourished? Because household food security is inadequate. Because the school day is long and children arrive without breakfast. Because nutritional knowledge is limited. Each cause implies a different intervention logic. The school feeding component addresses in-school hunger directly; the nutrition education component assumes that knowledge changes feeding practices at home. These are different causal chains with different mechanisms and different assumptions. The theory of change makes them explicit — and makes clear that measuring only "number of meals served" misses the most interesting questions about whether the programme's theory of household-level change is correct.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present theory of change as a one-time planning exercise completed before implementation.
**Why:** Theory of change is a living analytical tool. As implementation proceeds and evidence accumulates, the theory should be tested, refined, and if necessary revised. A theory of change that is never updated in light of evidence is a decoration, not a tool.

**Don't:** Produce a theory of change with thirty boxes and fifty arrows.
**Why:** Complexity in the diagram often signals confusion in the thinking. If the core causal logic cannot be stated clearly in a few linked propositions, the evaluator does not yet understand the programme well enough. Simplify until the causal argument is clear, then add necessary complexity.
