# Weiss vs Results-Based Management

## When to Use
- When someone asks about the relationship between Weiss's approach and results-based management (RBM).
- When critiquing the logframe or results framework from a theory-based evaluation perspective.
- When helping users understand why measuring outputs and outcomes without theory produces limited evaluation.

## Fails When
- **You reject everything about RBM.** RBM contributed the important discipline of specifying expected results and measuring progress toward them. The problem is not measurement but the absence of causal theory.
- **You present Weiss as opposing measurement.** She does not. She opposes measurement without theory. Measurement structured by programme theory is exactly what she advocates.

## Core Concept
Results-based management (RBM) and its operational tool, the logical framework (logframe), have dominated development evaluation since the 1990s. RBM asks programmes to specify expected results at multiple levels — outputs, outcomes, impacts — and to measure progress against these targets. The logframe arranges these in a hierarchy with assumptions and indicators at each level.

Weiss's theory-based evaluation shares RBM's commitment to specifying expected results but diverges in a critical respect: it demands causal reasoning, not just result specification. The logframe asks "what results do you expect?" Theory-based evaluation asks "why do you expect these results? Through what mechanisms? Under what conditions? What assumptions connect your activities to your outcomes?"

The difference is not academic. Consider a logframe that specifies: Activity: deliver teacher training; Output: 500 teachers trained; Outcome: improved student learning; Impact: improved educational attainment. This is a perfectly structured results framework. But it tells you nothing about how teacher training is supposed to improve learning. Is it through new pedagogical techniques? Through increased subject knowledge? Through changed attitudes toward students? Through improved classroom management? Each implies a different training design and a different evaluation focus.

When student learning does not improve despite teacher training being delivered, the logframe-based evaluation can report a gap between output and outcome. But it cannot explain the gap because it never specified the mechanism. Was the training content wrong? Were the mechanisms through which training affects practice not operating? Were the conditions for applying new methods absent in the classroom? The logframe does not help you answer these questions because it never asked them.

Theory-based evaluation insists on specifying the causal mechanisms at each link — turning the logframe from a results tracking tool into a testable theory about how change occurs. This adds analytical work upfront but produces dramatically more useful findings when things go wrong (as they often do).

The deeper problem with RBM as typically practised is that it incentivises reporting on targets rather than learning about mechanisms. When programmes are assessed primarily on whether they hit their output and outcome targets, the system drives measurement toward demonstrating results rather than understanding processes. Theory-based evaluation shifts the question from "did we hit the target?" to "did our theory of change hold, and where it did not, why?"

## How to Apply
1. **Supplement, do not replace.** Add causal theory to the existing results framework. Keep the discipline of result specification but add mechanism specification at each link.
2. **Ask "why" at every arrow.** For each connection in the logframe or results chain, ask: why should this lead to that? What is the mechanism? What must be true for this connection to hold?
3. **Measure mechanisms, not just milestones.** Include indicators for mediating variables and mechanisms alongside the standard output and outcome indicators.
4. **Reframe failure as learning.** When targets are not met, use the theory of change to diagnose where the chain broke — not just to report a gap.
5. **Distinguish compliance from evaluation.** Results reporting for accountability purposes (compliance) is different from theory-based evaluation for learning purposes. Both are legitimate, but confusing them produces neither good accountability nor good learning.

## Examples
**Situation:** A donor requires a logframe for a rural water programme in Mpumalanga. The evaluator wants to apply theory-based evaluation but must work within the RBM framework.
**Application:** The logframe specifies: output — water points constructed; outcome — increased access to clean water; impact — reduced waterborne disease. The evaluator supplements this with theory of change questions: does constructing water points automatically produce access (or do distance, queue times, social exclusion, and maintenance failures intervene)? Does access to clean water reduce disease (or do hygiene practices, sanitation, and food handling dominate the disease pathway)? Each question identifies a mechanism and an assumption. The evaluator adds mediating indicators: distance to water point, queue time, maintenance status, hygiene practice scores, sanitation access. When waterborne disease does not decline despite water point construction, the theory-based approach diagnoses the failure at the hygiene-and-sanitation link, not at the water-access link. The logframe alone would report an outcome gap without explanation.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Blame RBM for problems caused by bureaucratic implementation rather than the framework itself.
**Why:** RBM as a management approach has value. The problem is when it crowds out causal reasoning and reduces evaluation to target-tracking. The critique should target the absence of theory, not the presence of targets.

**Don't:** Present theory of change as simply a better logframe.
**Why:** Theory of change is a fundamentally different intellectual exercise — it is about causal reasoning, not result tracking. Presenting it as a logframe upgrade invites superficial adoption: new labels on old boxes.
