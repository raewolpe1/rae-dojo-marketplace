# Evaluation Utilisation Pathways

## When to Use
- When discussing the broader field of evaluation utilisation research.
- When helping users understand the multiple factors that affect whether and how evaluation findings are used.
- When designing evaluations and communication strategies with utilisation in mind.

## Fails When
- **You present utilisation as primarily a communication problem.** While communication matters, the primary determinants of evaluation use are structural, political, and organisational — not communicative.
- **You treat process use as a substitute for findings-based use.** Process use (learning that occurs through participating in evaluation) is real and valuable, but it does not replace the need for rigorous findings that test programme theory.

## Core Concept
Evaluation utilisation research — the study of how, when, and why evaluation findings are used or not used — is one of Weiss's major intellectual contributions. Her work laid the foundation for a research stream that has identified multiple factors affecting evaluation use, multiple types of use, and multiple pathways through which evaluation influence operates.

Weiss's framework for understanding utilisation rests on several key insights. First, the distinction among types of use (instrumental, conceptual, enlightenment, symbolic, political) reveals that "use" is not a single phenomenon. Evaluations that appear unused by one criterion may be highly influential by another. Second, the factors that affect use are multiple and interact: the quality and relevance of findings, the timeliness of the evaluation, the credibility of the evaluator, the political context, organisational receptivity, the presence of champions, the alignment of findings with existing beliefs and interests, and the availability of actionable alternatives.

Weiss identifies several consistent findings from the utilisation research. **Relevance matters more than methodological sophistication.** Findings that address questions decision-makers are actually grappling with are more likely to be used than methodologically elegant findings on topics of peripheral interest. **Timeliness matters.** Evaluation findings that arrive after the decision window has closed cannot influence that decision, regardless of quality. **Credibility of the source matters.** Findings from evaluators perceived as independent and competent carry more weight than findings from evaluators perceived as biased or incompetent. **Existing beliefs matter.** Findings that confirm existing beliefs are adopted more readily than findings that challenge them — a pattern Weiss notes with concern because it reduces evaluation's capacity to challenge assumptions.

The most important barrier to use, in Weiss's analysis, is not methodological quality or communication effectiveness but **the structure of decision-making itself.** Decisions in government and organisations are rarely made at identifiable moments by identifiable decision-makers acting on identifiable evidence. Decisions emerge from complex, iterative, multi-actor processes in which evaluation evidence is one input among many. This structural reality limits instrumental use but creates space for conceptual use and enlightenment.

Weiss also identifies the conditions under which use is most likely. Evaluations are more likely to be used when there is a genuine information gap (decision-makers do not already know the answer), when the political context permits action on the findings, when the findings are clear and actionable, when there is a champion within the decision-making system who values and promotes the findings, and when the evaluation was perceived as credible and relevant by its intended users.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose the utilisation context before designing the evaluation.** What type of use is most realistic? What are the structural, political, and organisational factors likely to affect use? Design accordingly.
2. **Invest in relevance.** Ensure evaluation questions address genuine information needs — not just interesting academic questions.
3. **Build credibility.** Independence, methodological rigour, and transparent reporting build the credibility that supports use.
4. **Create multiple dissemination channels.** Do not rely on a single report to a single commissioner. Ensure findings reach multiple audiences through multiple channels.
5. **Identify and cultivate champions.** Within the decision-making system, identify individuals who value evidence and are positioned to promote findings. These champions amplify evaluation influence.

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluation of a public health programme in Gauteng produces rigorous findings showing mixed results — some components effective, others not. The evaluation report is submitted and nothing appears to change.
**Application:** Weiss's utilisation framework provides a systematic diagnosis. First, what type of use was expected? If instrumental use (specific programme changes), check: were the findings actionable? Were there specific recommendations? Was there a decision window? Was there a champion within the department? If conceptual use was the realistic expectation, look more carefully: has the discourse about the programme changed? Are department officials thinking differently about programme design, even if no formal decision has been made? The mixed findings may be particularly suited to conceptual use — they provide nuance rather than a verdict, which is what changes thinking. For the future, the evaluator should build relationships with internal champions, time dissemination to coincide with planning cycles, and communicate the conceptual framework clearly — not just the numbers.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Measure evaluation success solely by instrumental use.
**Why:** This sets an unrealistic standard that most evaluations will fail. Evaluate evaluation influence across all use types — instrumental, conceptual, enlightenment, and process.

**Don't:** Assume that non-use is always a failure of the evaluation.
**Why:** Non-use may reflect political constraints, organisational inertia, or structural features of decision-making that no evaluation can overcome. Sometimes the evaluation was rigorous, relevant, and well-communicated, and the political context simply did not permit action. Weiss would call this a feature of the system, not a failure of the evaluation.
