# Mechanisms and Mediators

## When to Use
- When explaining why evaluations should measure intermediate causal processes, not just endpoints.
- When helping users identify the mechanisms through which a programme is expected to produce change.
- When diagnosing programme outcomes by tracing the causal chain through mediating variables.

## Fails When
- **You confuse mechanisms with activities.** An activity is what the programme does. A mechanism is the causal process through which the activity produces change. Training is an activity; skill acquisition is a mechanism; increased self-efficacy is a mechanism; changed employer perceptions are a mechanism. These are different things, and collapsing them obscures the analysis.
- **You use "mechanism" as a vague gesture toward causation.** A mechanism must be specific enough to be testable. "The programme works through empowerment" is not a mechanism statement — it is a label. What specifically changes in the participant, and how does that change connect to the outcome?

## Core Concept
The distinction between mechanisms and mediators is central to Weiss's theory-based evaluation. A **mechanism** is the causal process through which a programme activity produces a change. A **mediator** is a variable that lies on the causal pathway between the programme activity and the final outcome — it is the observable indicator that the mechanism has operated.

Consider a programme that provides mentoring to first-generation university students. The programme activity is mentoring sessions. The intended outcome is degree completion. But what connects the two? Several possible mechanisms: the mentor provides academic guidance that improves study strategies (mechanism: skill transfer). The mentor provides emotional support that sustains motivation during difficult periods (mechanism: psychosocial support). The mentor connects the student to institutional resources they would not otherwise access (mechanism: network brokerage). The mentor serves as a role model who makes degree completion seem attainable (mechanism: self-efficacy enhancement). Each of these is a different causal process. Each predicts different mediating variables: study habits, persistence through difficulty, use of institutional resources, self-efficacy scores. And each implies a different mentoring programme design.

Measuring only the endpoint — did the student complete the degree? — tells you almost nothing about how to improve the programme. If students who received mentoring completed at higher rates, you do not know which mechanism produced the effect. If they completed at the same rate, you do not know whether all mechanisms failed or whether some operated while others did not. Measuring mediating variables opens the black box. If self-efficacy increased but study habits did not change, the mentoring is working through one mechanism but not another. If all mediators shifted but degree completion did not improve, the theory of change is wrong at a higher link — perhaps the barriers to completion lie elsewhere (financial, family, institutional).

Weiss argues that evaluators should routinely identify the key mechanisms their programme assumes and design measurement strategies for the corresponding mediating variables. This is what makes evaluation theory-based rather than merely outcome-based.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify the assumed mechanisms.** For each link in the theory of change, ask: through what process does this activity produce this change? Name the mechanism specifically.
2. **Specify observable mediators.** For each mechanism, ask: what would we observe if this mechanism is operating? These observable indicators are the mediating variables.
3. **Design measurement for mediators.** Include mediating variable measurement in the evaluation design alongside outcome measurement. This may require different methods — qualitative methods for understanding mechanisms, quantitative methods for measuring mediator levels.
4. **Analyse the chain.** When results come in, trace the causal chain: did the mechanism operate (did the mediators change)? If so, did the mediator change translate into outcome change? Where the chain breaks, you have located the failure point.
5. **Report mechanism-level findings.** Do not collapse the analysis into a single "the programme worked / did not work" verdict. Report what mechanisms operated, which mediators changed, and where the chain held or broke.

## Examples
**Situation:** A youth development programme in the Western Cape provides life skills training and expects it to reduce risky behaviour among adolescents.
**Application:** The programme's theory assumes at least two mechanisms: knowledge (participants learn about risks and consequences) and self-efficacy (participants develop confidence in their ability to make different choices). Mediating variables for the first mechanism include risk knowledge scores; for the second, self-efficacy scales and reported decision-making in risk scenarios. The evaluation measures these mediators alongside the outcome measures of risky behaviour. If knowledge increases but self-efficacy does not, and risky behaviour does not decline, the evaluation reveals that the programme's information-based mechanism is operating but is insufficient — the barrier is not ignorance but agency. This finding has direct implications for programme redesign: add components that build decision-making capacity, not just knowledge.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** List "empowerment" or "capacity building" as mechanisms without specification.
**Why:** These are labels, not mechanisms. What specifically changes? Decision-making authority? Access to resources? Skill levels? Confidence? Each implies a different causal process and a different measurement strategy. Vague mechanism labels produce vague evaluations.

**Don't:** Assume there is only one mechanism per programme activity.
**Why:** Most programme activities operate through multiple mechanisms simultaneously. Identifying and distinguishing among them is analytically difficult but necessary. If multiple mechanisms are operating, they may reinforce or undermine each other, and understanding the programme requires understanding their interaction.
