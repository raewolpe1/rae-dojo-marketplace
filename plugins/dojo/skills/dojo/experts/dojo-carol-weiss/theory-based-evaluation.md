# Theory-Based Evaluation

## When to Use
- When explaining theory-based evaluation as an evaluation approach — its logic, procedures, and value.
- When helping users design evaluations that test programme theory rather than merely measuring outcomes.
- When someone asks how theory-based evaluation relates to or differs from other evaluation approaches.

## Fails When
- **You present theory-based evaluation as a method rather than a logic.** Theory-based evaluation is not a specific data collection technique or research design. It is a way of structuring the evaluation around the programme's causal theory. It can use quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods.
- **You treat it as opposed to experimental design.** Theory-based evaluation is not anti-experimental. An RCT that tests the programme's theory — that measures mediating variables and mechanisms along the causal chain — is doing theory-based evaluation. The issue is not the design but what questions the design is structured to answer.

## Core Concept
Theory-based evaluation is Weiss's most distinctive contribution to evaluation methodology. The argument, developed most fully in her 1997 paper "Theory-Based Evaluation: Past, Present, and Future" and elaborated throughout her career, is that evaluation should be structured around the programme's theory of change — the set of causal hypotheses about how and why the programme is expected to produce its intended outcomes.

The conventional approach to evaluation asks: did the programme produce the intended outcomes? This is the "black box" approach — it measures inputs and outcomes without examining what happens in between. Theory-based evaluation opens the black box. It asks: did each step in the programme's causal chain operate as expected? Did the activities produce the intended intermediate outcomes? Did the mechanisms the programme assumed actually function? Did the conditions the theory requires actually obtain?

The practical procedure involves several steps. First, surface the programme's theory of change — the causal chain from activities to outcomes, with the mechanisms and assumptions at each link. This often requires extensive engagement with programme designers, implementers, and stakeholders, because the theory is usually implicit rather than stated. Second, identify the key links in the chain — the critical assumptions and mechanisms that the evaluation should test. Third, design data collection to measure the variables at each link — not just the endpoints but the mediating variables and contextual conditions. Fourth, analyse the data to assess whether each link in the chain held, and where it did not, why.

The value of theory-based evaluation is explanatory power. When a programme fails to achieve its outcomes, a theory-based evaluation can diagnose whether the failure was in implementation (the activities were not delivered as planned), in the programme theory (the activities were delivered but the assumed mechanisms did not operate), or in the context (the mechanisms operated but the conditions required for the final outcomes were absent). Each diagnosis implies a fundamentally different response.

Theory-based evaluation also accumulates knowledge across evaluations. When multiple evaluations test similar theories of change, the evidence builds about which mechanisms work under which conditions — contributing to the broader understanding of how social change occurs.

## How to Apply
1. **Surface the theory first.** Before designing the evaluation, work with programme staff and stakeholders to articulate the theory of change explicitly. Make the causal chain, mechanisms, and assumptions visible.
2. **Prioritise the links.** Not every link in the chain needs equal evaluation attention. Identify which links are most uncertain, most contested, or most critical to the programme's success. Focus evaluation resources there.
3. **Design for mediating variables.** Ensure your data collection captures the intermediate outcomes and mediating variables, not just the final outcomes. These are what make the evaluation theory-based rather than merely outcome-based.
4. **Use appropriate methods for each link.** Different links may require different methods. Qualitative methods may be best for understanding mechanisms; quantitative methods may be best for measuring outcome changes. The theory structures the design; it does not dictate a single method.
5. **Report findings link by link.** Present the evaluation findings as an assessment of each step in the causal chain. This gives users actionable diagnostic information, not just a verdict.

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluator is commissioned to evaluate a community policing programme in Gauteng that aims to reduce crime through improved police-community relations.
**Application:** The programme's implicit theory is: community policing forums → improved trust between police and community → increased information flow from community to police → better-targeted policing → reduced crime. A conventional evaluation would measure crime rates before and after the programme. A theory-based evaluation measures each link: did the forums actually occur and were they well-attended? Did trust increase (measured through surveys or qualitative evidence)? Did information flow to police increase? Did policing strategies change in response? Did crime patterns shift? If crime did not decrease but trust and information flow did increase, the theory-based evaluation reveals that the failure is at the policing-response link, not at the community-engagement link. This is a fundamentally more useful finding.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Equate theory-based evaluation with simply having a theory of change document.
**Why:** Many evaluations now include a theory of change diagram without actually using it to structure the evaluation. If the theory of change sits in the inception report and the evaluation measures only outcomes, it is not theory-based evaluation. The theory must drive the evaluation questions, the measurement, and the analysis.

**Don't:** Assume that theory-based evaluation is only for formative or process evaluation.
**Why:** Theory-based evaluation is equally applicable to summative evaluation. It provides a more nuanced and useful summative judgment than outcome-only measurement, because it explains what worked, what did not, and why.
