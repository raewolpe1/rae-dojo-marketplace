# Social Experimentation

## When to Use
- When discussing the role of experimental and quasi-experimental designs in programme evaluation.
- When explaining Weiss's nuanced position on the strengths and limitations of RCTs.
- When helping users decide whether an experimental design is appropriate for their evaluation.

## Fails When
- **You present Weiss as opposed to experimental design.** She is not. She wrote appreciatively about the logic of the experiment and recognised its power for establishing causal claims. Her concern is with how experimental design is applied in social programme evaluation and what it can and cannot tell you.
- **You use the limitations of experiments to argue against rigorous causal inference.** Weiss's point is not that causal inference does not matter but that experimental design alone does not answer the most important evaluation questions.

## Core Concept
Weiss engaged seriously with experimental evaluation throughout her career, beginning with her early work on evaluation research in the 1970s. Her position is best described as appreciative but incomplete: she values the experiment's causal logic but argues that it answers only one of the questions that good evaluation must address.

The randomised controlled trial answers one question with considerable power: did the programme produce a difference in outcomes relative to what would have occurred without it? This is the counterfactual question, and the experiment's great strength is its ability to answer it by creating a comparison group through random assignment. Weiss does not dispute this.

Her concern is with what the experiment does not answer. The experiment tells you whether the programme produced an effect but not how it produced it — through what mechanisms, under what conditions, for which participants. It tells you the net effect across the treatment group but not the variation within it — who benefited, who did not, and why. It tells you the effect in the specific context and time of the study but not how the programme would work in different contexts, at different scales, or at different times.

These limitations become critical when evaluation is expected to inform programme improvement and policy learning — not just render a verdict. Knowing that a programme "worked" (or did not) is less useful than knowing how it worked, why it worked, for whom, and under what conditions. This is precisely the territory of theory-based evaluation: it structures the evaluation around the programme's causal theory and tests the links in the chain, including mechanisms and mediating variables.

Weiss argues that experimental design and theory-based evaluation are not competitors but complements. An experiment structured around the programme's theory of change — one that measures mediating variables and mechanisms, not just endpoints — combines the causal power of random assignment with the explanatory power of theory-based evaluation. But an experiment that measures only the endpoint outcome, ignoring the causal chain, produces a result that is rigorous but uninformative.

She also raises practical and ethical concerns about experimentation in social programmes: the political feasibility of withholding services from a control group, the ethical implications of random assignment in contexts of need, the problem of contamination when control group members access services through other channels, and the difficulty of maintaining experimental conditions over the long time horizons of social change.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask what question you need answered.** If the primary question is "does this programme produce better outcomes than no programme?", experimental design is powerful. If the primary question is "how does this programme work and why?", theory-based evaluation is essential — with or without an experimental design.
2. **When using experimental design, measure the causal chain.** Do not rely on endpoint measurement alone. Measure mediating variables, mechanisms, and contextual conditions. This transforms the experiment from a black box evaluation into a theory-testing evaluation.
3. **Consider feasibility and ethics.** Random assignment is not always feasible or ethical. When it is not, quasi-experimental designs, contribution analysis, or theory-based evaluation without a counterfactual comparison may be the best available approaches.
4. **Report heterogeneity.** Average treatment effects may conceal important variation. Examine whether the programme worked differently for different subgroups, and if so, why.
5. **Be modest about external validity.** Experimental results from one context may not generalise to another. The mechanisms identified through theory-based evaluation are often more generalisable than the specific effect sizes from a single experiment.

## Examples
**Situation:** A funder insists on an RCT for a livelihood programme in rural KwaZulu-Natal and wants to know whether Weiss would support this.
**Application:** Weiss would not oppose the RCT but would insist on structuring it around the programme's theory of change. The programme assumes: asset transfer → productive use of assets → increased income → improved food security → improved well-being. The RCT can test whether the treatment group has better outcomes than the control group. But without measuring the causal chain, a positive result tells you only that something worked — not what. A negative result tells you only that something failed — not where. Weiss would design the RCT to measure at each link: did participants actually use the transferred assets productively? Did income increase? Was the increase sufficient to improve food security? She would also examine heterogeneity: did the programme work differently for female-headed versus male-headed households? For participants with prior business experience versus those without? These theory-driven questions transform the RCT from a verdict machine into a learning tool.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Position the debate as "RCTs versus everything else."
**Why:** This is a false dichotomy. The real question is what evaluation logic you are using — black box or theory-based — and what methods best serve that logic. Experimental design is one powerful method; it is not the only rigorous approach, and it is not self-sufficient.

**Don't:** Use Weiss's concerns about experiments to dismiss the importance of counterfactual reasoning.
**Why:** Weiss values counterfactual reasoning. She wants it enriched by theory, not replaced by narrative. An evaluation that cannot address the counterfactual at all has a serious limitation, regardless of how well it describes mechanisms.
