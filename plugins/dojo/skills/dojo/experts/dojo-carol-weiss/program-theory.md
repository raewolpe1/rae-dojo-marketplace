# Programme Theory

## When to Use
- When explaining what programme theory is and why it matters for evaluation.
- When helping users distinguish between the programme's own theory and broader social science theory.
- When someone needs to articulate or surface the causal assumptions embedded in a programme.

## Fails When
- **You treat programme theory as something the evaluator creates.** The evaluator surfaces it, tests it, sometimes refines it — but the theory belongs to the programme. It is the set of assumptions that programme designers and implementers hold about how and why their activities will produce intended outcomes.
- **You confuse programme theory with programme description.** A description tells you what the programme does. A theory tells you why the programme's designers believe those activities will produce the intended changes. The difference is the causal reasoning.

## Core Concept
Programme theory is the set of causal assumptions that a programme embodies about how its activities will produce its intended outcomes. Every programme has a theory, whether or not anyone has ever stated it. A literacy programme assumes that certain teaching methods will produce reading competence. A microcredit programme assumes that access to capital, in the hands of the target population, under certain conditions, will generate income. A conditional cash transfer programme assumes that financial incentives will change household behaviour regarding school attendance or health service use. These assumptions are the programme's theory.

Weiss distinguishes between two components of programme theory. The first is the **implementation theory** — the theory about how the programme's activities will be delivered to the intended participants. Will the services reach the right people? Will they be delivered with adequate quality? Will participation be sufficient? The second is the **programmatic theory** (sometimes called the programme's causal theory) — the theory about how the programme's activities, once delivered, will produce the intended changes. Will the services actually cause the outcomes they are supposed to cause, and through what mechanisms?

This distinction is analytically important because it separates two fundamentally different kinds of programme failure. **Implementation failure** occurs when the programme fails to deliver its activities as intended — the training was not delivered, the participants did not attend, the quality was poor. **Theory failure** occurs when the programme delivers its activities as intended but the assumed causal mechanisms do not operate — the training was delivered well, participants attended, but the skills acquired did not translate into employment because the programme's theory about the labour market was wrong. Confusing these two types of failure leads to wrong responses: redesigning implementation when the theory is flawed, or abandoning a sound theory because of poor implementation.

The evaluator's task is to make the programme theory explicit and then to test it. This requires engagement with programme designers and implementers to understand what they believe will happen and why, followed by systematic measurement of the variables that the theory identifies as critical — not just the final outcomes but the intermediate steps, the mechanisms, and the conditions.

## How to Apply
1. **Interview programme architects.** Ask: why do you believe this programme will produce these outcomes? What has to happen between the activity and the outcome? What assumptions are you making about participants, context, and mechanisms?
2. **Review programme documents critically.** Programme documents often describe activities and list intended outcomes without specifying the causal logic connecting them. Read for the implicit theory — the unstated assumptions about how change occurs.
3. **Distinguish implementation theory from programmatic theory.** Map the delivery logic separately from the causal logic. This allows you to diagnose implementation failure and theory failure independently.
4. **Look for gaps.** Where are the causal assumptions weakest? Where is the connection between activity and outcome most uncertain? These are the evaluation's highest-priority questions.
5. **State the theory in testable propositions.** "If activities A are delivered with quality Q to participants P, then intermediate outcome I will occur through mechanism M, provided conditions C hold." Each element of this statement is a variable that can be measured.

## Examples
**Situation:** A funder asks an evaluator to evaluate a school governance programme in Limpopo that trains school governing bodies (SGBs) in financial management.
**Application:** The programme's implementation theory is that training workshops delivered by accredited trainers will equip SGB members with financial management skills. The programmatic theory is that these skills will lead to improved school financial management, which will lead to better resource allocation, which will lead to improved educational outcomes. The evaluator surfaces these theories and immediately identifies critical assumptions: that SGB members will retain and apply the skills, that they have the authority to implement changes, that school principals will cooperate, and that improved financial management actually translates into better educational outcomes (rather than being consumed by other constraints). Each assumption can be tested. The evaluation design measures not just whether SGB members attended training and whether educational outcomes improved, but whether financial management practices actually changed, whether authority dynamics allowed implementation, and whether resource allocation shifted.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present programme theory as interchangeable with a logframe.
**Why:** A logframe describes expected sequences (inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes → impact). Programme theory explains why those sequences are expected to occur — the causal mechanisms and assumptions. A logframe without causal reasoning is a wish list with arrows.

**Don't:** Assume programme staff always know their own programme theory.
**Why:** Programme theories are often implicit. Staff may know what they do but may never have articulated why they believe it works. Surfacing the theory is often a process of collaborative discovery, not simply asking and recording.
