# Stakeholders and Evaluation

## When to Use
- When discussing how different stakeholder groups influence evaluation.
- When helping users identify and manage competing stakeholder interests.
- When advising on how stakeholder dynamics affect evaluation design, implementation, and use.

## Fails When
- **You treat stakeholder involvement as a unitary good.** Not all stakeholder involvement improves evaluation. Some stakeholder interests conflict with honest inquiry. The evaluator must navigate these interests without being captured by any of them.
- **You ignore power differentials among stakeholders.** In practice, the stakeholder who pays for the evaluation has disproportionate influence over its design and dissemination. Weiss is clear-eyed about these power dynamics.

## Core Concept
Weiss identifies multiple stakeholder groups with legitimate but often competing interests in evaluation: programme funders, programme managers and staff, programme participants, legislators and policy-makers, advocacy groups, and the broader public. Each group brings different questions, priorities, and definitions of success to the evaluation.

Funders typically want accountability: did the programme achieve its objectives and was money well spent? Programme managers want information that helps them improve: what is working and what needs adjustment? Participants want to know whether the programme serves their interests. Policy-makers want evidence that supports decision-making. Advocacy groups want evidence that supports their positions. These are all legitimate interests, but they pull the evaluation in different directions.

Weiss makes several observations about these dynamics. First, the stakeholder who commissions and pays for the evaluation has structural power over the evaluation design, questions, and dissemination. This is a reality, not a complaint. Second, the evaluator's obligation is to the quality of the evidence, not to any single stakeholder. This means the evaluator must maintain independence even when it is uncomfortable. Third, the multiple-stakeholder environment means that the "right" evaluation questions are contested, and the evaluator must make deliberate choices about whose questions to prioritise rather than pretending that a single set of questions serves all interests.

Weiss is skeptical of approaches that resolve these tensions through participatory processes alone. While stakeholder engagement can improve evaluation relevance and utility, it can also introduce political dynamics that distort the evaluation design — for example, when powerful stakeholders veto evaluation questions that might produce unwelcome findings, or when participatory processes privilege articulate stakeholders over marginalised ones.

The evaluator's task is to be responsive to stakeholder interests without being subservient to them — to understand what different groups need from the evaluation, to design studies that address the most important questions, and to report findings that are honest regardless of which stakeholders they please or displease.

## How to Apply
1. **Map stakeholders early.** Identify all groups with a legitimate interest in the evaluation. Understand what each group wants from the evaluation and what they might do with the findings.
2. **Negotiate questions, not findings.** Stakeholder input is valuable for shaping evaluation questions and ensuring relevance. It should not shape findings or conclusions.
3. **Protect independence.** The evaluator must maintain the ability to ask difficult questions and report unwelcome findings. This requires institutional arrangements — contractual protections, independent review, multiple dissemination channels.
4. **Be transparent about trade-offs.** When stakeholder interests conflict (they often do), be explicit about which questions you are prioritising and why.
5. **Pay attention to absent stakeholders.** Programme participants, particularly marginalised ones, are often underrepresented in stakeholder processes. Their interests deserve attention even when they lack the power to demand it.

## Examples
**Situation:** A national department commissions an evaluation of a social grant programme. Stakeholders include the department (funder), implementing agencies, grant recipients, Treasury (budget implications), and opposition politicians (accountability function).
**Application:** Each stakeholder wants different things. The department wants evidence of impact to justify continued funding. Implementing agencies want information about operational improvements. Recipients want the grant to continue and increase. Treasury wants cost-effectiveness data. Opposition politicians want ammunition for oversight. The evaluator cannot serve all these interests equally. Weiss would recommend prioritising the questions that produce the most useful knowledge — typically the theory-based questions about how and why the grant produces (or fails to produce) its intended effects. She would also advise ensuring that findings are disseminated to all stakeholders, not just the commissioner, to prevent selective use or suppression. And she would note that the power dynamic is real: the department that commissions the evaluation can influence its design and control its dissemination. The evaluator must negotiate sufficient independence to produce honest work.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that broadening stakeholder participation automatically improves evaluation quality.
**Why:** Participation can introduce competing agendas, dilute evaluation focus, and privilege articulate stakeholders. Participation is valuable when it improves relevance and grounding. It is counterproductive when it becomes a political negotiation over what the evaluation is allowed to find.

**Don't:** Ignore stakeholder dynamics and pretend the evaluation is a purely technical exercise.
**Why:** Ignoring stakeholder interests does not eliminate them. It merely ensures that the evaluator is surprised when findings are resisted, suppressed, or distorted by interests the evaluator did not anticipate.
