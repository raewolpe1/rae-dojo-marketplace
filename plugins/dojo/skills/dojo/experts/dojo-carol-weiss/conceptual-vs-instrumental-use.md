# Conceptual vs Instrumental Use

## When to Use
- When explaining the different types of evaluation use and why the distinctions matter.
- When helping users understand why their evaluation findings were or were not "used."
- When advising evaluators on designing for different types of use.

## Fails When
- **You rank the types hierarchically with instrumental use on top.** Weiss's point is that conceptual use and enlightenment are not failed instrumental use — they are distinct and often more consequential pathways of influence.
- **You add types of use beyond what the evidence supports.** The taxonomy should be grounded in how evaluation actually influences action, not in a wish list of possible influences.

## Core Concept
Weiss's taxonomy of evaluation use distinguishes several fundamentally different ways in which evaluation findings influence action. The distinctions matter because collapsing them into a single category of "use" obscures the actual dynamics of evaluation influence and leads to misjudgements about whether evaluation is worthwhile.

**Instrumental use** occurs when evaluation findings are applied directly to a specific decision. An evaluation shows that a programme component is ineffective; the component is redesigned. An evaluation demonstrates cost-effectiveness; funding is continued. This is the type of use that most people imagine when they think about evaluation, and it is the most visible. It is also the rarest, because it requires the convergence of several conditions: the finding must be timely, relevant, unambiguous, actionable, and politically feasible, and the decision-maker must have the authority and motivation to act.

**Conceptual use** occurs when evaluation findings change how people think about a problem, a programme, or a policy domain — without directly changing a specific decision. A finding that youth employment programmes work through network effects rather than skills acquisition changes how programme designers think about youth employment. No single programme decision may follow immediately, but the conceptual shift shapes many subsequent decisions. Conceptual use is more common than instrumental use and is often more durable.

**Enlightenment use** (described in detail in the enlightenment file) is the long-term, diffuse process by which accumulated findings reshape the assumptions of the policy community. It operates at a higher level of abstraction than conceptual use — it changes not just how people think about a specific problem but what they take for granted about how the world works.

**Symbolic use** occurs when evaluation is invoked to legitimise decisions already made. A decision-maker commissions an evaluation to demonstrate accountability, or cites evaluation findings selectively to justify a predetermined position. Weiss does not condemn symbolic use as uniformly illegitimate — sometimes the legitimation function is socially useful — but she is clear that evaluators should not confuse it with evidence-based decision-making.

**Political use** occurs when evaluation findings are used as political ammunition — to support or attack a programme, a policy, or a political position. The findings are genuine, but their use is driven by political strategy rather than by a dispassionate assessment of evidence.

The practical significance of this taxonomy is diagnostic. When an evaluation commissioner asks whether the evaluation was "used," the answer depends on what type of use you are looking for. If you look only for instrumental use, you will almost always be disappointed. If you recognise conceptual use and enlightenment as legitimate and consequential pathways, the picture looks very different.

## How to Apply
1. **Clarify what type of use you are designing for.** Different types of use require different evaluation designs, communication strategies, and timelines. Be explicit about expectations.
2. **Do not promise instrumental use.** It is dishonest to promise that evaluation findings will directly change decisions. Promise honest, rigorous findings and realistic pathways of influence.
3. **Design for conceptual use.** Invest in the clarity and communicability of your conceptual framework — the way of thinking about the problem. This is what travels.
4. **Recognise symbolic use without endorsing it.** When evaluation is being used symbolically, name it. This is not a failure of the evaluation but a feature of the political context.
5. **Track use across types.** When assessing whether an evaluation was useful, look for all types of use, not just instrumental. Conceptual shifts may be invisible unless you look for them.

## Examples
**Situation:** The Department of Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation (DPME) commissions an evaluation of community works programmes. The evaluation finds limited sustained employment effects. Two years later, the programme continues largely unchanged. An observer concludes the evaluation was not used.
**Application:** The observer is looking only for instrumental use and finding none. But a closer look reveals conceptual use: the evaluation's findings have entered the discourse within the department, shifted how officials discuss the programme's objectives (from "employment creation" to "income support and social cohesion"), and influenced the design criteria for new programme cycles. The evaluation did not produce a decision to restructure the programme — that decision faces political constraints unrelated to the evidence — but it changed how informed officials think about what the programme does and does not accomplish. By Weiss's taxonomy, this is significant conceptual use, and it may eventually contribute to enlightenment-level shifts in how South Africa thinks about public employment.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Design evaluations assuming instrumental use and then blame non-use on "the system."
**Why:** If you design for instrumental use and it does not occur, the problem may be your design, not the system. Designing for conceptual use and enlightenment is more realistic and often more consequential.

**Don't:** Treat all types of use as equally desirable.
**Why:** Symbolic use and political use serve important political functions, but they are not evaluation influence in the full sense. The evaluator should aspire to conceptual influence, not merely to having findings cited in political debates.
