# Weiss and Patton Compared

## When to Use
- When someone asks how Weiss's approach differs from Patton's.
- When helping users choose between or combine theory-based and utilisation-focused approaches.
- When discussing the major intellectual currents in evaluation methodology.

## Fails When
- **You present the two as opposing camps.** Weiss and Patton share more common ground than their followers sometimes recognise. Both care about use. They differ on what drives it.
- **You present either approach as uniformly superior.** Each has strengths in different contexts and for different evaluation purposes.

## Core Concept
Carol Weiss and Michael Quinn Patton represent two of the most influential intellectual streams in evaluation. Their approaches are complementary but rest on different premises about what makes evaluation valuable and how evaluation influence operates.

**Weiss: evaluation is valuable because of what it reveals about programme theory.** The primary function of evaluation is to test the causal assumptions embedded in programmes — to surface and test the theory of change. Evaluation influence operates primarily through conceptual use and enlightenment: the findings change how people understand problems and programmes, gradually reshaping the knowledge base that informs decisions. Weiss is sober about instrumental use — she documents its rarity and warns against designing evaluations around the expectation of direct decision impact.

**Patton: evaluation is valuable because it is used.** The primary function of evaluation is to produce findings that intended users actually use for intended purposes. Evaluation influence is maximised by engaging intended users from the beginning, designing the evaluation around their questions, and ensuring the findings are directly relevant to decisions they face. Patton's utilisation-focused evaluation is a framework for increasing the probability that evaluation findings will be used instrumentally or processually.

The differences flow from different diagnoses of the evaluation use problem. Weiss diagnoses the problem as structural: evaluation findings operate in political contexts where multiple forces compete for influence, and instrumental use is inherently rare. The appropriate response is to design for conceptual influence and contribute to enlightenment. Patton diagnoses the problem as relational: evaluation findings go unused because evaluators fail to engage intended users, design around irrelevant questions, and communicate poorly. The appropriate response is to orient the evaluation around use from the beginning.

Both diagnoses have merit. Weiss is more realistic about the political and structural constraints on evaluation influence. Patton is more actionable about what the evaluator can do within those constraints. Weiss provides the deeper analysis of how evaluation actually influences the world. Patton provides the more practical guidance for maximising influence in a specific evaluation.

Where they disagree most sharply is on the role of the evaluator. Weiss sees the evaluator as an independent analyst whose primary obligation is to produce rigorous, honest findings — and whose influence operates through the quality and conceptual power of those findings. Patton sees the evaluator as a facilitator of use who works collaboratively with intended users throughout the evaluation process. Weiss worries that Patton's approach risks compromising independence. Patton worries that Weiss's approach produces rigorous findings that nobody reads.

## How to Apply
1. **Use Weiss for evaluation design.** Structure the evaluation around the programme's theory of change. Identify the causal chain, mechanisms, mediating variables, and assumptions. This is the analytical foundation.
2. **Use Patton for evaluation process.** Engage intended users in the evaluation process. Ensure the evaluation questions are relevant to decisions they face. Communicate findings in accessible ways.
3. **Use Weiss for expectations about use.** Be realistic about the pathways of influence. Expect conceptual use and enlightenment more than instrumental use. Do not judge the evaluation's value solely by whether a specific decision changed.
4. **Use Patton for maximising influence.** Within the realistic constraints Weiss identifies, do what Patton recommends: build relationships with intended users, ensure relevance, communicate actively.
5. **Recognise the tension and manage it.** The tension between independence (Weiss) and engagement (Patton) is real and productive. The best evaluations manage both — maintaining analytical rigour while remaining responsive to user needs.

## Examples
**Situation:** A funder asks an evaluation team to design an evaluation of a justice sector reform programme in South Africa. The funder wants both rigorous evidence and practical utility.
**Application:** The evaluation team uses Weiss's framework to structure the evaluation around the programme's theory of change: what causal mechanisms connect capacity building in courts to improved case processing to improved access to justice? This provides the analytical backbone. They use Patton's framework to engage the key intended users — the department, court managers, and oversight bodies — in refining evaluation questions and ensuring relevance. They measure mediating variables (Weiss) and create feedback mechanisms during the evaluation (Patton). They set expectations realistically (Weiss: expect conceptual influence more than immediate restructuring) while maximising the probability of influence (Patton: ensure findings reach the right people at the right time in accessible formats).

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the Weiss-Patton difference as a tribal identity.
**Why:** Both approaches contain valuable insights. Identifying exclusively with one and dismissing the other produces weaker evaluation than combining the strengths of both.

**Don't:** Assume that combining the two is straightforward.
**Why:** The tension between independence and engagement is real. The evaluator who is simultaneously an independent analyst (Weiss) and a facilitator of use embedded in the user community (Patton) faces genuine role conflicts. Managing this tension requires awareness and deliberate choices, not just good intentions.
