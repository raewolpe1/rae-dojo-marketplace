# Theories of Change in Development

## When to Use
- When discussing how the theory of change concept has been adopted by development agencies and donors.
- When helping users construct rigorous theories of change for development programmes.
- When critiquing the gap between theory of change as Weiss conceived it and as the development sector practises it.

## Fails When
- **You accept development-sector theory of change practice at face value.** Much of what passes for "theory of change" in development is relabelled logframe thinking — result chains with arrows but without causal reasoning. Weiss would be unsatisfied.
- **You dismiss the development sector's adoption of theory of change as entirely superficial.** Some practitioners and organisations are doing genuine theory-based work. The critique should be selective and constructive.

## Core Concept
The theory of change concept migrated from Weiss's evaluation methodology into international development practice during the late 1990s and 2000s, driven by growing dissatisfaction with logframes and results-based management as evaluation tools. The Aspen Institute's theory of change work, influenced by Weiss, helped bridge the academic concept into development practice. Today, virtually every major development agency, bilateral donor, and large INGO requires theories of change for programmes and strategies.

But Weiss would observe a significant gap between the concept as she articulated it and the practice as the development sector implements it. In her framework, a theory of change is a set of testable causal hypotheses about how programme activities produce outcomes through specific mechanisms under specified conditions. In development practice, "theory of change" has often become a diagram — a map of expected causal pathways, sometimes elaborate and visually impressive, but often lacking the critical elements: explicit mechanisms, testable assumptions, and specification of the conditions under which the causal logic holds.

Several dynamics drive this gap. First, the institutional incentive is to produce theories of change for proposal writing and log frame compliance, not for evaluation. The theory of change is produced before implementation to satisfy the funder, and then rarely revisited, tested, or revised. Second, the complexity of development programmes — which often operate across sectors, levels of governance, and time horizons — makes genuine causal specification difficult. The temptation is to map everything rather than specifying the core causal logic clearly. Third, the participatory norm in development means that theories of change are often produced through stakeholder workshops where the goal is consensus rather than analytical rigour. The result is a politically acceptable diagram rather than a sharp causal argument.

Weiss's corrective would be consistent with her general approach: insist on mechanisms. For each arrow in the diagram, ask: through what causal process does this change occur? If the answer is vague or absent, the theory of change is not yet a theory. It is a map of hopes.

The constructive contribution of the development sector's adoption of theory of change should also be acknowledged. At its best, the practice has pushed programmes to think more carefully about causal logic, to consider contextual conditions, and to move beyond activity-output-outcome chains to more nuanced models of change. The task is to push this practice toward the analytical rigour that Weiss intended.

## How to Apply
1. **Insist on mechanisms at every link.** When facilitating or reviewing a development programme's theory of change, ask at every arrow: what is the mechanism? Why should this activity produce this change?
2. **Separate the theory of change from the operational plan.** The theory of change is an analytical tool for understanding how change occurs. The operational plan is a management tool for organising activities. They are related but not identical.
3. **Test and revise.** Build evaluation around the theory of change, and use evaluation findings to test and revise the theory. A theory of change that is never tested is a hypothesis, not knowledge.
4. **Resist the complexity trap.** Development theories of change often try to capture every possible pathway and influence. The result is an unreadable diagram and an untestable theory. Focus on the core causal logic.
5. **Be honest about context dependence.** Development programmes operate in contexts that vary enormously. A theory of change that works in one context may fail in another. Specify the contextual conditions that the theory requires.

## Examples
**Situation:** A bilateral donor requires a theory of change for a governance programme in Mozambique that aims to improve service delivery through citizen accountability mechanisms.
**Application:** The typical development-sector theory of change would map: citizen scorecards → citizen voice → government responsiveness → improved service delivery. Weiss would push much harder on the middle links. Through what mechanism does citizen voice produce government responsiveness? What are the accountability relationships that translate citizen demand into government action? Are those accountability relationships present in the Mozambican political context? If local government officials are appointed rather than elected, the citizen-voice-to-government-responsiveness link rests on a very different mechanism than in contexts with elected local government. The theory of change must specify this mechanism and the conditions under which it operates — and the evaluation must test whether those conditions obtain and whether the mechanism functions.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Accept a theory of change diagram as evidence of theoretical thinking.
**Why:** Many development theories of change are diagrams without theory — they map expected sequences without specifying mechanisms or assumptions. The diagram is the representation; the theory is the causal reasoning.

**Don't:** Require theories of change to be comprehensive maps of all possible change pathways.
**Why:** Comprehensiveness trades off against testability. A theory of change that tries to capture everything is untestable and therefore unfalsifiable. Better to have a sharp, testable theory of the core causal logic than a comprehensive but vague map.
