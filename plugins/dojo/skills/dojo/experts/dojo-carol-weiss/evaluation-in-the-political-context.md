# Evaluation in the Political Context

## When to Use
- When explaining why evaluation operates within and is shaped by political dynamics.
- When helping users understand why technically sound evaluations are sometimes ignored or resisted.
- When advising evaluators on how to maintain integrity within politically charged contexts.

## Fails When
- **You treat politics as an obstacle to be overcome rather than the environment in which evaluation operates.** Weiss's point is not that politics is unfortunate but that it is constitutive — evaluation is inherently political because the programmes it evaluates are political creations serving political purposes.
- **You recommend that evaluators become political operators.** Weiss does not advocate political advocacy by evaluators. She advocates political awareness — understanding the political context well enough to navigate it without being captured by it.

## Core Concept
Weiss's 1973 paper "Where Politics and Evaluation Research Meet" is one of the field's landmark contributions. Her argument is that evaluation and politics intersect at three levels, and that understanding these intersections is essential for evaluators who want their work to matter.

First, **programmes are the creatures of political decisions.** They are created through legislative or executive action driven by political priorities, constituent demands, and ideological commitments. The decision to fund a programme reflects political judgements about what problems deserve attention and what approaches are acceptable. This means that evaluation of the programme enters a political story already in progress.

Second, **evaluation itself is embedded in political dynamics.** The decision to evaluate (or not), the choice of evaluation questions, the selection of the evaluator, the design of the study, and the dissemination of findings are all influenced by political considerations. Who wants the evaluation done and why? What questions are asked reflects whose interests are prioritised. What findings are likely to be acceptable?

Third, **evaluation findings enter a political process.** Findings compete with other inputs — ideology, constituency pressure, bureaucratic interests, leadership preferences — for influence over decisions. Even unambiguous findings may be overridden by political considerations, not because decision-makers are irrational but because they face pressures and constraints that the evaluator does not.

Weiss draws several implications from this analysis. The evaluator who ignores the political context is naive — likely to produce findings that are technically sound but politically irrelevant, or to be surprised when findings are ignored or distorted. But the evaluator who becomes a political player risks compromising the integrity that gives evaluation its value. The middle path is political awareness without political capture: understanding the political dynamics well enough to design evaluations that are both rigorous and relevant, and to communicate findings in ways that give them the best chance of influence within the constraints of the political process.

Weiss also notes that the political context of evaluation changes over the programme's lifecycle. During programme creation, evaluation is often unwelcome because it threatens the political consensus that created the programme. During programme maturation, evaluation becomes more acceptable as the political dynamics stabilise. During programme crisis, evaluation findings may suddenly become relevant because the political calculus shifts.

## How to Apply
1. **Map the political landscape before designing the evaluation.** Who are the stakeholders? What are their interests? What findings would be welcome, and what findings would be threatening? This is not accommodation — it is intelligence.
2. **Protect methodological integrity.** Political awareness means understanding the context, not compromising the design. The evaluation must be designed to produce honest findings, regardless of whether those findings are politically convenient.
3. **Anticipate resistance to unwelcome findings.** If your evaluation is likely to produce findings that threaten powerful interests, plan your communication strategy accordingly. Ensure findings are documented, disseminated through multiple channels, and supported by transparent methodology.
4. **Distinguish political use from evaluation influence.** When findings are cited selectively to support predetermined positions, recognise this as political use. It is not the same as evidence-informed decision-making, and evaluators should not confuse them.
5. **Time your communication.** Even in a political environment, there are moments when evidence is more likely to be heard — budget cycles, policy reviews, leadership transitions, crises. Understanding the political calendar improves the chances of influence.

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluator is commissioned by a provincial government in South Africa to evaluate a flagship housing programme. Preliminary findings suggest significant implementation failures.
**Application:** The political context includes a governing party that created the programme and identifies with it publicly, opposition parties that would use negative findings as political ammunition, beneficiary communities that value the programme regardless of implementation quality, and national government departments that co-fund it. Weiss would advise the evaluator to understand these dynamics — not to soften findings but to anticipate how they will be received and to design the communication accordingly. Report implementation failures clearly but also specify what would need to change for the programme to succeed (giving the government a path forward rather than just a verdict). Ensure findings are available to multiple audiences — not just the commissioner — to prevent suppression. And recognise that the evaluation's most important influence may be conceptual rather than instrumental: it may not change this programme immediately, but it may change how the next housing programme is designed.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Conclude that political context makes evaluation pointless.
**Why:** Evaluation operates within politics, but it is not rendered useless by politics. The enlightenment and conceptual use pathways operate precisely within political environments. Understanding the political context makes evaluation more effective, not less.

**Don't:** Self-censor findings to avoid political discomfort.
**Why:** The evaluator's credibility — and the value of the evaluation enterprise — depends on honest reporting. Once evaluators begin shaping findings to suit political audiences, the evaluation enterprise loses the thing that makes it valuable: its independence from the interests it evaluates.
