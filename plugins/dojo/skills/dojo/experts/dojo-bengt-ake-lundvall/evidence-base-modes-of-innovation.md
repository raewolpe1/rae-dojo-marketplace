# Evidence Base — Modes of Innovation

> **Provenance.** Compiled July 2026, principally from Jensen, Johnson, Lorenz & Lundvall (2007), *Research Policy* 36(5): 680–693, and secondary literature summarising it. Verify against the primary source before quoting in a client deliverable. Entries marked `[CHECK]` are unverified to the primary source.

## THE EVIDENCE

### The founding study
- **Study:** Jensen, M. B., Johnson, B., Lorenz, E. & Lundvall, B.-Å. (2007), "Forms of Knowledge and Modes of Innovation", *Research Policy* **36(5): 680–693**. DOI 10.1016/j.respol.2007.01.006. Republished as Chapter 7 in *The Learning Economy and the Economics of Hope* (Anthem Press, 2016), pp. 155–182.
- **Country:** **Denmark.**
- **Data:** The **2001 Danish DISKO Survey**.
- **Method:** **Latent class analysis** to identify groups of firms practising the two modes with different intensities; **logit regression** to relate cluster membership to innovation outcomes.
- **Finding:** Four clusters identified — **low learning, STI, DUI, and DUI/STI**. **Firms combining the two modes are more likely to innovate new products or services than those relying primarily on one mode or the other.** Firms establishing a stronger science base are more innovative than the rest. **But the most significant and important result is that firms using mixed strategies — combining organisational forms promoting learning with R&D efforts and with co-operation with researchers at knowledge institutions — are much more innovative than the rest.**
- **Caveat — the big one.** **Denmark, 2001.** A small, rich, high-trust economy with unusually flat work organisation and a strong tradition of worker participation — arguably the most DUI-favourable environment in the world. **The distinction travels. The cluster proportions and the magnitudes do not.** Do not import Danish shares into a South African document.
- **Second caveat:** cross-sectional and self-reported. The paper establishes association between cluster membership and innovation outcomes, not a causal effect of adopting a mode.

### The cluster structure and firm size
- **Study:** As above, Table (cluster conditional probabilities).
- **Finding:** For firms with **fewer than 50 employees** (n = 339), the conditional probabilities of cluster membership were reported as approximately: **low learning 0.56, STI 0.09, DUI 0.26, DUI/STI 0.10** `[CHECK]`.
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]` — these figures are read from a secondary reproduction of the table and **must be verified against the paper before use**. If they hold, the implication is important and uncomfortable: **even in Denmark, the modal small firm is in the low-learning cluster** — not DUI. That is a caution against romanticising small-firm innovation, and it means "most small firms innovate through DUI" is too strong a claim. The accurate claim is that *where small firms innovate, it is predominantly through DUI* — and many do not innovate at all.

### The policy conclusion the authors themselves drew
- **Study:** As above, concluding section.
- **Finding:** The paper concludes by considering **the implications for benchmarking innovation systems and for innovation policy.**
- **Caveat:** This is the paper's own framing and it is the reason it matters for your work. The implication is that innovation system benchmarks constructed on STI indicators mis-rank countries and mis-direct policy — but note that the paper *raises* the implication rather than establishing empirically that benchmarks have caused policy failure.

### The follow-on literature — subsequent findings
- **Study:** Systematic review and subsequent quantitative work on DUI/STI. `[CHECK]` — individual citations unverified.
- **Finding:** Reported in the subsequent literature: **STI sub-modes perform relatively well for radical innovation**; **DUI sub-modes gain in performance when combined with STI sub-modes**; and for **incremental innovation the opposite holds**. Also reported: firms with stronger **incentives management** obtain higher productivity pay-offs from IT.
- **Caveat:** `[CHECK]` throughout. The radical/incremental split is intuitively important for a strategy choosing between innovation ambitions, but source it before relying on it.

### Application to African micro and small enterprises — the bridge
- **Study:** Kraemer-Mbula, E., **Lorenz, E.**, Takala-Greenish, L., Jegede, O. O., Garba, T., Mutambala, M. & Esemu, T. (2019), "Are African micro- and small enterprises misunderstood? Unpacking the relationship between work organisation, capability development and innovation", *International Journal of Technological Learning, Innovation and Development* **11(1): 1–30**.
- **Countries:** African (multi-country). `[CHECK]` — country set unverified.
- **Finding:** Applies the work-organisation and capability lens to African MSEs. `[CHECK]` — specific findings unverified.
- **Caveat:** **Note the author overlap: Edward Lorenz co-authored both the 2007 DUI/STI paper and this one.** The framework has already been pointed at a firm population resembling yours, by one of its own creators, and the title asks precisely the question your strategy should be asking. **This is the single most relevant follow-on citation in this file and it should be obtained and read directly.** See also the Kraemer-Mbula dojo.

## How to Apply

1. **Quote the finding correctly: combination wins.** Not DUI over STI.
2. **State that it is Danish and 2001.** Every time. The distinction travels; the numbers do not.
3. **Do not claim most small firms innovate via DUI.** The accurate claim: where they innovate, it is predominantly DUI — and the modal small firm may be in the low-learning cluster even in Denmark.
4. **Go to the Kraemer-Mbula/Lorenz 2019 paper** for the African application. It is the bridge and it is `[CHECK]` — read it.
5. **Treat the association as association.** Cross-sectional; not a causal estimate of adopting a mode.

## Examples

**Situation:** A strategy wants to cite Lundvall to justify de-emphasising R&D support in favour of practical learning support for MSMEs.

**Application:** The citation does not support that. The finding is that **combining** the modes out-performs either alone; firms with a stronger science base *are* more innovative than the rest. What the evidence supports is different and more interesting: that an innovation pillar consisting **only** of STI instruments serves a fraction of firms and cannot detect the rest, and that the highest-performing configuration is a learning organisation **connected to** codified knowledge. For an MSME population, that argues for cheap bridges to the science base — a technical adviser, a lab test, a student placement — rather than either building laboratories or abandoning the science base.

**Result:** A pillar aimed at the combination, which is what the evidence actually rewards.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Cite this to argue R&D doesn't matter.
**Why:** Firms with a stronger science base are more innovative than the rest. The finding is combination.

**Don't:** Import Danish cluster proportions.
**Why:** Denmark 2001 — small, rich, high-trust, unusually flat work organisation. Possibly the most DUI-favourable economy on earth.

**Don't:** Claim most small firms innovate through DUI.
**Why:** The reported small-firm cluster probabilities put the largest share in **low learning** `[CHECK]`. Where they innovate it is DUI; many do not innovate.
