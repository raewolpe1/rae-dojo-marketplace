# Equity and Evaluation

## When to Use
- When evaluating programmes with equity goals — reducing inequality, serving marginalised populations, promoting inclusion.
- When asking whose questions drive the evaluation, whose voices are heard, and whose outcomes are measured.
- When power dynamics risk excluding the populations the programme is meant to serve.
- When examining distributional effects — not just average outcomes but how outcomes differ across groups.
- When designing evaluation in post-colonial, post-apartheid, or other contexts shaped by historical exclusion.

## Fails When
- **You treat equity as a checklist item.** Adding a demographic disaggregation table does not make an evaluation equity-focused. Equity requires examining systemic patterns, structural barriers, and power dynamics — not just counting who participated.
- **You impose an equity lens where it is genuinely irrelevant.** Not every evaluation needs a full equity analysis, but every evaluation should consider who is included and excluded, and whether the evaluation design itself creates or reinforces inequities.
- **You speak for marginalised communities without engaging them.** Equity-focused evaluation requires the genuine participation of the communities whose equity is at stake — not just the participation of professionals who claim to represent them.

## Core Concept
Evaluation is not a neutral technical exercise. Every evaluation makes choices — about which questions to ask, whose voices to include, which outcomes to measure, and how to interpret findings — that have equity implications. Equity-focused evaluation makes these choices deliberately, centering the experiences, perspectives, and outcomes of marginalised and underserved populations.

Several dimensions of equity are relevant to evaluation. **Procedural equity** asks: who participates in the evaluation process? Whose questions are asked? Whose voices are heard? **Distributional equity** asks: how are programme benefits and costs distributed across groups? Who benefits most and least? Who bears the costs? **Structural equity** asks: does the programme address or reinforce structural barriers — systemic patterns of exclusion, discrimination, and disadvantage? **Representational equity** asks: are findings reported in ways that accurately represent the experiences and perspectives of all groups, especially those with the least power?

In the South African context, equity-focused evaluation has particular resonance. Programmes operate in a society shaped by deliberate historical exclusion along racial, geographic, and gender lines. Evaluation that does not examine who benefits, who is excluded, and whether programmes are reducing or reproducing historical patterns of disadvantage is incomplete — regardless of whether it demonstrates "programme effectiveness" in aggregate.

Equity-focused evaluation is not a separate methodology — it is a lens that can be applied across evaluation approaches. In UFE, it means ensuring that marginalised stakeholders are among the primary intended users. In DE, it means tracking whether innovations are reaching underserved populations. In qualitative inquiry, it means purposefully sampling marginalised voices and creating safe spaces for honest participation.

## How to Apply
1. **Ask equity questions explicitly.** Who benefits from the programme? Who does not? Are there differential outcomes across groups? Does the programme reduce or widen existing inequalities?
2. **Include marginalised voices in the evaluation design.** Ensure that the people the programme is meant to serve participate in defining evaluation questions, not just in answering surveys.
3. **Disaggregate data.** Report outcomes by relevant equity dimensions — race, gender, geography, income, disability, age — to reveal distributional patterns that averages conceal.
4. **Examine structural barriers.** Look beyond individual-level outcomes to the structural and systemic factors that shape access, participation, and benefit.
5. **Use culturally appropriate methods.** Select data collection methods that are accessible, respectful, and appropriate for the cultural contexts of marginalised communities.
6. **Report with equity specificity.** Name the equity findings explicitly. Do not bury distributional analysis in annexures or present only aggregate results.

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluation of a provincial enterprise development programme reports that 60% of supported enterprises are still operating after two years. The programme is deemed successful.
**Application:** Equity-focused evaluation disaggregates this finding: 80% of enterprises in urban areas survive, versus 35% in township and rural areas. Men-owned enterprises survive at 70%, women-owned at 45%. Enterprises accessing the full support package (mentoring + finance + market access) survive at 85%, but only 20% of township-based enterprises access the full package due to travel distances and awareness gaps. The programme is "successful" in aggregate but reproducing historical patterns of spatial and gender exclusion in its distribution of outcomes.
**Result:** The evaluation findings shift from "the programme works" to "the programme works for some and not others, and the pattern of who it works for reproduces existing inequalities." This finding is far more useful for programme improvement and far more honest about the programme's actual impact.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat demographic disaggregation as sufficient equity analysis.
**Why:** Disaggregating data reveals patterns but does not explain them. Equity-focused evaluation goes beyond disaggregation to examine why differential patterns exist — what structural barriers, access constraints, and power dynamics produce unequal outcomes.

**Don't:** Assume the evaluator is neutral.
**Why:** Evaluators carry their own positionality — assumptions, biases, cultural perspectives — that shape what they see, what they ask, and how they interpret. Reflexivity about one's own positionality is an essential component of equity-focused practice.
