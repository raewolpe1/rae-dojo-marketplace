# Evaluation Design

## When to Use
- When selecting the overall evaluation approach and specific design elements for a new evaluation.
- When matching evaluation design to the nature of the programme, the evaluation purpose, and the decision context.
- When the user is defaulting to one design type (typically RCT or pre-post surveys) without considering alternatives.
- When designing an M&E framework that encompasses both monitoring and evaluation components.

## Fails When
- **You start with design before purpose.** The design should follow from the evaluation questions, the intended uses, and the nature of the evaluand — not precede them.
- **You apply one design to all situations.** Experimental designs suit attribution questions for stable interventions. Developmental designs suit innovations in complex environments. Case studies suit in-depth understanding. No single design fits all purposes.
- **You ignore practical constraints.** The most rigorous design that cannot be implemented within the available budget, timeline, and data infrastructure is not the best design. The best design is the most rigorous one that is feasible and useful.

## Core Concept
Evaluation design is the process of configuring an evaluation to produce the most useful, credible information for the intended users and uses, given the evaluation questions, the nature of the evaluand, the context, and the available resources. It is not a purely technical exercise — it requires judgement about trade-offs between rigour and feasibility, depth and breadth, timeliness and comprehensiveness.

The central principle is fitness for purpose. The evaluation design should be driven by the evaluation questions, which are driven by the intended uses, which are driven by the intended users. This is the UFE logic applied to design: every design decision is justified by how it serves use.

Several evaluation approaches offer different design logics. Experimental and quasi-experimental designs test causal attribution by comparing treated and untreated groups. Theory-based evaluation tests the programme's theory of change — the assumed causal pathway from activities to outcomes. Realist evaluation asks "what works, for whom, in what circumstances, through what mechanisms?" Case study evaluation provides in-depth understanding of particular instances. Participatory evaluation engages stakeholders as co-evaluators. Developmental evaluation provides real-time feedback for adaptive management.

The choice among these approaches — and they can be combined — depends on the evaluation questions (what do we need to know?), the evaluand (what kind of programme is this?), the context (what is feasible?), the resources (what can we afford?), and the intended uses (what will the findings be used for?).

Design also encompasses data collection methods (quantitative, qualitative, or mixed), sampling strategies, data analysis approaches, quality assurance mechanisms, and the timeline and reporting structure. Each of these is a design decision that should be justified by fitness for purpose.

## How to Apply
1. **Start with evaluation questions.** What do the intended users need to know? Different questions require different designs.
2. **Assess the evaluand.** Is the programme simple, complicated, or complex? Stable or adaptive? Large-scale or small? The nature of the evaluand shapes the design options.
3. **Consider the decision context.** When are decisions being made? What level of evidence is needed? What are the consequences of being wrong?
4. **Select the approach.** Choose the evaluation approach(es) that best fit the questions, evaluand, and context. Combine approaches when no single one suffices.
5. **Configure the details.** Select data collection methods, sampling strategies, analysis approaches, quality safeguards, and the reporting structure — all justified by fitness for purpose.
6. **Reality-test the design.** Is it feasible within the budget, timeline, and data infrastructure? If not, adjust — preserving the most important design elements and accepting trade-offs on less critical ones.

## Examples
**Situation:** A government department wants to evaluate a national youth employment programme and the ToR mandates an RCT.
**Application:** Before accepting or rejecting the RCT mandate, diagnose: is an RCT feasible and appropriate? RCTs require random assignment, a stable intervention, adequate sample sizes, and willingness to withhold treatment from a control group. If the programme has already been rolled out nationally (no untreated group available), if it adapts to each context (no standardised treatment), or if ethical concerns prevent withholding support from unemployed youth, an RCT is neither feasible nor appropriate. Alternative designs — regression discontinuity, difference-in-differences, contribution analysis, or a mixed-methods design combining quasi-experimental comparison with qualitative process evaluation — may produce more useful and credible findings for the specific evaluation questions.
**Result:** Negotiate with the funder. Explain why the mandated design does not fit the evaluation context, propose alternatives that are both rigorous and feasible, and frame the alternatives in terms of what they will enable the funder to learn — which is what the funder actually needs.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the RCT as the "gold standard" for all evaluation questions.
**Why:** RCTs answer one question well — causal attribution. They do not answer questions about implementation quality, participant experience, contextual variation, or programme improvement. Most evaluation questions are not pure attribution questions. Match design to question, not to hierarchy.

**Don't:** Design the evaluation in isolation from the users.
**Why:** A technically excellent design that does not serve the users' information needs will not be used. Engage users in design decisions — they bring contextual knowledge that shapes design feasibility and relevance.
