# Evaluation Use and Influence

## When to Use
- When designing the use pathway for an evaluation — how findings will get from the evaluation to decisions.
- When diagnosing why previous evaluations were not used.
- When explaining to commissioners or funders the different types of evaluation use.
- When designing the communication and dissemination strategy for evaluation findings.

## Fails When
- **You treat use as automatic.** Good methodology does not guarantee use. Use requires deliberate design: the right users engaged, the right questions asked, the right findings communicated at the right time.
- **You measure only instrumental use.** Direct use of findings to make a specific decision (instrumental use) is only one pathway. Evaluations also influence how people think about issues (conceptual use), contribute to accumulated evidence (knowledge use), and build evaluation capacity (process use).

## Core Concept
Evaluation use is the central concern of utilization-focused evaluation — but "use" is more nuanced than it first appears. Research on evaluation use identifies multiple pathways through which evaluations influence decisions, practice, and thinking.

**Instrumental use** (also called direct use) occurs when evaluation findings directly inform a specific decision — continuing or discontinuing a programme, modifying implementation, reallocating resources. This is the most visible form of use, but it is not the most common.

**Conceptual use** (also called enlightenment) occurs when evaluation findings change how people think about an issue, even without directly informing a specific decision. A finding that a skills programme's theory of change is flawed may not immediately change the programme, but it may reshape how programme managers think about skills development in future programme design.

**Process use** occurs when participating in the evaluation process changes how people think and act — independent of the findings. This pathway operates through the mechanisms described in the process use topic.

**Symbolic or political use** occurs when evaluation findings are used to justify decisions already made, to demonstrate accountability, or to legitimise political positions. While sometimes characterised negatively, symbolic use can be legitimate when evaluation provides evidence for positions that are genuinely supported by data.

**Misuse** occurs when evaluation findings are selectively cited, misrepresented, or suppressed to support predetermined conclusions. Evaluators have an ethical obligation to design for legitimate use and to resist misuse.

Designing for use requires a theory of use — an explicit account of how findings will travel from the evaluation to decisions. This includes identifying the intended users, the timing of decisions, the format and language of communication, and the mechanisms for engaging users with findings. Delivering a 40-page report to a busy decision-maker two weeks after the decision was made is not a theory of use — it is a theory of non-use.

Effective communication for use involves: timeliness (delivering findings when they can influence decisions), accessibility (presenting findings in language and format intended users can engage with), interaction (facilitating discussion and interpretation, not just transmitting a document), and follow-up (checking whether findings were understood and used, and providing support for application).

## How to Apply
1. **Develop a theory of use.** For each evaluation, make explicit: who will use the findings, for what purpose, at what point in the decision cycle, in what format, through what communication channel?
2. **Time the findings.** Align the evaluation timeline with the decision timeline. Findings that arrive after the decision has been made are, by definition, too late.
3. **Communicate for your audience.** Decision-makers need focused briefs, not comprehensive reports. Programme staff need actionable recommendations, not academic discussions. Match the format to the audience.
4. **Facilitate interpretation.** Present findings interactively, not just in documents. Facilitate stakeholder discussion of what the findings mean and what should be done.
5. **Follow up.** After delivering findings, check whether they were understood, discussed, and applied. Offer support for implementation of recommendations.
6. **Document use.** Track whether and how the evaluation influenced decisions, thinking, and practice. This evidence of use justifies investment in future evaluations.

## Examples
**Situation:** A comprehensive evaluation of a national youth development programme produced a 120-page report with 47 recommendations. Two years later, none of the recommendations had been implemented.
**Application:** Diagnosis: the evaluation had no theory of use. The report was too long for busy decision-makers, too late for the funding cycle, and too comprehensive to prioritise. No one was personally engaged as a primary intended user, and no facilitated session connected findings to decisions. Prescription for the next evaluation: identify the programme director and treasury representative as primary intended users, focus on three to five evaluation questions linked to the upcoming funding decision, deliver a two-page executive brief at the critical decision point, and facilitate a half-day interpretation workshop with the steering committee.
**Result:** The next evaluation is designed for use from the start: specific users, focused questions, timely delivery, accessible format, facilitated interpretation.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume the evaluation report is the primary use vehicle.
**Why:** Reports are often too long, too late, and too technical for decision-makers. Interactive presentations, focused briefs, facilitated workshops, and direct conversations are often more effective for promoting use. The report may serve as a reference document, but it is rarely the pathway through which decisions are actually influenced.

**Don't:** Blame users for not using findings.
**Why:** Non-use is usually a design failure, not a user failure. If the evaluation was not designed for use — with specific users, focused questions, timely delivery, and accessible communication — non-use is the predictable and legitimate outcome.
