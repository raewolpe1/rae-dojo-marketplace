# Evaluation Questions

## When to Use
- When developing or refining evaluation questions for a new evaluation.
- When critiquing evaluation questions in a ToR — a frequent source of evaluation failure.
- When helping stakeholders move from broad interests to focused, evaluable questions.
- When the stated questions do not match the actual information needs of intended users.

## Fails When
- **You develop questions in isolation from users.** Evaluation questions must reflect the information needs of primary intended users. Questions developed by the evaluator alone, without stakeholder input, may be technically sound but irrelevant to decisions.
- **You accept unevaluable questions.** "Is the programme effective?" is too broad. "What lessons have been learned?" is too vague. Good evaluation questions are specific, focused, and answerable with available methods and resources.

## Core Concept
Evaluation questions are the bridge between intended uses and evaluation design. They translate what users need to know into questions that the evaluation can systematically investigate. The quality of the evaluation questions determines the quality of the evaluation — good questions lead to useful findings; poor questions lead to shelf-ware.

Good evaluation questions share several characteristics. They are specific enough to guide data collection and analysis — not so broad that any data could be relevant. They are evaluable — answerable with available methods, data, and resources within the timeline. They are connected to decisions — the answers will inform specific actions or judgements by identified users. They are prioritised — there are few enough that the evaluation can investigate each one thoroughly rather than superficially addressing many.

Common weaknesses in evaluation questions include: excessive breadth ("to what extent has the programme achieved its objectives?" — which objectives, for whom, measured how?), vagueness ("what lessons have been learned?" — by whom, about what, how will the lessons be applied?), unevaluability ("what would have happened without the programme?" — this is an attribution question that requires specific counterfactual designs), and disconnection from decisions (questions that produce interesting information but do not inform any identified decision).

Developing good evaluation questions is a facilitated process, not a solo exercise. The evaluator helps intended users articulate what they need to know by probing their interests, exploring their decision contexts, and testing whether proposed questions are specific, evaluable, and connected to use. The process often reveals that the initially stated questions are not the real questions — that users have deeper, more specific information needs that emerge through dialogue.

## How to Apply
1. **Start with intended uses.** What decisions will the evaluation inform? Work backward from decisions to the information needed to inform them.
2. **Facilitate question development.** Through structured dialogue with intended users, generate candidate questions, test them for specificity and evaluability, and prioritise.
3. **Apply the specificity test.** For each question, ask: would two competent evaluators interpret this question the same way and collect similar data to answer it? If not, the question is too vague.
4. **Apply the use test.** For each question, ask: if we had the answer, who would use it, for what decision? If no one can articulate the use, the question is not worth investigating.
5. **Prioritise ruthlessly.** Fewer well-investigated questions produce more useful findings than many superficially addressed questions. Limit the evaluation to the questions that matter most.

## Examples
**Situation:** A ToR for an evaluation of a provincial skills programme lists eight evaluation questions, including: "Has the programme been effective?", "What lessons have been learned?", "What is the impact of the programme?", and "Is the programme sustainable?"
**Application:** These questions are too broad, too numerous, and too disconnected from specific decisions. The evaluator facilitates a session with the intended users — the programme director and the provincial head of department — to identify their actual information needs. Through dialogue, it emerges that they need to know two things: (1) which of the three delivery models (classroom, workplace, blended) produces the best employment outcomes for which participant profiles, and (2) whether the per-participant cost is justified by the outcomes across the three models. These two focused questions replace the eight generic ones and directly inform the decision about how to design the next phase.
**Result:** Two focused, evaluable questions that will produce findings directly connected to an imminent decision, instead of eight broad questions that would produce a comprehensive but unused report.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Accept every question a commissioner proposes.
**Why:** Commissioners often include aspirational questions — things it would be interesting to know — alongside the questions that actually drive decisions. The evaluator's job is to help commissioners prioritise the questions that will be most useful, not to accept an unmanageable list.

**Don't:** Develop questions that the evaluation cannot answer.
**Why:** Promising answers to unevaluable questions — whether due to methodological limitations, data constraints, or timeline restrictions — undermines the evaluation's credibility when it inevitably fails to deliver.
