# Purposeful Sampling

## When to Use
- When selecting cases, sites, or participants for qualitative inquiry where the goal is depth and understanding rather than statistical generalisation.
- When explaining why evaluations do not always require random samples.
- When choosing among the many purposeful sampling strategies to find the one that best fits the evaluation questions.

## Fails When
- **The question requires population estimates.** Purposeful sampling does not support statistical generalisation. For prevalence, rates, or population parameters, use probability sampling.
- **You confuse purposeful with convenient.** Convenience sampling selects whatever is easiest to access. Purposeful sampling strategically selects information-rich cases that illuminate the evaluation questions. The logic, rigour, and justification are fundamentally different.

## Core Concept
Purposeful sampling is the qualitative alternative to random sampling. Rather than selecting a statistically representative sample from which to generalise to a population, purposeful sampling selects information-rich cases for in-depth study — cases from which one can learn a great deal about matters of central importance to the evaluation.

The logic is different from statistical sampling. Statistical sampling seeks representativeness and generalisability. Purposeful sampling seeks understanding and illumination. You do not need a random sample when your purpose is to understand a phenomenon in depth. You need cases that will teach you the most about that phenomenon.

Patton identifies over 40 purposeful sampling strategies. Key strategies include: maximum variation sampling (selecting cases that span the range of variation to identify common patterns across diverse cases); homogeneous sampling (selecting similar cases for focused group analysis); extreme or deviant case sampling (selecting unusual cases — the outstanding successes and notable failures); typical case sampling (selecting cases that illustrate what is normal or average); critical case sampling (selecting cases that are strategically important — "if it works here, it will work anywhere"); snowball sampling (asking informants to identify further cases); criterion sampling (selecting all cases meeting a specified criterion); and intensity sampling (selecting cases that manifest the phenomenon intensely but not extremely).

The choice of sampling strategy should be driven by the evaluation questions. If the question is about common patterns across diverse contexts, use maximum variation sampling. If the question is about what distinguishes successes from failures, use extreme case sampling. If the question is about the typical participant experience, use typical case sampling.

## How to Apply
1. **Clarify the purpose.** What are you trying to learn? The sampling strategy follows from the learning purpose.
2. **Select the strategy.** Choose the purposeful sampling strategy that best serves the evaluation questions.
3. **Define the selection criteria.** What characteristics define an "information-rich" case for this evaluation? Be specific and document the criteria.
4. **Select the cases.** Apply the criteria to identify and select specific cases, sites, or participants.
5. **Document the rationale.** Explain and justify the sampling strategy in the evaluation design. Purposeful sampling requires explicit justification because it is not random.
6. **Determine sample size by saturation.** In qualitative inquiry, sample size is determined by when new cases stop producing new information — the point of saturation — not by a statistical formula.

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluation of a national enterprise development programme needs to understand why some supported enterprises thrive while others fail, across diverse provincial contexts.
**Application:** Use maximum variation sampling to select enterprises spanning the range of outcomes (thriving, surviving, failed) and contexts (urban/rural, different provinces, different sectors). Within each category, select information-rich cases — enterprises whose trajectories are well-documented and whose owners are willing to participate in in-depth interviews. The sample of perhaps 15–20 enterprises, purposefully selected, will illuminate the patterns of success and failure far more effectively than a random sample of 200 enterprises surveyed with a standardised questionnaire.
**Result:** The purposeful sample produces rich, contextualised understanding of the mechanisms of success and failure — findings that directly inform programme design improvements. A random sample would have produced averages without explanations.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Apologise for not using random sampling.
**Why:** Purposeful sampling is not a compromise — it is the appropriate strategy when the purpose is understanding rather than generalisation. It has its own logic, its own rigour, and its own strengths. Present it with confidence, not as a consolation prize.

**Don't:** Select convenience samples and call them purposeful.
**Why:** "We interviewed whoever was available" is convenience sampling. Purposeful sampling requires a deliberate strategy, explicit criteria, and documented rationale. The distinction matters for credibility.
