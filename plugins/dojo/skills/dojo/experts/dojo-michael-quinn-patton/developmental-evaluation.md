# Developmental Evaluation

## When to Use
- When evaluating innovations, pilots, or social experiments where the programme model is still being developed and adapted.
- When the programme operates in a complex adaptive system where outcomes are emergent, non-linear, and unpredictable.
- When the evaluation needs to provide real-time feedback that supports adaptive management, not summative judgements after the fact.
- When traditional evaluation designs fail because they assume a fixed programme model, predetermined outcomes, and linear causality — none of which apply.

## Fails When
- **The programme is stable and established.** DE is designed for innovation and emergence. For well-established programmes with clear models and predictable processes, utilization-focused evaluation with conventional designs is more appropriate and more efficient.
- **DE becomes an excuse for lack of rigour.** Developmental evaluation is rigorous — it requires systematic data collection, disciplined reflection, and evidence-based adaptation. It is not "making it up as you go" or "we're complex, so we can't be evaluated."
- **There is no genuine innovation.** Some programmes are labelled "innovative" when they are simply poorly implemented versions of conventional approaches. DE is for genuine innovation in complex environments, not for conventional programmes that lack management discipline.

## Core Concept
Developmental evaluation is an evaluation approach designed to support programme development in complex dynamic environments. It applies complexity concepts — emergence, non-linearity, adaptation, self-organisation — to evaluation design and practice. Where traditional evaluation asks "did the programme achieve its predetermined outcomes?", developmental evaluation asks "what is emerging, and what does it mean for what we should do next?"

The developmental evaluator is not a detached external assessor. The evaluator is an embedded member of the programme development team whose primary function is to infuse team discussions with evaluative questions, evaluative thinking, and evaluative data, and to facilitate systematic data-based reflection. The evaluator brings an evaluative perspective to real-time decision-making, helping the team track what is happening, understand why, and adapt accordingly.

DE is distinguished from formative evaluation by the nature of the evaluand. Formative evaluation helps improve a programme model that already exists — tweaking, refining, and optimising an established design. Developmental evaluation supports the development of a programme model that is still being created — tracking emergence, documenting adaptation, and feeding back learning to innovators who are navigating uncharted territory.

Eight principles guide DE practice: developmental purpose (supporting ongoing development), evaluation rigour (systematic, evidence-based inquiry), utilization focus (designed for use by innovation team members), innovation niche (supporting innovation in complex systems), complexity perspective (embracing complexity concepts), systems thinking (understanding interconnections and feedback loops), co-creation (evaluator and innovators working together), and timely feedback (real-time information for adaptive management).

## How to Apply
1. **Verify the DE niche.** Confirm that the programme is genuinely innovative and operating in a complex environment. If it is conventional and stable, use conventional evaluation.
2. **Embed the evaluator.** The evaluator joins the programme team, participates in planning and strategy discussions, and provides evaluative input in real time — not just at reporting milestones.
3. **Track what emerges.** Document the programme's evolution: what was tried, what happened, what was adapted, and why. Create a learning history of the innovation process.
4. **Provide rapid feedback cycles.** Feed data back to the team on a short cycle — weekly, monthly, or aligned with programme decision points — not just in a final report.
5. **Facilitate sense-making.** Help the team interpret data, identify patterns, and make evidence-informed decisions about what to do next.
6. **Document the developing model.** As the programme evolves, capture the emerging programme theory — the evolving understanding of what works, for whom, in what circumstances.

## Examples
**Situation:** A provincial government is piloting a new youth employment programme that combines work readiness training, mentorship matching, and employer partnerships. The model is being adapted for each district based on local labour market conditions.
**Application:** Traditional evaluation would evaluate against fixed outcome targets set at the start. But the programme model is still being developed — the training content, mentorship structure, and employer partnership model are being adapted based on early experience. DE embeds an evaluator in the programme team to track: what adaptations are being made and why? What is working in each district context? What is the emerging programme model? The evaluator provides monthly feedback briefs that inform the team's adaptive decisions and documents the evolving theory of change.
**Result:** Instead of a summative report after two years that evaluates a programme that has already changed, the evaluation produces ongoing learning that shapes the programme's development in real time. By the end, the team has a documented, evidence-informed programme model that can be scaled.

**Situation:** A development finance institution is testing a blended finance instrument for township enterprise development. The instrument structure, risk-sharing mechanisms, and selection criteria are being adjusted based on early transactions.
**Application:** The instrument is innovative — there is no established model to evaluate against. DE tracks each transaction: what was the deal structure? What worked? What didn't? How was the instrument adjusted? The evaluator facilitates quarterly reflection sessions with the investment team to review patterns across transactions and inform adjustments to the instrument design.
**Result:** The evaluation produces a documented learning journey: the evolving instrument design, the evidence behind each adaptation, and the emerging understanding of what blended finance configurations work for township enterprises. This is far more useful than a summative evaluation of a fixed model that never existed.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use DE for every evaluation.
**Why:** DE is resource-intensive and designed for a specific niche — innovation in complex environments. Most programmes are well-enough established that conventional evaluation approaches, properly utilization-focused, are more appropriate and more efficient.

**Don't:** Confuse DE with "no evaluation plan."
**Why:** DE requires systematic inquiry: clear evaluation questions (even if they evolve), disciplined data collection, rigorous analysis, and documented learning. The difference from conventional evaluation is that the design adapts alongside the programme — not that there is no design.

**Don't:** Let the embedded evaluator lose their evaluative perspective.
**Why:** The risk of embedding is "going native" — becoming so identified with the programme team that the evaluator loses the capacity for critical, evidence-based assessment. The evaluator must maintain an evaluative stance even while being a team member.
