# Evaluation Facilitation

## When to Use
- When stakeholder engagement is central to the evaluation's success — which is most of the time in UFE.
- When you need to facilitate group processes: developing evaluation questions, interpreting findings, planning for use.
- When the evaluator's role is to facilitate evidence-based learning rather than render independent verdicts.

## Fails When
- **Facilitation becomes an end in itself.** The purpose of facilitation in evaluation is to enhance evidence-based decision-making, not to run workshops. If the facilitation does not lead to better evaluation questions, better interpretation, or better use, it is wasted effort.
- **You use facilitation to avoid evaluative judgement.** The evaluator brings analytical expertise. Facilitation means deploying that expertise in service of stakeholder learning, not abdicating the responsibility to assess evidence and reach conclusions.
- **Power dynamics prevent genuine participation.** Facilitation assumes that participants can speak freely. In contexts where power imbalances prevent honest participation, facilitation may produce performative engagement rather than genuine learning.

## Core Concept
The evaluator in utilization-focused evaluation is fundamentally a facilitator — not a judge, auditor, or independent assessor. The evaluator facilitates the evaluation process: helping intended users think clearly about what they need to know and why, guiding them through data interpretation, and supporting them in applying findings to decisions.

This facilitative role requires skills beyond technical evaluation competence: interpersonal skills, group process management, conflict navigation, active listening, synthesis, and the ability to make complex evidence accessible to non-technical audiences. The evaluator must be comfortable sharing control of the evaluation process with stakeholders while maintaining professional standards of rigour and integrity.

Facilitation operates at multiple points in the evaluation: during design (facilitating the process of identifying users, uses, and questions), during data collection (facilitating stakeholder participation in data gathering), during analysis and interpretation (facilitating collective sense-making), and during use planning (facilitating discussions about what the findings mean for decisions and action).

The evaluator brings an evaluative lens that stakeholders typically lack: the ability to frame evaluative questions, to identify appropriate evidence, to assess data quality, to interpret findings in context, and to connect evidence to action. Facilitation means deploying these skills in service of stakeholder learning, not performing them in isolation.

## How to Apply
1. **Prepare the group.** Establish purpose, roles, ground rules, and expectations before facilitated sessions. Ensure participants understand what the evaluation process entails and what their role is.
2. **Facilitate question development.** Help stakeholders move from broad interests ("is it working?") to specific, answerable evaluation questions through structured dialogue.
3. **Facilitate interpretation.** Present findings accessibly, then facilitate discussion: what do these findings mean? What alternative explanations should we consider? What are the implications for action?
4. **Manage power dynamics.** Create conditions for honest participation: anonymous input methods, small-group discussions before plenary, explicit attention to whose voices are heard.
5. **Balance stakeholder input with evaluative rigour.** Stakeholders shape the questions and uses; the evaluator maintains standards of evidence quality, analytical integrity, and honest reporting.

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluator presents preliminary findings from a skills programme evaluation to the programme steering committee. The findings suggest that the programme's theory of change is flawed — the assumed causal pathway from training to employment is not operating as expected.
**Application:** Rather than presenting a verdict ("your theory of change is wrong"), the evaluator facilitates a structured interpretation session: here is what the data show (participation data, outcome data, participant narratives), here is where the assumed pathway breaks down (the transition from training to employment), and here are possible explanations (skills mismatch, employer reluctance, labour market saturation, inadequate post-training support). The steering committee engages with the evidence, contributes contextual knowledge the evaluator lacks, and arrives at a shared understanding of what needs to change — with far greater ownership than if the evaluator had simply pronounced a judgement.
**Result:** The findings are used because the stakeholders are co-owners of the interpretation. The facilitated process builds evaluative thinking capacity in the steering committee and creates commitment to programme improvement.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Facilitate without substance.
**Why:** Workshop-style engagement without rigorous evidence produces group opinions, not evaluative conclusions. Facilitation must be grounded in systematically collected and carefully analysed data. The evaluator brings evidence to the table, not just process skills.
