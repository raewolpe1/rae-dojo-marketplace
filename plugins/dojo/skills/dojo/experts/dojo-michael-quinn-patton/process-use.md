# Process Use

## When to Use
- When stakeholder learning and capacity building are important evaluation goals alongside findings.
- When you want to design evaluation to build evaluative thinking capacity within the organisation.
- When engagement in the evaluation process — defining questions, collecting data, interpreting findings — can change how programme staff think about their work.
- When explaining to commissioners why stakeholder engagement is not just a nicety but a mechanism for use.

## Fails When
- **Process becomes a substitute for findings.** Process use supplements findings use — it does not replace it. An evaluation that produces great learning through the process but no credible findings has missed half its purpose.
- **You claim process use without designing for it.** Process use does not happen automatically. It requires deliberate design: structured stakeholder engagement, facilitated sense-making, and intentional capacity building.

## Core Concept
Process use refers to changes in thinking and behaviour that occur among those involved in an evaluation as a result of the evaluation process itself, independent of the evaluation findings. It is one of Patton's most important contributions to the field because it captures something experienced evaluators know intuitively: engaging people in evaluation changes how they think.

When programme staff participate in defining evaluation questions, they must think clearly about what the programme is trying to achieve. When they participate in data collection, they see their programme through an evidence lens. When they engage in group interpretation of findings, they practise weighing evidence, considering alternatives, and connecting data to decisions. These are evaluative thinking skills that persist long after the evaluation is complete.

Process use occurs through several mechanisms: enhanced shared understanding among stakeholders about programme logic and goals; infusion of evaluative thinking into organisational culture; development and refinement of programme theory through the discipline of articulating it; increased engagement, ownership, and morale from being involved in learning about one's own work; and network development among stakeholders who interact through the evaluation process.

The practical implication is that evaluation should be designed to maximise process use: engage stakeholders as partners throughout, facilitate collective sense-making, create structured learning opportunities within the evaluation process, and value the capacity that is built, not just the conclusions that are reached.

## How to Apply
1. **Design stakeholder engagement for learning.** Structure the engagement to build skills and understanding, not just extract information.
2. **Facilitate collective sense-making.** Bring stakeholders together to interpret data, not just to receive findings.
3. **Document process use outcomes.** Track changes in thinking, behaviour, and capacity that result from the evaluation process.
4. **Balance process and findings.** Ensure that the investment in process use does not compromise the quality of findings.

## Examples
**Situation:** A SETA commissions an evaluation of its skills programmes. Programme managers are initially resistant, viewing the evaluation as an external audit.
**Application:** The evaluator designs the process to maximise process use: programme managers participate in developing evaluation questions (forcing them to articulate programme logic), join data collection teams in field visits (seeing their programme through participant eyes), and participate in facilitated workshops to interpret findings (practising evaluative thinking). By the end, programme managers have moved from defensive resistance to engaged learning — not because the findings were positive, but because the process changed how they think about their programmes.
**Result:** The SETA gets both useful findings and an enhanced capacity for evidence-based programme management. The process use — changed thinking among programme managers — may be more valuable long-term than any single set of findings.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat stakeholder engagement as data extraction.
**Why:** If stakeholders are involved only as informants — people to interview, survey, and observe — you get data but not process use. Process use requires genuine partnership in the evaluation, not just participation as subjects.

**Don't:** Claim process use happened without evidence.
**Why:** Process use should be documented and assessed, not merely assumed. Ask participants how their thinking changed, observe changes in behaviour, and track subsequent use of evaluative approaches. Process use, like findings use, should be evaluated.
