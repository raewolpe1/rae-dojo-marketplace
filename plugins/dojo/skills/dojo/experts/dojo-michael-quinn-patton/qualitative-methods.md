# Qualitative Methods

## When to Use
- When evaluation questions ask about how and why — processes, experiences, meanings, and contexts — not just how much or how many.
- When you need to understand the diversity of participant experiences, not just the average.
- When the programme context is critical to understanding outcomes and a decontextualised measurement would miss what matters.
- When you need to capture unanticipated outcomes, unintended consequences, or emergent effects that standardised instruments would miss.
- When building understanding of a phenomenon before designing quantitative measurement.

## Fails When
- **The question requires population-level generalisation.** Qualitative methods are designed for depth and understanding, not for estimating population parameters. Use quantitative methods for prevalence, rates, and distributions.
- **You treat qualitative as easy.** Good qualitative inquiry requires disciplined skill in observation, interviewing, analysis, and interpretation. Conducting a few casual interviews does not constitute qualitative research.
- **You lack a sampling strategy.** Qualitative inquiry uses purposeful sampling — deliberate selection of information-rich cases. Without a sampling strategy, you have convenience data, not qualitative research.

## Core Concept
The purpose of qualitative inquiry is understanding — understanding what people experience, how they interpret their experiences, and what meaning they construct. This requires methods designed for depth, context, and nuance: in-depth interviews, participant observation, focus group discussions, document analysis, and case studies.

Qualitative data are thick descriptions, verbatim quotations, observed behaviours, and documented contexts. They capture what standardised instruments miss: the unanticipated, the particular, the contextual, the meaningful. The strength of qualitative inquiry is its capacity to illuminate the complexity of real-world programmes and the diversity of human experience within them.

Quality in qualitative inquiry rests on criteria suited to the paradigm: credibility (are the findings believable and trustworthy?), transferability (can the findings inform understanding in other contexts?), dependability (is the inquiry process consistent and documented?), and confirmability (are the findings grounded in the data rather than the researcher's biases?). Triangulation — using multiple data sources, methods, investigators, or theoretical perspectives — strengthens credibility. Member checking — sharing findings with participants for validation — enhances trustworthiness. An audit trail — documenting the analytical process — supports dependability.

Qualitative analysis involves identifying patterns, themes, and categories in the data through systematic coding and interpretation. Inductive analysis lets themes emerge from the data; deductive analysis uses pre-existing frameworks. In practice, most analysis combines both — starting with sensitising concepts and remaining open to emergent themes.

## How to Apply
1. **Design questions that call for qualitative depth.** How questions, why questions, and what-is-the-experience questions are qualitative territory.
2. **Select a purposeful sampling strategy.** Choose information-rich cases deliberately — not randomly, not conveniently. Match the sampling strategy to the inquiry purpose.
3. **Collect data with disciplined skill.** Open-ended interviewing, skilled observation, and careful document analysis require training and practice. Prepare protocols but follow the data.
4. **Analyse systematically.** Code data, identify patterns, develop themes, and build interpretations grounded in the evidence. Document the analytical process.
5. **Ensure quality.** Use triangulation, member checking, peer debriefing, and audit trails to strengthen the credibility and trustworthiness of findings.
6. **Report with rich description.** Qualitative findings are communicated through thick description, illustrative quotations, case narratives, and interpretive analysis — not summary statistics.

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluation of a government mentorship programme for emerging contractors needs to understand why completion rates vary dramatically across districts.
**Application:** Quantitative data show the variation but not the explanation. Qualitative inquiry — in-depth interviews with mentors, mentees, and programme coordinators in high-performing and low-performing districts — reveals the mechanisms: in high-performing districts, mentors have construction industry experience and pre-existing networks; in low-performing districts, mentors are generalist business advisors without industry knowledge. The mentorship relationship quality, not programme inputs, drives completion.
**Result:** The qualitative findings explain the quantitative pattern and point to a specific improvement: recruit mentors with sector-specific experience. This finding could not have emerged from surveys alone.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat qualitative findings as anecdotal.
**Why:** Rigorous qualitative inquiry — with purposeful sampling, systematic analysis, and quality safeguards — produces credible evidence. The findings are grounded in data, not in impressions. Dismissing them as "just stories" reflects methodological prejudice, not analytical judgement.

**Don't:** Use qualitative methods without a clear inquiry framework.
**Why:** Going into the field without clarity about what you are trying to understand, how you will collect data, and how you will analyse it produces data overload and interpretive chaos. Qualitative inquiry requires as much planning as quantitative — the planning is just different.
