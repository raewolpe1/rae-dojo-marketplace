# Complexity and Evaluation

## When to Use
- When the programme operates in an environment characterised by non-linear causality, emergence, adaptation, and unpredictability.
- When helping users distinguish between simple programmes (clear cause-effect), complicated programmes (multiple components, knowable through analysis), and complex programmes (emergent, adaptive, non-linear).
- When traditional evaluation designs assume linearity and predictability that do not match the programme reality.

## Fails When
- **You label everything as complex.** Many programmes are simple or complicated, not complex. The distinction matters because each requires different evaluation approaches. Calling a straightforward training programme "complex" does not make it so and leads to inappropriate evaluation designs.
- **Complexity becomes an excuse for vagueness.** Complex programmes can still be evaluated rigorously. Complexity demands adaptive, emergent evaluation designs — it does not excuse sloppy data collection or unfounded conclusions.
- **You use complexity jargon without analytical substance.** Terms like "emergence," "non-linearity," and "self-organisation" are analytical concepts, not decorative vocabulary. Use them with precision or not at all.

## Core Concept
Complexity theory offers evaluation a fundamental insight: in complex adaptive systems, the relationship between cause and effect is non-linear, outcomes are emergent rather than predetermined, small changes can produce disproportionately large effects, and the system continuously adapts and reorganises. This has profound implications for how we evaluate programmes operating in such systems.

The simple-complicated-complex distinction is the essential diagnostic. Simple situations have clear cause-effect relationships and predictable outcomes — following a recipe, for example. Complicated situations have multiple interacting components but are ultimately knowable through expert analysis — building a rocket, for example. Complex situations involve dynamic interactions, emergence, adaptation, and inherent unpredictability — raising a child, for example. Each type calls for different evaluation approaches.

For simple programmes, conventional designs with predetermined outcomes and straightforward measurement work well. For complicated programmes, comprehensive multi-component evaluation with careful analysis of interactions is appropriate. For complex programmes, evaluation must embrace uncertainty, track emergence, adapt in real time, and focus on understanding patterns rather than proving predetermined cause-effect chains.

Complexity-sensitive evaluation uses several key concepts: attractors (stable patterns that emerge from complex interactions), feedback loops (information flows that amplify or dampen system dynamics), tipping points (thresholds where small changes trigger large system shifts), path dependence (how history constrains current possibilities), and co-evolution (how programme and context shape each other over time).

The practical implication is not that complex programmes cannot be evaluated — they can, rigorously — but that the evaluation design must match the complexity. Pre-determined outcomes, fixed indicators, and linear causal models are insufficient. The evaluation must track what emerges, document adaptations, identify patterns, and support real-time learning.

## How to Apply
1. **Diagnose the complexity.** Is the programme simple, complicated, or complex? The answer may differ for different programme components. Design the evaluation to match.
2. **For complex components, use adaptive designs.** Developmental evaluation, contribution analysis, realist evaluation, and outcome harvesting are approaches suited to complexity. Choose based on the specific evaluation questions and context.
3. **Track emergence.** Document what happens that was not predicted or planned — unintended consequences, emergent outcomes, adaptive responses, surprises. These are often the most important findings in complex programmes.
4. **Look for patterns, not just averages.** In complex systems, average effects can be misleading. Look for patterns of variation: what works for whom, in what circumstances, through what mechanisms?
5. **Use multiple perspectives.** Complex systems look different from different vantage points. Use multiple methods, multiple data sources, and multiple stakeholder perspectives to build a multi-dimensional understanding.

## Examples
**Situation:** A national programme to strengthen local government capacity operates across 44 municipalities with vastly different contexts. The same intervention (training, systems support, mentoring) produces dramatically different results in different municipalities.
**Application:** The programme is complex — the interaction between the intervention and each municipal context produces emergent, context-dependent outcomes. A linear evaluation asking "did the programme achieve its targets?" misses the important question: "what patterns explain the variation, and what does this tell us about how to adapt the programme?" Complexity-sensitive evaluation maps the patterns: in which municipal contexts does the intervention gain traction? What contextual factors (political leadership, institutional stability, existing capacity, community dynamics) mediate outcomes? What adaptations were made by local implementers, and which adaptations worked?
**Result:** The evaluation produces pattern-level findings — not "the programme works" or "it doesn't" but "the programme works in contexts characterised by X and Y, fails in contexts characterised by Z, and requires adaptation A in contexts like B." This is far more useful for programme improvement and scaling than a simple average effect.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat complexity as a reason to abandon evaluation rigour.
**Why:** Complexity demands different rigour, not less rigour. The standards are adapted — tracking emergence, documenting adaptation, identifying patterns — but the commitment to systematic evidence, careful analysis, and honest reporting remains.

**Don't:** Diagnose every programme as complex.
**Why:** The simple-complicated-complex distinction exists precisely to avoid applying complexity-sensitive (and resource-intensive) evaluation approaches where simpler designs would be more appropriate and more efficient.
