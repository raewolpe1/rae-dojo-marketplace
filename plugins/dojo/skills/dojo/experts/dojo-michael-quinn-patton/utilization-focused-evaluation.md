# Utilization-Focused Evaluation

## When to Use
- When designing any evaluation and needing the foundational approach — start here.
- When a previous evaluation was methodologically sound but its findings were never used.
- When the ToR focuses on methods and deliverables but not on who will use the evaluation or for what.
- When stakeholders are disengaged and the evaluation feels like an external audit rather than a learning process.

## Fails When
- **Intended users genuinely cannot be identified.** In some large-scale accountability evaluations, the "users" are diffuse — legislatures, publics, oversight bodies. UFE can still be applied but requires adaptation to identify the most proximate decision-makers.
- **You treat it as a rigid step-by-step process.** The steps are iterative. You revisit earlier decisions as the evaluation unfolds. Rigidly following the sequence misses the adaptive spirit of UFE.
- **You equate "use-focused" with "giving users the answers they want."** UFE focuses on use, not on pleasing clients. Findings may be unwelcome. The commitment is to producing findings that are useful and credible, not comfortable.

## Core Concept
Utilization-Focused Evaluation begins with a premise that redefines how evaluations should be judged: an evaluation should be assessed by its actual use to its intended users. This shifts the evaluator's central preoccupation from methodological elegance to practical utility.

UFE has two essential elements. First, the primary intended users must be clearly identified and personally engaged at the beginning of the evaluation process. These are specific, real people — not categories ("stakeholders," "government," "beneficiaries") but named individuals who have the authority, opportunity, and interest to use the evaluation. Second, the primary intended uses must be identified through dialogue with those users. Uses include making decisions about the programme's future, improving programme operations, generating knowledge, holding the programme accountable, and building evaluation capacity.

Once intended users and uses are clear, every subsequent evaluation decision — questions, design, methods, data collection, analysis, interpretation, reporting, and dissemination — is made in light of how it will affect use. The evaluator facilitates this process, helping users think clearly about what they need to know and why, adapting the design based on stakeholder input rather than imposing a predetermined plan.

The UFE process has been articulated in 17 steps across five editions, though earlier versions used 5 or 12. The steps begin with assessing readiness for evaluation, identifying and engaging primary intended users, conducting a situation analysis, determining evaluation purpose, focusing evaluation questions, and selecting methods — all in service of maximising use. The steps are presented sequentially but practised iteratively.

UFE is methodologically agnostic. Because the approach prioritises use over methodological purity, it is compatible with any data collection method — quantitative, qualitative, or mixed. The choice of methods is driven by what will produce the most useful information for the intended users, not by the evaluator's disciplinary preferences.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess readiness.** Is the organisation ready for evaluation? Is there interest, capacity, and willingness to use findings? If not, readiness-building may be needed before evaluation begins.
2. **Identify primary intended users.** Name the specific people. Engage them personally. Ensure they have authority and interest to act on findings.
3. **Identify primary intended uses.** Through facilitated dialogue, determine what the users need from the evaluation: programme improvement, accountability, knowledge generation, decision-making.
4. **Focus the evaluation questions.** With users, develop specific, answerable questions that will produce the information needed for the intended uses.
5. **Select methods for utility.** Choose methods that will produce credible, useful information for the specific questions and users — not the most sophisticated methods or the evaluator's preferred methods.
6. **Design for use throughout.** At every stage, ask: will this design choice increase or decrease the likelihood that findings will be used? Adjust accordingly.

## Examples
**Situation:** A SETA commissions an evaluation of its workplace-based learning programme. The ToR specifies a quasi-experimental design with propensity score matching, a 40-page report, and six months for completion.
**Application:** UFE asks: who will use this evaluation? The SETA board needs information for the next funding cycle decision (in four months). Programme managers need information about which delivery models work best. Neither will use propensity score matching results — they need accessible findings about programme effectiveness and improvement. Redesign: identify the board and programme managers as primary intended users, focus questions on their decisions, use mixed methods appropriate to those questions, and deliver a focused brief to the board at month four with a detailed learning report to programme managers at month six.
**Result:** Two targeted products used by specific users, instead of one 40-page report used by no one.

**Situation:** An evaluation of a municipal enterprise development programme produced rigorous findings showing limited impact, but the report sat on a shelf for two years.
**Application:** Retrospective diagnosis: the evaluation was designed without engaging the intended users. The evaluation questions were generic, the methods were chosen by the evaluator, and the report was delivered after the programme had already been renewed. No one owned the findings. UFE would have identified the municipal economic development manager and the programme steering committee as primary intended users, shaped the questions around their actual decisions, and timed the findings to arrive before the renewal decision.
**Result:** The lesson is not that the evaluation was wrong — it may have been excellent methodologically. The lesson is that methodological quality without a theory of use produces shelf-ware.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Design the methodology before identifying users and uses.
**Why:** This is the most common error and the most consequential. Methods chosen in the absence of a use theory produce data that may not answer the questions users actually have.

**Don't:** Accept "stakeholders" as a sufficient answer to "who will use this?"
**Why:** "Stakeholders" is a category, not a group of people. Primary intended users must be specific individuals who will actually use the evaluation. If you cannot name them, you have not identified them.

**Don't:** Confuse use-focused with user-controlled.
**Why:** UFE focuses on use, not on giving users veto power over findings. The evaluator maintains professional independence in data collection, analysis, and interpretation. Users shape the questions and uses; they do not control the answers.
