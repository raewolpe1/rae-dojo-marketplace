# Mixed Methods

## When to Use
- When evaluation questions require both quantitative measurement and qualitative understanding.
- When you need to challenge the false dichotomy between quantitative and qualitative approaches.
- When triangulation — converging multiple methods on the same question — will strengthen credibility.
- When different stakeholders value different types of evidence and a single method will not serve all intended users.

## Fails When
- **You combine methods without rationale.** Adding focus groups to a survey "for triangulation" without a clear purpose for each method is methodological decoration, not mixed methods. Each method must answer specific questions that the other cannot.
- **You treat qualitative as illustration for quantitative findings.** Using quotes to "bring numbers to life" is not integration. True mixed methods involves both approaches contributing substantively to the analysis and conclusions.
- **Resources are insufficient for both.** A poorly executed mixed-methods study is worse than a well-executed single-method study. If the budget supports only one approach done well, do one well.

## Core Concept
The debate between quantitative and qualitative methods — the "paradigm wars" — has consumed enormous energy in evaluation and social science. Patton's position is firmly pragmatic: methods are tools, not ideologies. The right method is the one that produces the most useful information for the evaluation questions and intended uses. Often, the right answer is to combine methods.

Mixed methods designs integrate quantitative and qualitative approaches in a single evaluation to address questions that neither approach alone can adequately answer. The integration can take multiple forms: sequential designs (one method informs the other), concurrent designs (both methods are used simultaneously), or embedded designs (one method is embedded within the other).

The most common purposes for mixing methods include complementarity (each method addresses different facets of the evaluation questions), triangulation (multiple methods converge on the same question to strengthen credibility), development (results from one method inform the design of the other), initiation (deliberately seeking divergent findings to deepen understanding), and expansion (extending the breadth and range of the evaluation).

The key to genuine mixed methods is integration — not just using both methods but actively connecting them in design, analysis, and interpretation. This means designing the methods to speak to each other, analysing the quantitative and qualitative data in relation to each other, and interpreting the combined findings as a whole rather than presenting them in separate sections that never connect.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify what each method contributes.** For each evaluation question, determine whether it is best answered quantitatively (how much, how many, what proportion), qualitatively (how, why, what is the experience), or both (what is the pattern and what explains it).
2. **Design for integration.** Plan how the methods will connect: will qualitative findings explain quantitative patterns? Will quantitative data validate qualitative themes? Will one method inform the design of the other?
3. **Sequence appropriately.** Decide whether methods will be sequential (qual → quant or quant → qual) or concurrent. The sequence should reflect the purpose of mixing.
4. **Integrate in analysis.** Analyse the connections between quantitative and qualitative findings, not just the findings from each method separately. Where do they converge? Where do they diverge? What does the combined picture reveal that neither alone would show?
5. **Report the integration.** Present the mixed-methods findings as an integrated narrative, not as separate quantitative and qualitative chapters that never connect.

## Examples
**Situation:** An evaluation of a municipal business support programme needs to assess both the scale of outcomes (how many businesses created, how many jobs) and the quality of outcomes (what kind of businesses, what is the participant experience, why do some succeed and others fail).
**Application:** Mixed methods design: quantitative component (programme administrative data, participant tracking database) answers the scale questions — how many, what proportion, what trends. Qualitative component (in-depth interviews with a purposeful sample of successful and unsuccessful participants, observation of business support sessions) answers the quality questions — what is the experience, what mechanisms drive success or failure, what contextual factors matter. Integration: the quantitative data identifies patterns (e.g., higher success rates in certain sectors or for participants with certain characteristics) that the qualitative data explains (the mechanisms behind the patterns).
**Result:** The evaluation produces both the numbers the funder needs for accountability and the understanding the programme managers need for improvement. Neither method alone would have been sufficient.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Add qualitative components as an afterthought.
**Why:** Qualitative inquiry requires its own design: purposeful sampling, skilled interviewers, systematic analysis. Adding "a few interviews" to a survey study without proper qualitative design produces thin, unreliable qualitative data.

**Don't:** Maintain methodological apartheid in the report.
**Why:** Presenting quantitative findings in one chapter and qualitative findings in another, with no integration, is not a mixed-methods evaluation. It is two single-method evaluations bound together. The value of mixed methods lies in the integration.
