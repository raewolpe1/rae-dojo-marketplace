# Evaluative Thinking

## When to Use
- When building long-term organisational capacity for evidence-based decision-making.
- When the goal is evaluation independence — an organisation that thinks evaluatively without always needing an external evaluator.
- When explaining why investing in evaluation culture is more valuable than commissioning one-off evaluations.

## Fails When
- **You present it as a substitute for formal evaluation.** Evaluative thinking complements rigorous evaluation — it does not replace the need for systematic, independent evidence gathering.
- **You assume it develops automatically.** Evaluative thinking must be cultivated through deliberate practice, leadership modelling, and organisational incentives for learning.

## Core Concept
Evaluative thinking is the discipline of asking evaluative questions, seeking evidence, valuing critical reflection, and using data for learning and improvement as a routine part of organisational practice. It is a habit of mind, not a technical skill — though it draws on technical skills in data collection, analysis, and interpretation.

Building evaluative thinking capacity is more lasting than any single evaluation. The goal is not evaluation dependency — an organisation that must hire an external evaluator every time it needs to learn about its programmes — but evaluation capacity: an organisation that asks evaluative questions as a matter of course, collects and uses evidence routinely, and subjects its assumptions to critical examination.

Evaluative thinking involves four interrelated capacities: asking good questions (what are we trying to achieve? how would we know if we succeeded? what evidence would change our mind?), gathering evidence (systematically collecting relevant data), making judgements (interpreting evidence in context, weighing alternatives, reaching defensible conclusions), and using findings (connecting conclusions to decisions and actions).

## How to Apply
1. **Model evaluative questioning.** Leaders should routinely ask: what is the evidence for that claim? How do we know this is working? What would we need to see to change our approach?
2. **Build routine data practices.** Create simple, regular mechanisms for collecting and reviewing evidence — not elaborate M&E systems, but practical feedback loops.
3. **Create safe spaces for critical reflection.** Evaluative thinking requires the willingness to examine assumptions and acknowledge failures. This requires psychological safety.
4. **Invest in skills.** Provide training in basic evaluation concepts, data literacy, and evidence interpretation — not to create evaluators, but to create evidence-informed practitioners.
5. **Celebrate learning, not just success.** Organisations that reward learning from failure build evaluative thinking faster than organisations that only celebrate positive results.

## Examples
**Situation:** A development agency commissions evaluations every three years but staff between evaluations make decisions based on intuition and anecdote. The evaluations produce findings that surprise staff because they contradict assumptions held throughout the programme cycle.
**Application:** The problem is not the quality of the three-yearly evaluations but the absence of evaluative thinking between evaluations. The agency needs to build routine evaluative practices: quarterly reflection sessions where programme teams review implementation data, annual internal learning reviews, and structured after-action reviews following major programme milestones. These practices build evaluative thinking capacity so that staff continuously learn from evidence, not just when an external evaluator arrives.
**Result:** The agency moves from evaluation as an event (external, periodic, disruptive) to evaluation as a culture (internal, continuous, embedded). The three-yearly evaluations become more useful because staff are already evidence-informed.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Equate evaluative thinking with monitoring.
**Why:** Monitoring tracks indicators. Evaluative thinking interprets indicators — asking what they mean, whether the programme theory holds, and what should change. An organisation that monitors without interpreting has data, not evaluative thinking.
