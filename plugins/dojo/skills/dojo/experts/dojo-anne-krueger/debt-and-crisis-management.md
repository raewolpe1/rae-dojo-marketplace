# Debt and Crisis Management

## When to Use
- When analysing the origins of sovereign debt crises in developing countries.
- When evaluating proposals for debt restructuring, relief, or cancellation.
- When advising on fiscal and debt management to prevent future crises.

## Fails When
- **You treat debt as the cause rather than the symptom.** Debt crises are caused by the fiscal, exchange rate, and growth policies that generate unsustainable borrowing, not by the existence of debt markets or malicious creditors. Treating debt as the primary problem leads to debt relief that leaves the underlying policy failures unaddressed — producing the next crisis.
- **You ignore the moral hazard of unconditional debt relief.** Debt relief without policy reform teaches both borrowers and lenders that unsustainable borrowing will be forgiven, encouraging more of it.

## Core Concept
Krueger's analysis of sovereign debt crises, deepened by her experience managing IMF crisis responses from 2001 to 2006, identifies a consistent causal chain. Governments run fiscal deficits financed by external borrowing. The borrowed funds are used to finance consumption rather than productive investment, or to sustain overvalued exchange rates, or to finance prestige projects with low economic returns. External debt accumulates faster than the economy's capacity to service it. A trigger — commodity price decline, capital flow reversal, political crisis — exposes the unsustainability, and the country faces a sudden inability to refinance maturing debt.

The policy roots are typically fiscal indiscipline combined with exchange rate overvaluation. Fiscal deficits create a financing need; overvaluation makes external borrowing appear cheap (because the domestic currency cost of servicing foreign-currency debt is low when the currency is overvalued). When the inevitable devaluation occurs, the domestic currency value of foreign-currency debt explodes, transforming a fiscal problem into a solvency crisis.

Krueger's proposed Sovereign Debt Restructuring Mechanism (SDRM) was designed to address the collective action problem in sovereign debt workouts. When a country cannot service its debts, efficient resolution requires coordination among creditors to restructure — reducing the face value or extending maturities. But individual creditors have incentives to hold out, hoping that other creditors will accept reductions while they receive full payment. The SDRM would have created a legal framework for binding supermajority decisions on restructuring terms, analogous to corporate bankruptcy. Though the SDRM was not adopted, the logic influenced the subsequent development of collective action clauses in sovereign bond contracts.

## How to Apply
1. **When analysing a debt crisis, trace the fiscal and exchange rate policies that generated unsustainable borrowing.** The debt is the symptom; the policies are the disease.
2. **Evaluate debt relief proposals by their impact on policy incentives.** Does the relief come with credible conditionality that addresses the underlying policy failures?
3. **Assess debt sustainability by examining the primary fiscal balance, the growth rate, and the exchange rate trajectory — not just the debt-to-GDP ratio.**

## Examples
**Situation:** Zambia's sovereign debt default in 2020 and subsequent restructuring under the Common Framework highlighted the challenges of coordinating multiple creditors, including Chinese bilateral lenders, Eurobond holders, and multilateral institutions.
**Application:** Krueger's framework identifies the policy roots: Zambia borrowed heavily during the 2010s copper price boom to finance infrastructure and consumption, while maintaining fiscal deficits averaging 7–8 per cent of GDP. When copper prices fell and COVID-19 hit, debt service became unsustainable. The restructuring process validated Krueger's concerns about the absence of an orderly mechanism: the Common Framework proved slow and contentious, with disagreements between Chinese bilateral creditors and private bondholders delaying resolution for years while Zambia's economy contracted. Krueger would argue that the restructuring should be conditional on fiscal reforms that prevent recurrence — primarily a credible fiscal rule, improved debt management transparency, and reduced dependence on copper revenues through economic diversification. Debt relief without these conditions would create moral hazard, both for Zambia (which would face the temptation to borrow unsustainably again) and for creditors (who would lend at rates that do not adequately price default risk). The SADC region is particularly exposed to this dynamic: several member states (Mozambique, Zimbabwe, Malawi) face debt sustainability concerns rooted in the same fiscal indiscipline and exchange rate mismanagement that Krueger's framework identifies.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Advocate for comprehensive debt cancellation without conditionality.
**Why:** The HIPC and MDRI debt relief initiatives showed that cancellation without sustained policy reform produces temporary relief followed by renewed accumulation. Countries that reformed their fiscal and exchange rate policies after debt relief (Uganda, Mozambique initially) performed better than those that did not.

**Don't:** Blame debt crises on international capital markets.
**Why:** While creditor behaviour can amplify crises (herding, pro-cyclical lending), the root cause is domestic policy. Countries with sound fiscal and exchange rate policies do not accumulate unsustainable external debt. Shifting blame to creditors distracts from the necessary domestic policy reforms.
