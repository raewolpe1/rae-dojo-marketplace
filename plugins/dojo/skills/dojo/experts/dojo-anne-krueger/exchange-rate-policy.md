# Exchange Rate Policy

## When to Use
- When analysing the causes and consequences of exchange rate misalignment.
- When advising on exchange rate regime choices (fixed, floating, managed).
- When diagnosing the role of overvaluation in balance of payments crises and growth failures.

## Fails When
- **You treat the exchange rate as a purely monetary variable.** The real exchange rate is determined by fiscal policy, productivity, terms of trade, and capital flows as much as by monetary policy. Sustained overvaluation is almost always a fiscal problem: government spending exceeds revenue, the gap is financed by borrowing or money creation, and the resulting demand pressure appreciates the real exchange rate.
- **You recommend devaluation as a standalone policy.** Devaluation without fiscal adjustment is temporary — the real exchange rate returns to its overvalued level through domestic inflation. Devaluation must be accompanied by the fiscal and structural reforms that caused the overvaluation.

## Core Concept
Krueger regards exchange rate overvaluation as the single most damaging policy error in developing countries. Her analysis, developed through decades of country studies, identifies a characteristic pattern. Governments maintain an overvalued exchange rate — sometimes deliberately as an anti-inflation anchor, sometimes inadvertently through expansionary fiscal policy. The overvaluation operates as an implicit tax on all tradeable activities: exporters receive fewer units of domestic currency per unit of foreign exchange earned; importers pay fewer units of domestic currency per unit of foreign exchange spent.

The consequences cascade. Exporters become uncompetitive, reducing foreign exchange earnings. Cheaper imports displace domestic production in import-competing sectors. The resulting current account deficit requires financing — either through reserves drawdown, external borrowing, or capital controls. As reserves decline, the government imposes import restrictions (quotas, licensing, foreign exchange allocation) to manage the shortage. These restrictions create rents, generating the rent-seeking that Krueger documented in her 1974 paper. A black market for foreign exchange emerges, creating a dual exchange rate system with its own distortions and rent-seeking opportunities.

Agriculture suffers disproportionately because agricultural exports face the full force of the overvaluation while agricultural inputs (fertiliser, equipment) may benefit from cheaper imports — but the net effect is strongly negative, particularly for smallholders who produce exportable crops. Manufacturing suffers because the overvaluation subsidises imported capital equipment while taxing manufactured exports, creating a bias toward capital-intensive, import-dependent production.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess the real effective exchange rate relative to equilibrium.** Compare purchasing power parity estimates, the current account position, reserve adequacy, and the black market premium.
2. **Trace the fiscal roots of overvaluation.** Identify the spending programmes, subsidies, or revenue shortfalls driving excess demand.
3. **Design exchange rate adjustment as part of a comprehensive reform package.** Devaluation alone fails; it must accompany fiscal consolidation, trade liberalisation, and elimination of the administrative controls that overvaluation necessitated.

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's rand has experienced significant real appreciation during periods of commodity price booms, followed by sharp depreciations during busts, creating uncertainty for exporters and manufacturers.
**Application:** Krueger's framework clarifies the analytical priorities. South Africa's exchange rate volatility reflects its commodity dependence and capital account openness — terms of trade shocks and portfolio capital flows drive large real exchange rate swings. During appreciation phases, the manufacturing sector loses competitiveness, and workers displaced from tradeable sectors may not be re-employed when the currency depreciates — a hysteresis effect that makes each cycle ratchet down industrial capacity. Krueger would advise: maintain fiscal discipline during commodity booms to reduce demand-side appreciation pressure (the Norwegian model of saving resource revenues rather than spending them); resist the temptation to use the strong currency as an anti-inflation device; ensure that macroprudential tools are available to moderate capital inflow surges; and most critically, do not compound exchange rate volatility with additional trade restrictions that add policy-induced distortions to market-induced ones. The South African Reserve Bank's inflation-targeting framework provides some discipline, but Krueger would note that targeting consumer price inflation alone may not adequately address real exchange rate misalignment that harms the tradeable sector.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Recommend a specific exchange rate level.
**Why:** The equilibrium real exchange rate is determined by fundamentals (productivity, terms of trade, net foreign assets) that change over time. The goal is a policy framework that allows the exchange rate to adjust to fundamentals, not a target rate.

**Don't:** Assume that floating exchange rates solve all problems.
**Why:** For small, open, commodity-dependent economies, floating rates may produce excessive volatility that discourages investment in tradeable sectors. Krueger supports managed flexibility — allowing the exchange rate to adjust to fundamentals while smoothing excessive short-term volatility — rather than rigid commitment to pure floating.
