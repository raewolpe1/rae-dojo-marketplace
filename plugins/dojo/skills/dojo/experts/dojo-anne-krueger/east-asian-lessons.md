# East Asian Lessons

## When to Use
- When comparing the outcomes of outward-oriented and inward-looking development strategies.
- When using East Asian countries as counterfactuals for assessing policy alternatives.
- When evaluating whether East Asian development lessons apply to Africa and Latin America.

## Fails When
- **You present East Asia as a free-market paradise.** South Korea, Taiwan, and Singapore all had significant government intervention — in education, infrastructure, technology policy, and even selective industrial support. Krueger's argument is not that these governments did nothing but that their interventions were oriented toward building export competitiveness rather than protecting domestic markets from competition.
- **You ignore the non-replicable conditions.** East Asian success occurred under specific historical conditions: Cold War security guarantees, land reform that reduced inequality, prior Japanese colonial investment in education and infrastructure, relatively small populations (except South Korea), and geopolitical incentives for the United States to promote economic success in allied states.

## Core Concept
Krueger's use of East Asian development experience is primarily comparative and counterfactual. The East Asian economies — South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, and Hong Kong — provide the most compelling evidence that outward orientation produces superior development outcomes compared to import substitution. Starting from comparable (or lower) income levels in the 1960s, these economies grew several times faster than ISI-pursuing countries in Latin America, South Asia, and Africa.

The key policy differences that Krueger identifies are: competitive exchange rates that did not tax exports; low and relatively uniform tariffs that did not create large rents; export incentives that rewarded international competitiveness rather than domestic market capture; investment in education and infrastructure that built productive capacity; and macroeconomic discipline that provided a stable environment for private investment.

Krueger's South Korea work is particularly detailed. She documented how South Korea shifted from import substitution in the 1950s to export promotion in the early 1960s, achieving dramatic growth acceleration. The critical policy change was not the elimination of all government intervention but the reorientation of incentives from producing for a protected domestic market toward producing for the world market. Export performance became the criterion for government support — firms that exported received preferential credit, tax concessions, and bureaucratic facilitation. This created a competition for rents that, unlike ISI rent-seeking, rewarded productive performance.

The critical distinction is between interventions that reinforce market incentives and those that override them. East Asian governments intervened extensively, but their interventions were designed to correct specific market failures (coordination failures, credit rationing, technology diffusion) while maintaining the discipline of international competition. ISI governments intervened to override market signals, replacing comparative advantage with bureaucratic allocation.

## How to Apply
1. **Use East Asian comparators at equivalent income levels when evaluating developing country policy choices.** What was South Korea doing at $1,500 per capita GDP? What were its tariff rates, exchange rate policy, education spending, and infrastructure investment?
2. **Distinguish between government interventions that build export competitiveness and those that substitute for it.**
3. **Assess whether the institutional conditions for effective government intervention exist before recommending East Asian-style industrial policy.**

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's economic policy debate frequently references the "developmental state" model, with proponents citing South Korea as justification for more active industrial policy, including higher tariffs, directed credit, and state-owned enterprise expansion.
**Application:** Krueger would insist on precision. South Korea's developmental state operated under conditions fundamentally different from contemporary South Africa. South Korea in the 1960s had: a competitive (arguably undervalued) exchange rate; universal primary education and rapidly expanding secondary education; a meritocratic bureaucracy insulated from political patronage (at least in economic agencies); performance-based conditionality on government support (firms that failed to meet export targets lost access to preferential credit); and the existential motivation of military competition with North Korea. South Africa has: a volatile exchange rate subject to commodity price and capital flow shocks; a deeply unequal education system that produces insufficient technical and artisanal skills; a public service in which cadre deployment has compromised meritocratic appointment in many agencies; no credible mechanism for withdrawing support from underperforming firms or sectors; and no external competitive pressure equivalent to North Korea's threat. Invoking "developmental state" without replicating these institutional conditions is cargo cult economics — adopting the form (government intervention in industry) without the substance (competitive discipline and institutional capacity). Krueger would advise South Africa to focus on the preconditions: fix the education system, rebuild institutional capacity in economic agencies, maintain a competitive exchange rate, and ensure that any industrial support is conditional on measurable export performance.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the East Asian model as a single template.
**Why:** South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, and Hong Kong followed different strategies adapted to their specific conditions. Hong Kong was essentially laissez-faire; Singapore relied heavily on foreign direct investment; South Korea used directed credit and chaebol-based industrial strategy; Taiwan promoted SMEs and technology parks. The common element was outward orientation, not a specific institutional model.

**Don't:** Assume that East Asian lessons are irrelevant to Africa.
**Why:** The core principles — competitive exchange rates, investment in education and infrastructure, outward orientation, macroeconomic stability — are applicable everywhere. The specific instruments must be adapted to local institutional capacity and political economy, but the principles transfer.
