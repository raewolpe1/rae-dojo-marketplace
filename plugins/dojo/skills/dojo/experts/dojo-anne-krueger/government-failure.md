# Government Failure

## When to Use
- When assessing whether a proposed government intervention is likely to produce its intended outcomes.
- When explaining why market failure alone does not justify intervention.
- When evaluating the institutional prerequisites for complex regulatory or industrial policies.

## Fails When
- **You equate government failure with libertarian anti-statism.** Krueger supports government provision of public goods, macroeconomic management, basic infrastructure, education, and institutional development. Her critique targets government direction of economic activity — picking winners, managing prices, allocating credit — not all government functions.
- **You ignore the asymmetry between identifying market failure and demonstrating that government can correct it.** The existence of a market failure is necessary but not sufficient for intervention: the government must also be capable of designing and implementing an intervention that improves on the market outcome, net of all costs including rent-seeking.

## Core Concept
Krueger's theory of government failure develops from her empirical observation that government interventions in developing countries systematically produce outcomes worse than the market failures they were designed to correct. The sources of government failure are multiple and reinforcing.

Information failure is the first source. Governments lack the information to allocate resources efficiently across sectors. Which industries should be promoted? What is the socially optimal price of credit? What is the correct exchange rate? Market prices, for all their imperfections, aggregate dispersed information more effectively than any planning agency. When governments override market prices — through tariffs, price controls, interest rate ceilings, or exchange rate pegs — they replace imperfect information with systematically biased information.

Political economy is the second source. Government decisions are not made by benevolent social planners but by politicians and bureaucrats with their own interests. Politicians allocate resources to maintain political support; bureaucrats allocate resources to expand their budgets and authority. The resulting allocations reflect political power, not economic efficiency. Industries that are politically powerful receive protection regardless of comparative advantage. Regions that deliver electoral support receive infrastructure regardless of economic return.

Rent-seeking is the third source and Krueger's distinctive contribution. Every government intervention that creates differential treatment — one firm gets a licence, another does not; one sector gets protection, another does not — generates a rent. The resources devoted to capturing that rent are socially wasted. The more discretion the government exercises, the more rent-seeking it generates.

Bureaucratic incentives are the fourth source. Government agencies responsible for implementing interventions face incentives to expand their mandates, increase their budgets, and resist the elimination of programmes they administer — even when those programmes have failed.

## How to Apply
1. **For every proposed intervention, apply the government failure test alongside the market failure test.** Can the government plausibly implement this intervention better than the market allocation, given realistic assumptions about information, political economy, rent-seeking, and bureaucratic capacity?
2. **Assess the institutional capacity required for the proposed intervention and compare it with available capacity.**
3. **Favour interventions that require minimal bureaucratic discretion.** Rules over discretion, automatic mechanisms over case-by-case decisions, transparent criteria over opaque processes.

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's Industrial Development Corporation (IDC) proposes expanding its mandate to include direct equity investments in strategic industries identified by government policy, including green hydrogen, electric vehicle components, and advanced pharmaceuticals.
**Application:** Krueger's government failure framework raises four questions. First, information: does the IDC (or any government body) have the information to identify which specific firms within these broad sectors will succeed? The track record of government equity investment in developing countries is poor — political criteria typically override commercial criteria in investment decisions. Second, political economy: will investment decisions reflect economic potential or political connections? South Africa's experience with state-owned enterprise governance suggests that political influence over investment decisions is a significant risk. Third, rent-seeking: if IDC equity investment confers advantages (below-market capital, government procurement preference, regulatory access), firms will invest in obtaining IDC support rather than in productive activity. Fourth, bureaucratic incentives: the IDC has an institutional interest in expanding its portfolio regardless of returns, because its budget, staff, and prestige depend on the size of its operations, not its returns. Krueger's alternative: if the government wants to promote these sectors, invest in the public goods that all firms in these sectors need (research infrastructure, skills development, regulatory frameworks, grid infrastructure for green hydrogen) rather than picking specific firms to receive equity investment.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Present government failure as evidence that markets are perfect.
**Why:** Markets fail too — externalities, public goods, information asymmetries, and monopoly power are real. The question is comparative: given both market failure and government failure, which institutional arrangement produces the least-bad outcome for a specific problem in a specific context?

**Don't:** Assume that government failure is inherent and permanent.
**Why:** Government capacity can be built. Countries that invested in competent, meritocratic, insulated bureaucracies (South Korea, Singapore, Botswana) achieved better policy implementation than those with patronage-based civil services. The implication is to invest in institutional capacity before expanding government's economic role, not to abandon the aspiration to better governance.
