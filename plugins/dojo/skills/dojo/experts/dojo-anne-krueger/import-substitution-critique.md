# Import Substitution Critique

## When to Use
- When evaluating proposals for industrial development behind protective tariffs.
- When explaining the historical failure of inward-looking development strategies in Latin America, South Asia, and sub-Saharan Africa.
- When advising on whether domestic production targets justify trade restrictions.

## Fails When
- **You dismiss all domestic industrial development as misguided.** Krueger's critique targets the specific mechanism of high protection combined with discretionary allocation of import licences, not the aspiration to industrialise. The East Asian economies industrialised rapidly through outward orientation, proving that industrial development and openness are complements, not substitutes.
- **You ignore the self-reinforcing political economy.** ISI creates its own constituency: protected firms, their workers, the bureaucrats who administer the system, and the politicians who distribute the rents. This constituency resists reform even when the economic costs are obvious.

## Core Concept
Krueger's critique of import substitution industrialisation rests on systematic empirical evidence from the NBER multi-country studies she directed with Jagdish Bhagwati in the 1970s and 1980s. The findings were consistent across countries: ISI produced high-cost domestic industries that never achieved international competitiveness; generated massive rent-seeking around import licensing; created chronic balance of payments problems requiring ever-more-complex controls; and ultimately delivered slower growth than outward-oriented alternatives.

The theoretical logic is straightforward. Protection raises the domestic price of importable goods above the world price, creating a rent for domestic producers. These producers face no competitive pressure to improve efficiency, reduce costs, or innovate. The small domestic market limits economies of scale. The import licensing system required to administer protection creates further rents that attract DUP activities. Overvalued exchange rates — maintained to cheapen imported capital goods and intermediate inputs — tax exports and agriculture, worsening the balance of payments problem that protection was supposed to address.

The cascade of distortions is characteristic. Protection of final goods requires protection of intermediate inputs. Protection of intermediate inputs raises costs for downstream producers. Differential effective protection rates create arbitrary incentives across sectors. The bureaucratic apparatus required to manage differential tariffs, import licences, and exemptions grows continuously, absorbing administrative capacity that could be directed toward public goods provision.

## How to Apply
1. **When evaluating a proposed protection programme, demand the effective rate of protection, not the nominal tariff.** Effective protection rates reveal how much value-added is actually being subsidised and expose the arbitrary incentive structures that ISI creates.
2. **Trace the full chain of distortions from the proposed protection back through intermediate inputs, the exchange rate, and agricultural prices.**
3. **Identify the political constituencies that protection would create and assess whether credible graduation mechanisms exist to prevent permanence.**

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's Department of Trade, Industry and Competition proposes expanding local content requirements for renewable energy procurement (the REIPPPP) to include domestically manufactured solar panels, inverters, and battery storage systems.
**Application:** Krueger's framework identifies this as classic ISI logic applied to a new sector. The proposal assumes that protection (through local content requirements, which function as quantitative restrictions) will build a competitive domestic manufacturing base. Krueger's critique asks: What is the effective rate of protection for domestic solar panel manufacturing, accounting for the higher cost of domestically produced inputs? How much larger will the cost per megawatt of renewable energy be with mandatory local content? Who bears that cost — Eskom, municipalities, or end consumers through higher electricity tariffs? What is the graduation mechanism — at what point and by what measurable criteria will the local content requirements be reduced? The East Asian comparator is instructive: South Korea built a globally competitive solar manufacturing industry not through domestic content requirements but through export-oriented investment in scale and technology. Krueger would recommend instead: invest in the horizontal conditions for manufacturing competitiveness (reliable electricity supply — ironically the binding constraint — skilled technical labour, efficient logistics) rather than mandating domestic procurement that raises renewable energy costs in a country desperately short of generation capacity.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that every country's ISI experience was identical.
**Why:** The severity of ISI's costs varied with the degree of protection, the size of the domestic market, the quality of bureaucratic administration, and the duration of the regime. Brazil's ISI produced a larger and more diversified industrial base than Ghana's, though both ultimately required reform.

**Don't:** Use the ISI critique to argue against all public investment in industrial capability.
**Why:** Krueger supports public investment in infrastructure, education, and institutional capacity that enables industrial development. The critique targets the specific instruments of high protection and discretionary allocation, not the objective of industrialisation.
