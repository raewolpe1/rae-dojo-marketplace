# Agricultural Policy

## When to Use
- When analysing the impact of trade, exchange rate, and pricing policies on agriculture.
- When evaluating whether policy systematically taxes or subsidises the agricultural sector.
- When advising on agricultural reform to reduce rural poverty.

## Fails When
- **You apply the urban bias framework mechanically to every country.** While Krueger's analysis of agricultural taxation through exchange rate overvaluation and export restrictions was broadly accurate for the 1960s–1980s, some countries have shifted toward agricultural subsidy (particularly input subsidies and marketing board price supports). The framework must be applied empirically, not assumed.
- **You ignore the political economy of agricultural reform.** Urban consumers benefit from cheap food policies; removing agricultural taxation raises food prices, which is politically explosive. Reform must manage this transition.

## Core Concept
Krueger's analysis of agricultural policy, developed particularly in her work on the political economy of agricultural pricing, demonstrates that developing country governments have systematically taxed agriculture through multiple channels. The most powerful channel is exchange rate overvaluation, which reduces the domestic currency price of agricultural exports. A 30 per cent overvaluation is equivalent to a 30 per cent tax on all agricultural exports — a tax that appears nowhere in the fiscal accounts and is therefore invisible to public debate.

Export taxes and marketing board monopsonies constitute the second channel. Many developing countries imposed explicit taxes on agricultural exports (cocoa in Ghana, coffee in Tanzania, cotton in Francophone West Africa) or required farmers to sell to state marketing boards at prices below the export parity price. The difference between the marketing board's buying price and the export price was captured by the government — financing urban services, industrial development, and state employment.

Industrial protection constitutes the third channel. Tariffs on manufactured goods raise the domestic price of manufactured consumer goods and agricultural inputs (fertiliser, equipment, chemicals), reducing the real income of farmers. The net effect of protection — high prices for what farmers buy, low prices for what farmers sell — constitutes a massive implicit transfer from rural to urban areas.

The consequences for development are severe. Agriculture in developing countries typically employs 50–70 per cent of the labour force and produces the majority of export earnings. Taxing agriculture suppresses agricultural growth, reduces rural incomes, slows poverty reduction, drives rural-urban migration, and weakens export performance.

## How to Apply
1. **Calculate the total taxation of agriculture across all channels: exchange rate, export taxes, marketing board margins, and the protection of non-agricultural sectors.**
2. **Compare the implicit and explicit taxation of agriculture with public expenditure on agricultural support (extension, research, infrastructure).**
3. **Design agricultural reform as part of comprehensive trade and exchange rate reform, not as a standalone sectoral intervention.**

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa's agricultural sector operates under a complex policy environment: relatively open trade in most agricultural products (post-1996 Marketing Act reform), but significant structural legacies including dual agriculture (large-scale commercial farms alongside smallholder systems), land reform pressures, and periodic trade restrictions on strategic commodities.
**Application:** Krueger's framework highlights the residual taxation of agriculture through non-tariff channels. South Africa's agricultural trade is more liberalised than most developing countries, but agriculture is taxed through: rand volatility that creates planning uncertainty and discourages investment in long-cycle crops; infrastructure decay (rail freight from farming regions to ports, rural roads, water infrastructure) that raises marketing costs; electricity costs that have escalated dramatically with Eskom's pricing crisis; and regulatory uncertainty around land reform that suppresses agricultural land values and investment. The urban bias manifests differently in South Africa than in classical ISI countries: rather than explicit export taxes or marketing board monopsonies, the bias operates through underinvestment in rural infrastructure, inadequate support for smallholder integration into commercial value chains, and a trade policy that periodically restricts agricultural exports (maize export permits) to keep domestic food prices low. Krueger would advise: treat infrastructure investment in agricultural regions (rail, water, electricity) as the highest-return form of agricultural support; resist ad hoc export restrictions that tax producers to subsidise consumers; and design land reform to maximise agricultural productivity rather than political objectives.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that all agricultural subsidies are bad.
**Why:** Targeted subsidies that correct genuine market failures — agricultural research (public good), extension services (information asymmetry), rural infrastructure (positive externality) — can have high returns. The critique targets untargeted input subsidies, price supports, and marketing monopolies that create rents without addressing market failures.

**Don't:** Recommend agricultural liberalisation without addressing the complementary conditions.
**Why:** Removing agricultural taxation without investing in rural infrastructure, extension services, and market access may not produce the expected supply response. Farmers need roads, information, and market linkages to respond to improved price incentives.
