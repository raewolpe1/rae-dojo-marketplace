# Multilateral Trade Governance

## When to Use
- When evaluating the WTO's role in the global trading system.
- When advising developing countries on multilateral versus preferential trade strategies.
- When analysing the implications of WTO rules for development policy space.

## Fails When
- **You defend every WTO rule as optimal for development.** Some WTO provisions — particularly intellectual property rules under TRIPS and certain services liberalisation commitments — may impose costs on developing countries that exceed their benefits. Krueger supports the multilateral principle while acknowledging imperfections in specific rules.
- **You ignore the value of WTO rules as a commitment device for small countries.** For small, politically weak countries, WTO rules provide protection against arbitrary trade restrictions by larger trading partners that bilateral relationships cannot deliver.

## Core Concept
Krueger's support for the multilateral trading system rests on three foundations: the most-favoured-nation (MFN) principle as a guarantee of non-discrimination; the rule-based dispute settlement mechanism as protection for small countries; and the multilateral negotiating framework as a mechanism for reciprocal liberalisation.

The MFN principle ensures that any trade concession granted to one WTO member is automatically extended to all members. This prevents the discriminatory arrangements that characterised interwar trade policy and that preferential trade agreements reintroduce. For developing countries, MFN is particularly valuable because it ensures access to markets on the same terms as more powerful competitors — something that bilateral negotiations cannot guarantee.

The dispute settlement mechanism allows small countries to challenge trade restrictions by large countries through a legal process rather than through power-based negotiation. When Antigua can successfully challenge US restrictions on online gambling, or when Brazil can challenge US cotton subsidies, the system demonstrates its value for developing countries that lack the bargaining power to enforce their rights bilaterally.

Krueger's concern is that the proliferation of preferential trade agreements — bilateral FTAs, regional arrangements, mega-regionals — undermines the multilateral system by diverting trade (toward preferential partners and away from more efficient non-partner suppliers), creating a spaghetti bowl of rules of origin that raises compliance costs, and reducing incentives for multilateral negotiation. The political economy is perverse: each preferential agreement creates vested interests in maintaining preferences, making subsequent multilateral liberalisation (which would erode those preferences) more difficult.

## How to Apply
1. **Evaluate proposed trade agreements by their trade creation and trade diversion effects.** Does the agreement create new trade with efficient partners or divert trade from more efficient non-partners?
2. **Assess the rules of origin complexity.** Complex rules of origin raise compliance costs and may offset the tariff preference.
3. **For small developing countries, prioritise multilateral engagement over bilateral deals where the country has limited bargaining power.**

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa participates in multiple overlapping trade arrangements: SACU (common external tariff with Botswana, Lesotho, Namibia, Eswatini), SADC FTA, the EU-SADC EPA, and the AfCFTA. The country is also considering bilateral FTAs with additional partners.
**Application:** Krueger's framework identifies this as a textbook spaghetti bowl problem. Each arrangement has different rules of origin, different tariff schedules, different dispute resolution mechanisms, and different regulatory provisions. South African firms exporting to Mozambique face different rules depending on whether they claim SADC FTA or AfCFTA preferences. Cumulation rules differ across agreements, complicating regional value chain development. The compliance costs of managing multiple preference regimes fall disproportionately on smaller firms that cannot afford dedicated trade compliance staff. Krueger would recommend: use the AfCFTA as an opportunity to rationalise the overlapping arrangements by converging toward a single set of rules of origin and a single tariff schedule for intra-African trade; resist the temptation to negotiate additional bilateral FTAs that add complexity without adding significant market access (South Africa's existing agreements already cover its major trading partners); and invest negotiating capacity in WTO engagement, where the rules-based system provides the most valuable protections for South Africa's long-term trade interests — particularly in agricultural market access, where developed country subsidies remain a binding constraint.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that WTO membership prevents all trade distortions.
**Why:** WTO rules permit substantial protection (bound tariff rates are often far above applied rates), allow extensive agricultural subsidies (particularly by developed countries), and provide numerous exceptions and waivers. The WTO constrains the worst abuses but does not eliminate protection.

**Don't:** Dismiss the WTO as irrelevant because the Doha Round stalled.
**Why:** The existing WTO rules and the dispute settlement mechanism continue to provide value even without new multilateral agreements. The framework of rules, transparency, and dispute resolution is more important than any specific negotiating round.
