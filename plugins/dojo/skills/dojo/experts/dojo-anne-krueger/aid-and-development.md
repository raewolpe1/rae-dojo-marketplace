# Aid and Development

## When to Use
- When evaluating the design and effectiveness of foreign aid programmes.
- When advising on how to structure aid to promote rather than delay reform.
- When analysing whether aid dependence is undermining policy discipline.

## Fails When
- **You adopt a blanket anti-aid position.** Krueger recognises that aid can be effective for specific purposes: emergency relief, public health, infrastructure that governments cannot finance domestically, and technical assistance for institutional development. The critique is not of aid itself but of unconditional budget support that sustains bad policies.
- **You ignore the scale difference between aid and trade.** Even at its peak, aid flows to developing countries were dwarfed by the potential gains from trade liberalisation. The marginal dollar spent on trade facilitation may deliver far more development impact than the marginal dollar of budget support.

## Core Concept
Krueger's analysis of aid is grounded in her broader framework of government failure and political economy. Aid — particularly unconditional budget support — creates a fundamental incentive problem: it reduces the pressure on governments to reform policies that are causing economic failure. A government running unsustainable fiscal deficits faces a choice between painful reform and crisis. Aid that finances the deficit postpones the crisis without eliminating its cause, allowing bad policies to persist longer and accumulate larger costs than they would without external financing.

This is not an argument about donor intentions, which Krueger regards as generally benign. It is an argument about institutional incentives. Donors face their own political economy: disbursement pressure (aid agencies must spend their budgets), relationship maintenance (cutting aid damages bilateral relations), and optimism bias (each new programme is expected to succeed where predecessors failed). These pressures bias the system toward continued lending even when policy conditions are not met.

Conditionality was supposed to solve this problem by linking aid to policy reform. But conditionality faces a credibility problem: donors rarely cut off aid when conditions are not met, because the political costs of doing so (visible suffering, damaged relations, abandoned projects) are borne immediately while the benefits of policy discipline are diffuse and long-term. Recipients learn that conditions are soft constraints, which undermines the commitment device function.

The most effective aid, in Krueger's framework, is aid that builds the conditions for self-sustaining growth: infrastructure that reduces transaction costs, education that builds human capital, institutional development that improves governance, and technical assistance that transfers knowledge. The least effective aid is unconditional budget support that substitutes for domestic resource mobilisation and delays reform.

## How to Apply
1. **Evaluate whether aid is substituting for or complementing domestic policy reform.** If aid is financing deficits that result from unsustainable spending, it is delaying reform.
2. **Assess whether aid conditionality is credibly enforced.** If conditions are routinely waived, conditionality is not functioning as a commitment device.
3. **Compare the cost-effectiveness of aid with the potential gains from trade policy reform.** In many contexts, the largest gains come from policy reform that costs the donor nothing.

## Examples
**Situation:** Malawi receives budget support from multiple donors equivalent to approximately 15 per cent of government revenue. The country faces chronic fiscal deficits, recurrent food crises linked to agricultural policy failures, and governance challenges.
**Application:** Krueger's framework raises uncomfortable questions. Has sustained budget support reduced the political pressure on Malawi's government to address the root causes of fiscal instability — primarily the fertiliser subsidy programme (FISP), which absorbs a disproportionate share of the agricultural budget while delivering questionable productivity gains? Has donor financing of the deficit allowed the government to maintain an overvalued exchange rate longer than it otherwise could, taxing tobacco and other agricultural exports? Would Malawi's policy framework be better or worse if donors had linked budget support more rigorously to agricultural policy reform, fiscal consolidation, and exchange rate adjustment? Krueger would recommend restructuring aid toward specific investments with measurable returns — agricultural research and extension, trade facilitation at the Nacala corridor, primary health and education — while reducing unconditional budget support and strengthening conditionality on macroeconomic policy. The political difficulty is real: reducing budget support causes visible short-term harm (reduced social spending, currency depreciation) while the benefits of improved policy incentives are long-term and diffuse.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that cutting aid automatically produces better policy.
**Why:** In some cases, aid reduction without reform support produces crisis without reform — the worst of both worlds. The goal is to restructure aid to strengthen reform incentives, not to withdraw support entirely.

**Don't:** Ignore humanitarian considerations in the pursuit of policy discipline.
**Why:** Krueger does not advocate withholding emergency relief or basic health and education support to enforce macroeconomic conditionality. The critique targets budget support that sustains unsustainable fiscal and trade policies, not all categories of assistance.
