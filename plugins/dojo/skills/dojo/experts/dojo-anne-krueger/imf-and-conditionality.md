# IMF and Conditionality

## When to Use
- When evaluating IMF programme design and effectiveness.
- When explaining why conditionality exists and what function it serves.
- When advising on the structure of conditions attached to development lending.

## Fails When
- **You present conditionality as purely external imposition.** Krueger's central insight is that reform-minded officials within governments often welcome conditionality because it provides political cover for policies they know are necessary but cannot implement without external pressure. Conditionality is a commitment device, not just a disciplinary mechanism.
- **You defend every specific conditionality package.** Krueger acknowledges that conditionality has often been poorly designed — too many conditions, conditions unrelated to the core macroeconomic problem, conditions that exceeded government implementation capacity, and conditions that reflected donor ideology rather than recipient country needs.

## Core Concept
Krueger's understanding of conditionality, shaped by her tenure as IMF First Deputy Managing Director (2001–2006), is built on a political economy foundation. Governments facing economic crises typically know what reforms are necessary — fiscal consolidation, exchange rate adjustment, trade liberalisation, financial sector cleanup. The obstacle is not ignorance but political opposition from groups benefiting from the status quo.

Conditionality addresses this political economy problem by creating an external constraint that raises the cost of policy reversal. A finance minister who can tell parliament that the IMF programme requires fiscal consolidation faces less political opposition than one who must advocate for austerity on its own merits. The external commitment device makes reform politically feasible.

The effectiveness of conditionality depends on programme design. Krueger identifies several design principles from experience. Conditions should focus on the core macroeconomic and structural issues causing the crisis — fiscal sustainability, exchange rate policy, financial sector soundness — rather than expanding into peripheral areas. The number of conditions should be manageable given government capacity. Conditions should be monitorable and verifiable, not vague aspirations. And conditions should be sequenced to deliver early wins that build reform momentum.

Krueger also addressed sovereign debt restructuring during her IMF tenure, proposing a Sovereign Debt Restructuring Mechanism (SDRM) that would have created an international framework analogous to domestic bankruptcy law. The proposal was ultimately blocked by the United States and emerging market creditor nations, but it reflected Krueger's recognition that crisis resolution requires institutional mechanisms, not just case-by-case negotiation.

## How to Apply
1. **Design conditionality around the binding constraints causing the crisis.** Identify the three or four policy failures most responsible for the macroeconomic imbalance and focus conditions there.
2. **Assess government capacity to implement conditions before imposing them.** Conditions that exceed implementation capacity produce paper compliance without genuine reform.
3. **Sequence conditions to produce early visible benefits.** Quick wins build domestic support for continued reform and demonstrate that conditionality is not purely punitive.
4. **Build in flexibility for unforeseen shocks while maintaining credibility on core commitments.**

## Examples
**Situation:** A Southern African country faces a balance of payments crisis driven by fiscal deficits, exchange rate overvaluation, and declining commodity export revenues, and approaches the IMF for emergency financing.
**Application:** Krueger's framework structures the programme design. Core conditions should address the proximate causes: fiscal consolidation focused on reducing the deficit to a sustainable path; exchange rate adjustment to a competitive level (likely through devaluation combined with a move to greater flexibility); and elimination of the most distortionary trade restrictions (import bans, ad hoc tariff exemptions) that compound the overvaluation problem. Structural conditions should be limited to reforms directly linked to the crisis: improving revenue administration if tax collection is a core fiscal problem; cleaning up directed lending if financial sector fragility is contributing to fiscal drain. Conditions should not extend to issues like labour market reform, privatisation targets, or governance indices unless these are directly causing the macroeconomic crisis. The programme should include a clear adjustment path with quarterly reviews, allowing conditions to be modified if external shocks (commodity price movements, regional instability) change the situation. The political economy dimension is critical: identify which domestic actors benefit from reform (exporters gaining from devaluation, consumers gaining from lower import prices) and design the programme to deliver those benefits early.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Assume that conditionality failure means conditionality is wrong in principle.
**Why:** Programme failure often reflects poor design (too many conditions, wrong conditions, insufficient government ownership) rather than a fundamental problem with the concept. The solution is better-designed conditionality, not unconditional lending.

**Don't:** Treat all criticism of IMF programmes as evidence of anti-reform sentiment.
**Why:** Legitimate criticism exists regarding the pro-cyclicality of fiscal conditionality during demand contractions, the pace of capital account liberalisation conditions, and the social costs of adjustment. Krueger acknowledges these criticisms while maintaining that the alternative — unconditional lending that sustains unsustainable policies — is worse.
