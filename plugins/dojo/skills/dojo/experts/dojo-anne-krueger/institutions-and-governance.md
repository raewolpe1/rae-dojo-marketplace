# Institutions and Governance

## When to Use
- When assessing whether a country's institutional capacity is adequate for proposed policy interventions.
- When evaluating the relationship between governance, policy, and growth outcomes.
- When advising on how to sequence institutional reform relative to economic policy reform.

## Fails When
- **You adopt the "institutions first" position uncritically.** Krueger acknowledges that institutions matter but insists that getting economic policy right is both more immediately feasible and more immediately productive than institutional transformation. Good institutions develop alongside economic growth — they are as much a consequence as a cause of development.
- **You use institutional weakness as an excuse for inaction.** Every country has the institutional capacity to maintain a competitive exchange rate, avoid hyperinflation, and reduce the most extreme trade distortions. These basic policy reforms do not require sophisticated institutions.

## Core Concept
Krueger's position on institutions is distinctive within the development economics debate. Against the "institutions first" school (North, Acemoglu, Robinson), she argues that the causation between institutions and development runs in both directions and that waiting for institutional quality to improve before pursuing economic reform is both unnecessary and counterproductive.

Her reasoning is empirical. South Korea in 1961 had weak institutions by most measures — a recently impoverished, war-devastated country with a military government, corruption, and limited rule of law. It achieved transformative economic growth through policy reform (export orientation, competitive exchange rate, investment in education) that preceded and ultimately supported institutional development. Conversely, many countries with comparatively better institutions (India at independence, with its inherited British legal and administrative system) pursued terrible economic policies and grew slowly for decades.

The relationship Krueger identifies is: sound economic policies create the conditions for institutional improvement. Economic growth generates the fiscal resources for better public administration. Competitive markets reduce the rents that fuel corruption. Outward orientation exposes bureaucracies to international standards. Rising incomes create a middle class that demands better governance. The policy-institutional development nexus is virtuous when policies are good and vicious when policies are bad — poor policies generate rents, rents corrupt institutions, corrupted institutions defend poor policies.

This does not mean institutions are irrelevant. Krueger insists on assessing institutional capacity before recommending complex interventions. An industrial policy that would work with Singapore's civil service will fail catastrophically with a politicised, patronage-based bureaucracy. The institutional capacity assessment determines not whether to reform but which instruments are feasible.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess institutional capacity as a binding constraint on intervention design, not as a precondition for all reform.** Basic macroeconomic reforms require less institutional capacity than complex industrial policy.
2. **Favour policy instruments that require minimal bureaucratic discretion.** Rules-based systems are more robust to institutional weakness than discretionary systems.
3. **Design reforms that create virtuous cycles between policy improvement and institutional development.**

## Examples
**Situation:** South Africa scores relatively well on institutional quality indices (independent judiciary, free press, functioning parliament, strong central bank) yet faces persistent governance failures in service delivery, state-owned enterprises, and procurement.
**Application:** Krueger's framework identifies this as a case of institutional duality — strong formal institutions coexisting with weak implementation capacity. South Africa's constitution and legal framework are among the most sophisticated in the developing world, but the gap between institutional design and implementation capacity is large and growing. Cadre deployment has eroded meritocratic appointment in many government agencies. SOE governance has been compromised by political interference, most visibly at Eskom, Transnet, and Prasa. Municipal service delivery has collapsed in many areas despite constitutionally mandated frameworks. Krueger would argue that the institutional quality indices are misleading because they capture formal structures rather than functional capacity. Her advice: reduce the demands placed on weak implementation institutions by simplifying policy instruments (uniform tariffs rather than complex rebate systems, rule-based fiscal policy rather than discretionary spending, competitive procurement rather than preferential procurement requirements); rebuild meritocratic appointment in key economic agencies (National Treasury, SARS, the Reserve Bank have maintained capacity where others have not); and use the strong formal institutions (judiciary, auditor-general, public protector) to impose accountability on weak implementing agencies.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Rank countries on institutional quality indices and assume the ranking determines development potential.
**Why:** Institutional quality indices measure formal structures, not functional capacity. A country with a sophisticated legal framework and a corrupt bureaucracy may score well on indices while implementing policy poorly. Conversely, a country with simple but effective institutions may score poorly while delivering good outcomes.

**Don't:** Recommend "institutional reform" as a general solution without specifying which institutions, what reforms, and in what sequence.
**Why:** "Improve institutions" is not actionable policy advice. Krueger demands specificity: which institutional failure is binding? What specific change is needed? What is the political feasibility of that change?
