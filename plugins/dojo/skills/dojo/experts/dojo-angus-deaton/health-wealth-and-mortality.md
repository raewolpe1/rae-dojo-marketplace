# Health, Wealth, and Mortality

## When to Use
- When the health-income relationship is being analysed.
- When mortality data is being interpreted.
- When integrating health and economic policy.

## Fails When
- **The relationship is treated as purely income-driven.** Public-health institutional investment matters substantially, often more than household income.

## Core Concept

A central thread of Deaton's work is the empirical investigation of the relationship between health (especially mortality) and economic conditions. The findings:

**Income and health are positively related across countries and over time.** Higher-income countries have lower mortality, higher life expectancy, lower infant mortality. The Preston curve (life expectancy vs income per capita) captures this — though with important deviations and shifts over time.

**The relationship is not purely income-driven.** Two countries at the same income level can have substantially different mortality outcomes, depending on public-health infrastructure, sanitation, vaccination, education, and political-institutional factors. The Soviet Union historically had high mortality despite respectable income; Costa Rica has long had higher life expectancy than its income would predict.

**The Preston curve has shifted upward over time.** Countries at the same real income now have higher life expectancy than countries at that income earlier. The shift reflects diffusion of public-health knowledge and practice, vaccines, and infrastructure. Income matters but is not the whole story.

**Within-country mortality gradients are substantial.** Even within rich countries, lower-income groups have higher mortality. The gradient reflects differential access to health services, differential exposure to risk, differential health-related behaviour. The gradient is not solely income-driven; education, occupational risk, and social-network factors matter.

**The mortality response to income shocks is asymmetric.** Sustained income growth supports gradual mortality decline; income collapses (post-Soviet Russia, AIDS-era Africa, COVID-period spikes) can produce rapid mortality increases. The asymmetry suggests that the institutional foundations of mortality decline are more easily destroyed than built.

**Public-health institutions are the key durable foundation.** Vaccination, sanitation, basic health-service infrastructure, maternal and child health programmes, control of infectious disease — these institutional investments deliver mortality decline that income alone does not. Countries that invest in these institutions outperform their income peers; countries that don't underperform.

**Inequality and mortality.** Within societies, mortality inequality has often been increasing — the rich-poor mortality gap has widened in many countries over recent decades. The mechanisms include differential access to advances in medical technology, differential exposure to occupational and environmental risk, and differential socio-economic stress.

**The South African case is distinctive.** The country had long-running racial-mortality inequalities; the post-1994 period saw the AIDS pandemic produce massive mortality crisis (life expectancy fell from ~63 in 1990 to ~52 by 2005 before recovering with ART rollout); current life expectancy (~65) reflects the recovery and ongoing public-health challenges. Mortality patterns by race, geography, and HIV status remain unequal. The integration of HIV/TB/non-communicable-disease responses is the current frontier.

## How to Apply

1. **Integrate health and economic analysis** rather than treating them separately.
2. **Recognise that institutional investment matters more than income alone.**
3. **Pay attention to mortality inequality** within societies.
4. **Use mortality as a foundational development indicator**, not as an afterthought.

## Examples

**Situation:** South African development planning that focuses on economic growth.

**Application:** The Deaton lens: economic growth contributes to mortality decline but is not sufficient. Mortality decline depends substantially on public-health institutional investment — primary healthcare quality, vaccination coverage, maternal-and-child health, HIV/TB programmes. Growth without these investments would deliver less mortality decline than the integrated approach. The planning should include explicit health-institutional targets alongside growth targets.

**Result:** A development plan that integrates health and economic dimensions.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat income growth as automatic delivery of health improvement.
**Why:** Public-health institutional investment is what translates growth to mortality decline.

**Don't:** Treat health outcomes as separate from development.
**Why:** Health is constitutive of development; it is not an add-on.
