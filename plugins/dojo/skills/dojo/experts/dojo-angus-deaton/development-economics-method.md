# Development Economics Method

## When to Use
- When designing a research project.
- When evaluating methodological choices.
- When debating what counts as good development-economics evidence.

## Fails When
- **The framework is treated as one approach to the exclusion of others.** Deaton's argument is for pluralism.

## Core Concept

Beyond the critiques of RCTs and IV, Deaton has articulated what good development-economics method looks like. The elements:

**Theory matters.** Empirical work without theory is incomplete. Theory provides the framework for interpreting results, generalising findings, and connecting empirical results to policy questions. The anti-theory turn of the randomistas underweights what theory can do.

**Multiple methods are needed.** RCTs for the questions they answer. Structural empirical work for questions about mechanisms. Historical-comparative work for institutional questions. Descriptive work for measurement and pattern-finding. Each has its strengths; the methodology should match the question.

**Measurement comes first.** Before causal-identification, you need the data. Survey design, administrative-data integration, vital-registration completeness, GDP estimation — these are foundational. Poor measurement underlies poor inference, regardless of methodology.

**Context matters.** Development is context-specific. Findings from one context may or may not transfer to another. The work of careful contextualisation — institutional analysis, political-economy understanding, qualitative understanding — supports the quantitative work.

**Honesty about uncertainty.** Strong claims require strong evidence. Where evidence is uncertain, the claims should be qualified. The discipline's incentive structure rewards strong claims; resisting this is part of good practice.

**Engagement with policy at the right level.** Development economics has policy relevance, but the specific policy questions vary across contexts and time. Generic policy advice (more openness, more education, more cash transfers) is often less useful than contextually specific analysis.

**Replication and verification.** Findings should be replicable; data should be available; methods should be transparent. The discipline has made progress on this but still has work to do.

**Long-time-horizon perspective.** Many development questions operate at decades-long horizons. Short-term effects can mislead about long-term consequences. The historical-comparative methods that capture long horizons (institutions, growth, demographics) are essential alongside short-term empirical work.

**Pluralism in evaluation.** When evaluating evidence, give weight to multiple methods. A finding supported by multiple methods (RCT plus structural plus historical) is more trustworthy than one supported by one method only. Triangulation across methods is the goal.

**Engagement with non-economists.** Development is interdisciplinary — political science, anthropology, history, public health, sociology, philosophy all contribute. The economist who works in isolation produces narrower work. Cross-disciplinary engagement strengthens the discipline.

**For practical M&E work:** the Deaton approach supports a multi-method evaluation practice. RCTs where appropriate; quasi-experimental designs where RCTs aren't feasible; structural and contextual analysis to support generalisation; descriptive work to characterise the population and intervention; political-economy analysis to understand sustainability. The combination is more demanding than any single approach but produces more credible conclusions.

## How to Apply

1. **Match method to question.**
2. **Build measurement before causal-identification.**
3. **Use multiple methods for important questions.**
4. **Engage non-economists** in development analysis.
5. **Be honest about uncertainty.**

## Examples

**Situation:** Designing a South African evaluation of an early-childhood-development intervention.

**Application:** The Deaton-style multi-method approach: RCT or quasi-experimental design for causal identification of programme effects on specific child outcomes; descriptive measurement of programme implementation and population characteristics; structural analysis of how the intervention interacts with the household economy; comparative-historical analysis of similar programmes' long-run effects; political-economy analysis of sustainability under current state-capability conditions. The combination produces a fuller evaluation than any single method.

**Result:** An evaluation practice that engages the question multidimensionally.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat any single methodology as the gold standard.
**Why:** The right method depends on the question.

**Don't:** Confuse methodological pluralism with methodological softness.
**Why:** Pluralism requires more rigour, not less — each method has to be applied properly.
