# Household Economics and Consumption

## When to Use
- When analysing household-level data or behaviour.
- When designing household surveys.
- When interpreting consumption patterns.

## Fails When
- **The household is treated as a unified decision-making unit.** Intra-household variation matters.

## Core Concept

Deaton's foundational work — *The Analysis of Household Surveys* (1997), *Understanding Consumption* (1992), the AIDS demand system with Muellbauer — built the methodological apparatus for credible household-level economic analysis. The key elements:

**Household behaviour is the right level for much of microeconomic analysis.** Household-level data captures consumption, income, labour-supply decisions, savings, demographic change. Aggregate data without micro-foundations is often misleading.

**Surveys are the foundation.** Most household economic analysis depends on survey data. Survey design substantially affects what's measured: questions, recall periods, sample structure, response rates, weighting all matter. Good survey methodology is technical work; poor methodology produces unreliable data.

**Engel curves and the structure of consumption.** As income rises, the share of consumption spent on different goods changes systematically. Food's share falls (Engel's Law); housing's tends to remain stable; luxury goods' share rises. The patterns are durable across countries and times, but the levels differ. Engel curve estimation is foundational to welfare analysis.

**The AIDS demand system** (Almost Ideal Demand System, Deaton-Muellbauer) provides a flexible, empirically tractable framework for estimating consumer demand across goods. It has become a standard tool in applied consumer economics.

**Consumption smoothing.** Households use savings, borrowing, asset accumulation/decumulation to smooth consumption across income volatility. The smoothing is imperfect (credit constraints bind for the poor) but substantial. Income volatility translates only partly into consumption volatility.

**Intra-household allocation matters.** The household is not always a unified decision-maker. Bargaining models show different outcomes by who controls resources within the household. Gender, age, and intra-family power dynamics shape who gets what. Aggregate household measures can hide substantial within-household inequality.

**The lifecycle hypothesis** (Modigliani; Friedman's permanent-income hypothesis): households smooth consumption over the lifecycle, saving in working years to support retirement. The hypothesis has been substantially empirically supported but with important deviations (precautionary saving, bequests, credit constraints).

**The South African household structure.** Multi-generational households, remittance flows, fluid household membership (rural-urban migration), and the central role of social grants make the South African household economy distinctive. Standard household-economics frameworks need adaptation. The General Household Survey, Living Conditions Survey, and NIDS provide the empirical base.

**Implications for development practice:**

- Build the survey infrastructure that good analysis requires.
- Disaggregate within households when feasible (gender, age).
- Recognise that household structure affects what household-level data means.
- Use multiple data sources to triangulate.
- Be alert to measurement error and survey-design issues.

## How to Apply

1. **Treat household surveys as the empirical foundation** of poverty and welfare analysis.
2. **Engage methodology** before engaging conclusions.
3. **Disaggregate within households** where data permits.
4. **Use multiple datasets** to cross-check key findings.

## Examples

**Situation:** Evaluating South African social-grant impacts using household survey data.

**Application:** The Deaton lens: the grant flows to specific household members but household consumption decisions distribute the effect. Intra-household allocation patterns affect who benefits. The household economy includes complex remittance flows. The survey methodology has evolved; trend analysis requires care about comparability. Multiple data sources (GHS, NIDS, LCS) provide triangulation. The analysis should engage all these dimensions, not just report the headline effect.

**Result:** A household-economics analysis grounded in the actual structure of household behaviour.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Treat the household as monolithic.
**Why:** Intra-household variation is substantial and matters for welfare.

**Don't:** Use survey data without engaging the survey methodology.
**Why:** Methodology choices substantially shape what the data shows.
