# Critique of RCTs (Deaton Version)

## When to Use
- When RCT results are being used to guide policy.
- When the methodology debate in development economics is at issue.
- When external validity is being assumed without justification.

## Fails When
- **The critique is read as dismissing all RCT work.** Deaton acknowledges substantial value within scope.

## Core Concept

Deaton's *Instruments, Randomization, and Learning about Development* (JEL 2010) and subsequent work develops a methodologically sophisticated critique of RCT-led development economics. The argument:

**RCTs solve internal-validity problems within the experimental population.** Random assignment ensures that the treatment and control groups are statistically comparable on observed and unobserved characteristics. The estimated treatment effect is causal within the population.

**Internal validity does not give external validity.** The treatment effect estimated for the experimental population may not apply to other populations, other implementation conditions, or other periods. Different populations have different baseline characteristics, different complementary conditions, and different responses. External validity requires additional reasoning the RCT does not provide.

**The average treatment effect hides important heterogeneity.** Within the experimental population, the effect varies across individuals. The ATE summarises this variation but does not capture it. For policy purposes, knowing the variation matters as much as knowing the average.

**Random assignment can fail in subtle ways.** Compliance, spillovers, Hawthorne effects, demand characteristics — all can confound the inference. The randomistas' insistence on the experimental rigour can paper over these problems.

**RCTs cannot address the strategic questions of development.** Industrial policy, state-capability building, growth strategy, institutional reform — these are not amenable to random assignment at the relevant scale. The RCT toolkit answers a narrow band of micro-intervention questions. The strategic questions require other methods (historical-comparative, structural-empirical, theory-informed analysis).

**The methodological consensus underweights theory.** The randomistas' programme treats theory as suspect and empirical "atheoretical" identification as ideal. Deaton's argument: theory is necessary for generalising findings, for understanding mechanisms, and for connecting empirical results to policy questions. A-theoretical empiricism is incomplete.

**The discipline's incentive structure has narrowed development economics.** Top journals, donor funding, J-PAL's prominence have channelled attention toward what RCTs can answer. The big questions of development — industrial policy, state capability, growth strategy — have received less attention precisely because they don't fit the methodology.

**The aid-industry feedback loop.** RCTs are convenient for the aid industry. They produce measurable, reportable, fundable interventions. They reduce reliance on uncomfortable structural and political-economy questions. The methodology and the aid architecture have evolved together in mutually reinforcing ways.

**The Banerjee-Duflo response.** Banerjee and Duflo have responded that their methodology is not the only one, that they recognise its limits, that they apply it to specific questions where it works. Deaton has acknowledged these points but maintained that the discipline's centre of gravity has shifted in problematic ways.

## How to Apply

1. **Distinguish internal from external validity** in every application.
2. **Be sceptical of strong policy claims from any single RCT.**
3. **Combine RCT evidence with structural, historical, and political-economy reasoning.**
4. **Resist the methodological narrowing of development economics.**

## Examples

**Situation:** A donor is proposing a cash-transfer programme for South Africa based on RCT evidence from multiple African countries.

**Application:** The Deaton approach: the RCT evidence shows that cash transfers can affect specific outcomes under specific conditions. Whether the South African application would produce comparable effects depends on the structural conditions — the existing social grant system (substantial), the labour market (structurally different), the household economy, the implementation system. The evidence supports the consideration of cash transfers; it does not by itself justify the specific design or scale. Additional reasoning is required: theoretical, structural, political-economy. The right next step is not "scale based on RCT evidence" but "design with the RCT evidence as one input alongside structural analysis".

**Result:** A policy process that uses RCT evidence appropriately within a broader analytical framework.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Dismiss all RCT work.
**Why:** Within scope, the methodology is valuable. Deaton's argument is about its limits, not its illegitimacy.

**Don't:** Use the critique to defer policy action indefinitely.
**Why:** Decisions are made under uncertainty. The framework supports better-informed decisions, not paralysis.
