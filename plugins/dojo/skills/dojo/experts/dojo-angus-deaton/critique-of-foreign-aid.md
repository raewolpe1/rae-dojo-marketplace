# Critique of Foreign Aid

## When to Use
- When aid effectiveness is being debated.
- When designing or evaluating aid programmes.
- When considering the political-economy effects of aid.

## Fails When
- **The argument is read as opposition to all forms of assistance.** Deaton distinguishes humanitarian aid, technical cooperation, and structural aid.

## Core Concept

Deaton's critique of foreign aid is most fully developed in *The Great Escape*. The central argument:

**Aid changes the political relationship between government and citizens.** In a tax-funded state, the government is accountable to taxpayers — at least to some extent. In an aid-funded state, the government is accountable to donors. The political-economy literature is clear about what this does to incentives: it weakens the citizen-state accountability that is foundational to inclusive institutional development.

**Aid often props up bad governments.** Substantial aid flows have sustained extractive regimes that would otherwise face fiscal pressure or political instability. The aid relationship can extend the lifespan of governments that would otherwise be forced to reform.

**Aid creates parallel structures that weaken the state.** When donors fund their own projects, they create parallel implementation structures, recruit talent away from the state, and substitute for state functions. The long-run effect can be reduced state capability — the opposite of the development goal.

**The aid industry has its own incentives.** Donors need to demonstrate impact to their domestic constituencies; they need measurable outcomes; they need projects that can be reported on. These incentives shape what aid funds, often in ways that depart from what would actually advance development.

**Aid evaluation is structurally biased.** Donors fund the evaluators; the evaluators have incentives to find effects; the published literature is biased toward positive findings; the unpublished literature shows much more mixed results.

**Humanitarian aid is different.** In acute crises (famine, disaster, conflict displacement), aid saves lives that would otherwise be lost. The political-economy concerns are less pressing in these contexts. Deaton supports humanitarian aid robustly.

**Technical cooperation is also different.** Knowledge transfer, training, and specific technical assistance can be valuable without the political-economy distortions of large financial flows. The structural distinction matters.

**The Sachs-Easterly debate.** Jeffrey Sachs argues for a "big push" of aid to break poverty traps. William Easterly argues that top-down aid planning consistently fails and that bottom-up "searcher" approaches are better. Deaton sides more with Easterly on the political-economy issues but is sceptical of strong claims from either side. The empirical evidence on aid effectiveness is mixed and methodologically contested.

**The Banerjee-Duflo response.** The randomistas argue that specific aid-funded interventions have measurable effects on specific outcomes. Deaton acknowledges this within scope but maintains that the political-economy effects of structural aid flows can outweigh the intervention-level effects.

**Implications for practice:**

- Maintain humanitarian aid.
- Substantially reduce structural aid that funds government recurrent operations.
- Move toward technical cooperation rather than financial transfers where possible.
- Where structural aid continues, design it to support citizen-state accountability, not weaken it.
- Be honest about the political-economy effects in aid design and reporting.

**For South Africa:** the country has shifted from substantial aid recipient (1994-mid-2000s) to net contributor to African development. The current aid landscape is more about technical cooperation and specific programmes than structural budget support. The political-economy concerns are less acute than for highly aid-dependent countries, but the lessons inform South Africa's role in regional development.

## How to Apply

1. **Distinguish humanitarian, technical, and structural aid** and analyse each separately.
2. **Apply political-economy analysis** to any aid programme.
3. **Design aid to strengthen citizen-state accountability**, not substitute for it.
4. **Be honest about evaluation limits** in aid effectiveness claims.

## Examples

**Situation:** A donor proposes substantial budget support to a partner government in the SADC region.

**Application:** The Deaton lens: budget support increases the government's revenue from external sources, weakening the citizen-state fiscal accountability that supports inclusive institutional development. The political-economy effect should be weighed against the short-run delivery effect. Alternatives — sector-specific support with country ownership, technical assistance, support for civil society and accountability institutions — may produce better long-run development outcomes even if short-run measured impact is smaller.

**Result:** An aid design that takes political-economy effects seriously.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use the critique to oppose all forms of international cooperation.
**Why:** Humanitarian and technical cooperation can be valuable; the argument is about structural aid flows.

**Don't:** Reduce the question to "does aid work?".
**Why:** The question depends on the type of aid, the recipient context, and the time horizon.
