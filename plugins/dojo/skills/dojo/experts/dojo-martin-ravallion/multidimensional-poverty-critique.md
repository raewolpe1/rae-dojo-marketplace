# Multidimensional Poverty Critique

## When to Use
- When advising on whether to adopt the MPI as a national or international poverty measure.
- When critiquing a proposal to replace a consumption-based headcount with a multidimensional index.
- When constructing an alternative to the MPI for monitoring purposes.
- When responding to claims that the MPI captures dimensions of poverty money cannot buy.

## Fails When
- **The audience wants a one-sentence dismissal of the MPI.** The MPI rests on a serious framework; the critique deserves an argument, not a verdict.
- **No alternative dashboard is proposed.** A critique that does not articulate what the user should do instead has limited force.
- **You treat the debate as political.** The substantive issue is methodological: what the index obscures vs. what it reveals.

## Core Concept
The Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), developed by Sabina Alkire and James Foster and adopted by UNDP and OPHI, identifies the multidimensionally poor as those who experience deprivation across a weighted combination of indicators in three dimensions: education (years of schooling, child enrolment), health (nutrition, child mortality), and living standards (cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, assets). A household is multidimensionally poor if its weighted deprivation score exceeds a cut-off (typically one-third).

The MPI has substantial appeal. It claims to capture dimensions of poverty money cannot directly buy. It produces a single composite number that fits media communication. It is calculable from existing household surveys (Demographic and Health Surveys, Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys) that may not have consumption modules. It has been adopted by several countries as a complement or substitute for consumption-based poverty measurement.

The critique runs at several levels.

First, the weights. The MPI assigns one-third of the total weight to education, one-third to health, and one-third to living standards. Within dimensions, indicators receive equal weight. These weights are not derived from a welfare-theoretic framework; they are chosen by convention. The implicit trade-offs the weights embed — for instance, that a household with no electricity is twice as deprived as one with no improved water source within the same dimension, or that a year of schooling is interchangeable with a child mortality reduction — are not justified by the underlying capability framework the MPI invokes. The weights cannot be defended; they can only be asserted.

Second, the cut-off. The decision that a household is multidimensionally poor if its deprivation score exceeds one-third is a threshold no different in kind from a money-metric poverty line — except that the money-metric line is anchored to a basic-needs basket while the MPI cut-off is anchored to nothing. A household just above the cut-off is treated as non-poor; one just below is poor. The discontinuity has no welfare-theoretic foundation.

Third, the aggregation. The MPI multiplies the headcount (share of population that is multidimensionally poor) by the average intensity of deprivation among the poor. The product is a single number that combines incidence and intensity. It is presented as a poverty measure but conceals the underlying dimensions and the trade-offs among them. A country can improve its MPI by reducing the headcount without addressing the deepest deprivations, or by reducing the depth without addressing the breadth — and the index will not distinguish these patterns.

Fourth, the substitution for money-metric measurement. The MPI is sometimes advocated as a replacement for the consumption-based headcount on the grounds that the consumption measure misses non-monetary dimensions. The argument confuses two questions: whether consumption poverty is a sufficient measure (it is not) and whether the MPI is the best alternative (it is not, compared to a dashboard).

The alternative — the dashboard — reports each dimension separately. The headcount on the consumption-based poverty line is one indicator. The schooling indicator is another. The malnutrition indicator is another. The water and sanitation indicators are others. The dashboard makes the trade-offs explicit; the analyst and the policy-maker see what the country is doing well on, what it is failing on, and where the policy response should concentrate. The composite index conceals exactly the information that policy design requires.

## How to Apply
1. **Build the dashboard.** Identify the core consumption-based poverty measure and the priority non-monetary indicators (health, education, water and sanitation, housing). Report each separately.
2. **Document the joint distribution.** Show overlap between consumption poverty and non-monetary deprivations. This is where the dashboard goes beyond what either money-metric or non-monetary measures alone reveal.
3. **Resist the composite as the headline.** If the political demand for a single headline number is unavoidable, report the consumption-based headcount as the headline and the dashboard underneath. Do not let the MPI become the headline.
4. **Engage the indicator-level evidence.** Each indicator in the dashboard has a literature on the appropriate measurement methodology, threshold, and policy response. The dashboard makes that literature relevant; the composite makes it irrelevant.
5. **Be transparent about the limits of the consumption measure.** A consumption measure cannot capture all dimensions of welfare. Acknowledge this. Build the dashboard to complement the consumption measure, not to apologise for it.

## Examples
**Situation:** A government proposes replacing its consumption-based national poverty rate with an MPI on the grounds that the consumption measure misses health and education deprivations.
**Application:** The motivation is sound. The proposed response is not. The MPI does aggregate health and education with consumption proxies, but it does so with weights that conceal trade-offs. A dashboard that reports consumption poverty alongside health (under-five mortality, stunting), education (years of schooling, enrolment), and basic-services indicators (water, sanitation, electricity) captures the same dimensions, more transparently. The dashboard is what policy needs; the composite is what the press release will quote.
**Result:** Adopt the dashboard. If a single composite is required for international reporting, use the consumption-based headline and report the dashboard as the substantive measure.

**Situation:** A development partner argues that the country's MPI fell from 0.12 to 0.08, demonstrating success.
**Application:** Look at the dashboard underneath. Did consumption poverty fall? Did stunting fall? Did school enrolment rise? Did water access improve? The MPI movement may have been driven by improvement on one indicator (water access, which improves quickly with infrastructure investment) while another (stunting, which moves slowly) stayed flat. The MPI movement is a summary that may or may not reflect broad-based improvement. The dashboard is what reveals what actually happened.
**Result:** Do not let the MPI substitute for substantive policy assessment. Use the dashboard.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the MPI as a refinement of consumption-based measurement.
**Why:** It is not a refinement. It is a substitution with different properties. Treating it as a refinement misrepresents the methodological choice.

**Don't:** Adopt the MPI because it is internationally fashionable.
**Why:** International fashion is not a methodological argument. The MPI's adoption by several countries reflects political and institutional dynamics, not a settlement of the methodological debate.

**Don't:** Use the MPI as the only poverty measure.
**Why:** Even if you accept the MPI as informative, it cannot substitute for a consumption-based measure that captures command over goods and services. They answer different questions. Both can be useful; neither is sufficient alone.
