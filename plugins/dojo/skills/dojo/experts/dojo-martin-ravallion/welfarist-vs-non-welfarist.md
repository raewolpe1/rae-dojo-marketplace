# Welfarist vs Non-Welfarist Approaches

## When to Use
- When the design of a poverty measurement system has to justify its welfare-theoretic foundation.
- When responding to capability-approach critiques of money-metric measurement.
- When advising on whether to complement money-metric measures with non-monetary indicators.
- When defending the choice of consumption as the welfare metric against income, wealth, or composite indices.

## Fails When
- **The user wants a philosophical adjudication between the two frameworks.** The choice is partly philosophical, but the operational question is empirical: which framework supports which class of analysis.
- **The user wants to use the capability framework as a stick to beat money-metric measurement without engaging the alternatives the framework offers.** Capability-approach critiques deserve serious engagement, not dismissal.
- **The audience confuses the welfarist-vs-non-welfarist debate with the headcount-vs-FGT debate or the absolute-vs-relative debate.** These are independent dimensions of choice.

## Core Concept
Welfarist approaches to poverty measure deprivation in terms of welfare — typically a money-metric proxy for utility (consumption per capita or per adult equivalent), justified by the argument that money commands command over the goods and services that produce utility. The case for money-metric measurement rests on a specific welfarist premise: that command over private consumption captures most of the welfare-relevant variation across households at the lower end of the distribution.

Non-welfarist approaches measure deprivation in terms of objective indicators of functioning — what a person actually does or can do — independent of preferences or subjective utility. The capability approach, associated with Amartya Sen, holds that what matters intrinsically are the capabilities (real opportunities) and functionings (achieved states) of persons. Command over commodities is instrumentally valuable insofar as it expands capabilities, but the capability framework does not collapse welfare into commodity command.

The capability framework is philosophically attractive: it captures dimensions of deprivation (illiteracy, ill health, social exclusion, lack of voice) that money cannot directly purchase, and it gives an account of welfare that is responsive to interpersonal heterogeneity in the conversion of resources to functionings.

The operational challenge for the capability framework is the move from framework to index. The capability approach identifies the principle (we care about capabilities, not commodities) but is largely silent on operationalisation: which capabilities, with what cut-offs, with what weights, aggregated how. When the framework is operationalised, the operational choices collapse into a list of indicators, cut-offs, and weights — each of which is a choice that nobody can defend on the underlying capability framework. The composite multidimensional poverty index claims the philosophical authority of the capability approach while making operational choices that the framework cannot adjudicate.

The well-implemented welfarist measure with money-metric proxies has, by contrast, a clear operational foundation. The line is anchored to a basic-needs basket; the welfare metric is consumption per adult equivalent; the equivalence scale embeds a specific assumption about needs that can be defended or contested explicitly; the FGT measures are derived from clear axioms. The choices are made and can be examined.

The defensible position is not "money-metric measurement is right, capability measurement is wrong" but "money-metric measurement, complemented by a dashboard of non-monetary indicators, captures most of what policy needs to know, with clearer foundations than composite capability indices." The dashboard makes the trade-offs explicit; the composite obscures them.

## How to Apply
1. **Use consumption per adult equivalent as the primary welfare metric.** Document the equivalence scale. Defend the choice against per-capita and against alternative scales.
2. **Complement with a dashboard of non-monetary indicators.** Health (malnutrition, mortality), education (attendance, attainment), water and sanitation, housing quality. Report these alongside the money-metric measure, not aggregated with it.
3. **Engage the capability critique on its own terms.** When the capability framework points to a deprivation the money-metric measure misses, ask whether the missed deprivation is correlated with the money-metric measure (in which case the dashboard captures it) or independent (in which case it is a separate dimension worth its own measurement).
4. **Resist composite indices as the headline summary.** A composite index conceals the relative weighting choices behind a single number that looks scientific but rests on indefensible weights. The dashboard is more honest.
5. **Be explicit about the welfare-theoretic foundation.** Whether you adopt a welfarist or non-welfarist framework, document the foundation and the operational choices the framework forces you to make.

## Examples
**Situation:** A country debates replacing its consumption-based poverty rate with a multidimensional poverty index covering income, education, health, and housing.
**Application:** The proposal sounds like it captures more of what matters. In practice, the MPI aggregates dimensions with weights that nobody can defend on welfare-theoretic grounds — the weights are pulled from convention, not from a principled framework. The capability approach that the MPI invokes does not say what the weights should be. The dashboard of consumption poverty plus the non-monetary indicators captures the same dimensions, more transparently, and lets the analyst and policy-maker see what the trade-offs are.
**Result:** Adopt the dashboard. Report consumption poverty as the headline. Report the non-monetary indicators in their own right. If a single composite is required for media purposes, construct it transparently and report the dashboard alongside.

**Situation:** A capability theorist argues that consumption-based poverty cannot capture the welfare loss from social exclusion.
**Application:** Engage the argument. Ask which specific empirical implication of social exclusion is missed by a well-measured consumption metric. If the answer is that an excluded household has higher unmet needs at the same consumption level — because of stigma, denied access, or denied opportunities — then a per-capita measure misses the welfare loss and a needs-weighted equivalence scale partly captures it. If the answer is that the excluded household lacks dimensions money cannot buy (voice, dignity, recognition), then a dashboard of indicators capturing those dimensions is the appropriate complement.
**Result:** The critique has substance; the response is to expand the measurement system, not to abandon money-metric measurement.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat money-metric measurement as philosophically settled.
**Why:** The welfarist foundation is contestable, and treating it as settled misses the productive engagement with the capability tradition that has improved the field.

**Don't:** Treat the capability approach as a single operational instrument.
**Why:** The capability framework is a normative position with many possible operationalisations. The MPI is one operationalisation; it is not the framework. Critiques of the MPI are not critiques of the framework.

**Don't:** Collapse the welfarist-vs-non-welfarist debate into the absolute-vs-relative debate.
**Why:** These are independent. A welfarist measure can be absolute or relative. A non-welfarist measure can be absolute or relative. Confusing the two dimensions produces incoherent positions.
