# Conditional Cash Transfers

## When to Use
- When designing or evaluating a cash transfer programme.
- When advising on whether to add conditions to an existing transfer.
- When comparing CCTs to unconditional transfers, workfare, or in-kind programmes.
- When responding to claims that the CCT literature has demonstrated the value of conditions.

## Fails When
- **Supply-side capacity is missing.** Conditions on school attendance or health-clinic visits require schools and clinics with sufficient capacity to absorb the additional demand. Where the supply side is constrained, the conditions force a transfer of utilisation without producing additional service.
- **The audience wants an ideological answer.** The empirical case for conditions is more equivocal than either advocates or critics often admit.
- **You treat CCTs as a single homogeneous instrument.** Programme designs vary widely; the lessons from Progresa do not transfer mechanically to other contexts.

## Core Concept
Conditional cash transfers (CCTs) condition the receipt of cash on observable household behaviours, typically school enrolment for children, regular health-clinic visits for pregnant women and young children, and nutrition monitoring. The instrument originated in Mexico's Progresa (later Oportunidades, then Prospera) in the late 1990s and was adopted by dozens of countries in subsequent decades. Brazil's Bolsa Família, Colombia's Familias en Acción, and many others followed the basic design.

The case for conditions rests on a paternalistic-but-economic argument: poor households may underinvest in children's human capital because of liquidity constraints, time horizons, intra-household bargaining failures, or externalities. Conditioning the transfer on behaviours that build human capital corrects the underinvestment and produces long-run gains that the cash alone would not deliver.

The empirical literature on CCTs has documented substantial effects on the conditioned behaviours — school enrolment rises, clinic visits increase, immunisation rates improve. These effects are real and replicated across many programmes. The effects on the deeper outcomes the conditions are supposed to produce — learning, child health, adult earnings — are more equivocal. Enrolment can rise without learning rising; clinic visits can rise without health outcomes rising. The conditions buy the conditioned behaviour reliably; they buy the welfare gain that motivates the behaviour less reliably.

The harder empirical question is whether the conditions add value over an unconditional transfer of the same size. The literature comparing CCTs to UCTs in the same setting finds smaller differences than the unconditional comparison to no-programme would suggest. UCTs raise school enrolment too — through the income effect on the demand for schooling. The marginal effect of the conditions, over and above the unconditional transfer, is often small.

If the conditions add little marginal effect, they impose real costs. Administering the conditions requires monitoring, verification, and penalty enforcement — substantial administrative costs that the unconditional transfer does not require. The conditions create eligibility gaps that exclude the poorest households (those with the most constrained ability to comply). And the conditions create stigma and complexity that depress take-up.

The defensible position is not that CCTs are universally superior or inferior to UCTs but that the marginal value of conditions depends on context. Where supply-side capacity is constrained and the income effect on demand is large, conditions add little. Where supply-side capacity is robust and there are strong externalities or behavioural biases, conditions may add value.

Ravallion's substantive position has been that the case for conditions has been overstated in the literature, that the cash matters more than the conditions, and that simpler unconditional designs are often preferable.

## How to Apply
1. **Assess supply-side capacity first.** Are there schools and clinics with capacity to absorb additional demand? Without that capacity, conditions force a transfer of utilisation without producing additional service.
2. **Estimate the income effect on the conditioned behaviour.** How much would school enrolment rise from an unconditional transfer of the same size? If the income effect is large, the marginal effect of the conditions is small.
3. **Calculate the administrative cost of the conditions.** Monitoring, verification, penalty enforcement — these are substantial costs that do not arise for unconditional transfers. The administrative cost is the price of the conditions; it must be compared to the marginal welfare gain.
4. **Identify the excluded poor.** Conditions exclude households that cannot comply — often the poorest, with the most constrained ability to send children to school regularly or attend clinics. The exclusion is a welfare cost the unconditional design would avoid.
5. **Consider the political economy.** Conditional designs are often more politically palatable than unconditional designs because they invoke a behavioural quid pro quo. This is a real consideration but should not be confused with a welfare-economic case for conditions.

## Examples
**Situation:** A country proposes a CCT for poor households with school-age children, with eligibility conditional on attendance. The expected effect is to raise enrolment by 8 percentage points.
**Application:** Compare to an unconditional transfer of the same size. If the income effect of an unconditional transfer would raise enrolment by 5 percentage points, the marginal effect of the conditions is 3 percentage points. The administrative cost of conditions, the exclusion of households that cannot comply, and the stigma effect have to be valued against the 3-point marginal effect. The case for conditions is empirically weaker than the headline 8-point figure suggests.
**Result:** Consider the unconditional design as a serious alternative. The marginal effect of conditions is smaller than the literature commonly implies.

**Situation:** A CCT programme is evaluated as having raised school attendance, but learning outcomes are flat.
**Application:** This is the recurring pattern. Enrolment is responsive to the conditions; learning is not, because the supply-side quality of schooling has not changed. The CCT has bought attendance without buying the human-capital outcome the attendance was supposed to produce. The welfare gain is the cash transfer; the conditions have transferred utilisation without producing additional service.
**Result:** Either invest in the supply side alongside the demand-side CCT, or reconsider the design.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat the CCT literature's evidence of enrolment effects as evidence that conditions add welfare value.
**Why:** Enrolment effects are real but mostly attributable to the income effect of the cash. The marginal effect of conditions is smaller.

**Don't:** Adopt CCTs without supply-side analysis.
**Why:** Conditions without supply-side capacity force utilisation transfers without service delivery — the conditions transfer queueing rather than produce welfare gain.

**Don't:** Assume the political appeal of conditions is an economic argument.
**Why:** Political palatability is a real consideration but not a welfare-economic case for conditions. Be explicit about which argument you are making.
