# Living an Examined Life

## When to Use
- When someone is ready to stop living on autopilot and begin taking responsibility for their inner world.
- When someone recognises the gap between the life they are living and the life their soul requires.
- When someone asks: "How do I become more conscious?"

## Core Concept
Hollis takes Socrates' dictum — "The unexamined life is not worth living" — and gives it psychological teeth. The examined life is not intellectual self-analysis. It is the sustained, courageous practice of asking: What is running my life that I do not know about? Where am I living from my complexes rather than from my values? What am I avoiding?

The examined life requires regular engagement with several key questions. Hollis often formulates these as diagnostic probes that bypass the ego's defences:

Where does this pattern come from? Every repeating pattern — in relationships, in career, in emotional life — has a history. Tracing the pattern to its origin in childhood does not eliminate it but makes it visible, which changes the ego's relationship to it from unconscious repetition to conscious carrying.

Whose voice is speaking? When you hear the inner critic, the perfectionist, the people-pleaser, the anxious worrier — whose voice is that? Usually it is a parental voice, internalised decades ago and operating as though it were your own considered judgment. Distinguishing between internalised voices and your own authority is the central act of the examined life.

What would I do if I were not afraid? This question bypasses the ego's elaborate rationalisations and exposes the avoidance beneath. Most stuckness is fear disguised as reasonableness.

Does this choice make me larger or smaller? The practical test for every significant decision. Growth always produces anxiety; diminishment always produces a false sense of relief.

## How to Apply
1. **Establish a reflective practice.** Journaling, therapy, contemplative practice — the form matters less than the regularity. The unconscious responds to sustained attention, not occasional interest.
2. **Ask the diagnostic questions daily.** Not as a mechanical exercise but as a genuine inquiry. Where did I act from a complex today? Where did I avoid something I know matters? Where was I living someone else's script?
3. **Accept discomfort as data.** The examined life is not comfortable. It continually reveals gaps between who you think you are and how you actually behave. This discomfort is not failure — it is the feeling of consciousness expanding.
4. **Act on what you discover.** Insight without action is entertainment. The examined life demands that you change your behaviour in response to what you learn about yourself.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man realises, through journaling, that he has spent his entire career seeking the approval of authority figures — bosses, mentors, senior colleagues — in a pattern that mirrors his relationship with his father.
**Application:** This is the examined life producing its characteristic fruit: consciousness of a governing complex. The next step is not merely to understand the pattern but to act differently. Where is he currently seeking approval? Can he make a decision this week based on his own judgment rather than on what he believes an authority figure would endorse? Can he tolerate the anxiety of not receiving approval? Each act of independent judgment weakens the complex's hold — not by eliminating it but by establishing the ego's authority alongside it.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Make the examined life an intellectual exercise.
**Why:** Self-analysis that stays in the head and never reaches the heart or the hands is the ego's substitute for actual transformation. The examined life is embodied, enacted, and relational — not merely cognitive.

**Don't:** Examine yourself into paralysis.
**Why:** Excessive self-examination can become its own avoidance — the person who endlessly analyses their patterns without ever acting differently. At some point, the examined life requires a leap.
