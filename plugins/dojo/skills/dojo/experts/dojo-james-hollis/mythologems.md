# Mythologems

## When to Use
- When someone recognises that their life follows a narrative pattern they did not consciously choose.
- When myths, fairy tales, or stories grip someone with inexplicable emotional power.
- When mythological amplification can illuminate a personal situation that resists purely personal analysis.

## Core Concept
A mythologem is a core narrative motif — a recurring story pattern that appears across cultures and historical periods because it maps a universal human psychological experience. The hero's journey. The descent to the underworld. The search for the lost paradise. The battle with the dragon. The night sea journey. These are not quaint relics of pre-scientific cultures — they are the psyche's own narratives, and they are being lived, right now, by every person reading this.

Hollis uses mythologems as diagnostic and therapeutic tools. When a person's life is stuck, one productive question is: what myth are you living? The man who keeps starting over — new city, new career, new relationship — may be living a version of the eternal wanderer, the Odysseus who cannot find his way home because he does not know where home is. The woman who sacrifices everything for others may be living a version of the devouring mother myth, having identified with nurturing to the exclusion of her own life. The person who avoids all risk may be refusing the hero's call — the summons to departure that every mythological hero must answer.

Myths do not prescribe behaviour — they illuminate patterns. Recognising that you are living a particular mythologem does not tell you what to do, but it tells you what the psyche is working on and what the pattern typically demands. The hero must face the dragon. The wanderer must find home. The prisoner must escape the tower. These are not literal prescriptions but symbolic pointers toward the psychological work that the pattern requires.

## How to Apply
1. **Identify your mythologem.** What recurring narrative pattern structures your life? Are you the wanderer? The rescuer? The exile? The sleeper who needs to wake? The myth you are living shapes what feels possible and impossible.
2. **Read the myth's demands.** Every mythologem has a developmental arc. The hero must face the dragon — what is your dragon? The prisoner must escape the tower — what tower are you in? The wanderer must find home — where is your home?
3. **Use stories as mirrors.** The films, novels, and stories that grip you with inexplicable intensity are mirrors of your own mythologem. What story are you unable to stop thinking about? That story is telling you something about your own situation.
4. **Consciously step into the myth's next stage.** If you recognise that you are living the hero's refusal of the call, step into the departure. If you are living the exile, begin the return. Consciousness of the mythologem makes conscious participation possible.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man recognises that he has spent his entire adult life "building the kingdom" — acquiring, expanding, consolidating — and now, in his fifties, feels that the kingdom is hollow.
**Application:** He has been living the king-builder mythologem — the figure who constructs a realm through will and effort. But every mythological king reaches a point where the kingdom itself becomes the prison, and the king must descend (undergo a symbolic death) to find what his conquests could not provide: meaning, soul, the dimension of life that power cannot reach. This man's hollowness is the mythologem's inflection point — the moment where the story demands descent rather than further expansion. The question is whether he will heed the demand or continue building, which in mythological terms means becoming the tyrant — the king who rules over an empty realm.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Force mythological readings onto people.
**Why:** Mythological amplification works when the person recognises their pattern in the myth. Imposed from outside, it is merely intellectual and often feels invasive. Start with the person's own associations; reach for myth only when the personal level has been exhausted.

**Don't:** Use mythology to avoid the personal.
**Why:** "I'm on a hero's journey" can become a grandiose avoidance of the mundane, personal work that the psyche requires. Mythology illuminates the personal — it does not replace it.
