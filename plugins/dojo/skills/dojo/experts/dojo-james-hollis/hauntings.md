# Hauntings

## When to Use
- When someone recognises they are repeating a parent's pattern — the same career dissatisfaction, the same relationship dynamic, the same emotional avoidance.
- When someone is haunted by paths not taken and lives not lived.
- When someone wants to understand the intergenerational dimension of their psychological struggles.

## Fails When
- **You blame the parents.** Understanding that parental complexes shape your life is not an accusation against your parents — they were shaped by their parents, who were shaped by theirs. The point is not blame but consciousness: seeing the pattern so you can stop transmitting it.
- **You promise liberation from the past.** You cannot un-inherit what was transmitted to you. You can make it conscious, which changes your relationship to it — from unconscious repetition to conscious carrying.

## Core Concept
Hollis uses the metaphor of haunting for the way unlived life persists across generations. What a parent could not face, could not live, could not integrate becomes an invisible inheritance — a complex that the child absorbs and often enacts without knowing its origin.

The father who suppressed his creativity to be "practical" may produce a son who is haunted by a creative impulse he cannot explain. The mother who surrendered her independence to be "a good wife" may produce a daughter who feels simultaneously compelled toward and terrified of autonomy. The grandparent who survived war through emotional shutdown may produce a family line where feelings are dangerous and must be controlled.

These hauntings operate through the family's emotional atmosphere — what is said and unsaid, what is permitted and forbidden, what is celebrated and suppressed. Children absorb these atmospheric messages before they have language to process them. The family's unlived material becomes the child's unconscious agenda.

The unlived life also haunts the person who did not live it. Every choice forecloses other choices. The career pursued means the career abandoned. The relationship maintained means the relationship not explored. At midlife, the accumulated weight of unlived possibilities presses for recognition — not as regret (which is the ego's response) but as grief (which is the soul's response to genuine loss).

The therapeutic task is not to live the unlived life literally (you cannot go back to twenty-five) but to honour it — to acknowledge what was sacrificed, to grieve it, and to find ways to give expression to its essential energy in the present.

## How to Apply
1. **Map the family pattern.** What was your parents' unlived life? What did they sacrifice? What were they afraid of? What did they refuse to feel? Then ask: where does that pattern show up in your own life?
2. **Distinguish your life from your inheritance.** Not everything you feel is yours. Some of your depression may be your mother's unlived grief. Some of your anxiety may be your father's unresolved fear. Consciousness begins with the question: "Is this mine?"
3. **Grieve what was not lived.** The paths you did not take are real losses. Honour them as losses rather than dismissing them as irrelevant. The grief, fully experienced, liberates the energy that was bound up in the haunting.
4. **Break the transmission.** What you make conscious, you stop transmitting. The pattern that has passed unconsciously through generations ends with the person who sees it clearly and takes responsibility for carrying it differently.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man in his fifties realises that his father — a gifted musician who abandoned music for accounting — never recovered from the sacrifice, and that his own persistent, inexplicable sadness may be connected to his father's unlived creative life.
**Application:** This is intergenerational haunting. The father's unlived creativity became an atmospheric message: creative expression is a luxury you cannot afford; practical survival comes first. The son absorbed this message and built a practical life that mirrors his father's — and now carries the same inexplicable sadness. The work is dual: first, to recognise that some of this sadness is not his own but his father's, transmitted through the family atmosphere. Second, to engage with his own creative impulse — not to become his father's proxy but to break the pattern by giving creative expression the room his father could not. He does not need to become a musician. He needs to stop suppressing whatever creative impulse lives in him, thereby ending the haunting for both himself and for any children who might inherit it.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Use intergenerational dynamics as an excuse — "My parents made me this way."
**Why:** Understanding the inheritance increases responsibility, not decreases it. Once you see the pattern, you are accountable for what you do with it.

**Don't:** Try to "fix" your parents' unlived life.
**Why:** Your parents' unlived life is theirs. You can honour it, you can grieve it, but you cannot live it for them. Your task is your own unlived life — which may be related to theirs but is not identical.
