# Hollis Applied to Life Practice

## When to Use
- When someone has read Hollis (or absorbed his ideas through this dojo) and asks: "What do I actually do with this?"
- When someone wants a daily or weekly practice structure grounded in depth psychology.
- When someone is doing inner work without a therapist and needs practical guidance.

## Fails When
- **You mechanise the practices.** These are not steps in a self-improvement programme. They are ways of attending to the psyche — of keeping the dialogue with the unconscious alive. The moment they become routine obligations, they lose their power.
- **You promise results.** Depth psychological practice does not produce neat outcomes on a timeline. It produces consciousness, which produces change — but on the psyche's schedule, not the ego's.

## Core Concept
Hollis is a writer and analyst, not a self-help author, and he is suspicious of programmes, steps, and techniques. But across his body of work, a coherent practice emerges — a set of disciplines through which the ordinary person can sustain the examined life.

**The daily question practice.** Hollis repeatedly returns to a small set of diagnostic questions that, asked honestly and regularly, do more for self-knowledge than years of casual self-reflection. The core questions: What is running my life that I do not know about? Whose voice is speaking when I hear the inner critic, the people-pleaser, the anxious worrier? Does this choice make me larger or smaller? What would I do if I were not afraid? Where am I living from my complexes rather than my values? These questions are not meant to be answered quickly. They are meant to be carried — returned to daily, journalled about, lived with until the honest answer surfaces.

**Journalling as dialogue.** Hollis values journalling not as a diary or a record of events but as a dialogue with the unconscious. The practice: write about what is disturbing you — the dream you had, the emotion that surprised you, the pattern you noticed. Then ask: what is the psyche telling me through this? What does this material want from me? The unconscious responds to sustained attention. A journal kept regularly becomes a record of the psyche's communications over time — and the patterns that emerge are often revelatory.

**Dream attention.** Not dream interpretation as an intellectual exercise, but dream attention as a practice. Keep a notebook by the bed. Record whatever fragments survive. Do not rush to interpret — sit with the images. Over time, the dream material begins to speak on its own terms. Dreams compensate for the one-sidedness of conscious life; attending to them broadens the personality.

**The enlargement check.** Before every significant decision, pause and apply Hollis's foundational test: does this choice make me larger or smaller? Not happier or safer — larger or smaller. Growth always produces anxiety; diminishment always produces a false sense of relief. Use this check not as a rigid rule but as a compass.

**Shadow tracking.** Notice when you have a disproportionate emotional reaction — rage at a colleague, contempt for a public figure, infatuation with a stranger. Ask: what in me is being activated? What disowned quality am I projecting? Shadow tracking is uncomfortable and endlessly productive.

**Periodic life review.** Quarterly or at natural transition points, review the trajectory of your life against Hollis's core question: am I living the life my soul requires, or am I still living the provisional personality's script? This is not an audit for the purpose of self-criticism but for the purpose of course correction.

## How to Apply
1. **Start with the daily questions.** Choose one of Hollis's diagnostic questions and carry it for a week. Journal about it each evening. Let it work on you rather than trying to force an answer.
2. **Establish a journalling practice.** Even ten minutes a day, focused on inner life rather than outer events. What disturbed you today? What pattern did you notice? What dream fragment do you recall?
3. **Apply the enlargement check to one decision this week.** Not a trivial decision but a real one — a career choice, a relationship boundary, an avoidance you are considering. Larger or smaller?
4. **Track one shadow projection this week.** Identify someone who irritates or fascinates you disproportionately. Ask: what quality in them am I refusing to see in myself?
5. **Build gradually.** These practices are cumulative. Start with one and add others as the first becomes habitual. The psyche responds to sustained attention, not to bursts of enthusiasm followed by neglect.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man in his mid-fifties, having read several Hollis books, feels he understands the ideas intellectually but has not changed his behaviour. He still avoids conflict, still defers to authority, still feels flat. He wants to know what is missing.
**Application:** What is missing is practice. Understanding Hollis intellectually is the ego's way of absorbing the material without being changed by it. The shift from understanding to transformation requires embodied action. Concrete prescription: this week, journal each evening about one moment where he deferred to authority or avoided conflict. Apply Hollis's question: whose voice was speaking in that moment — his own or his father's? Next week, choose one situation where he would normally defer and instead act from his own authority — state his actual opinion, make his own decision, tolerate the anxiety of not being validated. The insight is not enough. The practice makes the insight real.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Turn these practices into a self-improvement programme with milestones and metrics.
**Why:** The ego will co-opt any practice and turn it into a project. The point is not to become a better self-improver but to become more conscious — which means remaining open to what the psyche reveals rather than directing the process toward the ego's goals.

**Don't:** Do this work entirely alone if you are in crisis.
**Why:** Independent practice is valuable for ongoing self-knowledge. But if you are in acute psychological distress — depression, anxiety, relationship crisis, suicidal ideation — seek professional support. Hollis himself is an analyst; he knows that some work requires a trained companion.
