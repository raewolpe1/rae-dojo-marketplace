# Living Between Worlds

## When to Use
- When someone is in the gap between an ended chapter and one that has not yet begun.
- When someone has lost their certainties — about career, identity, relationship, faith — and does not yet have new ones.
- When someone is experiencing the disorientation that comes when the old map no longer describes the territory.

## Core Concept
Hollis recognises that modern life is characterised by the dissolution of the shared myths, institutions, and certainties that once provided existential orientation. We are, collectively and individually, living between worlds — between the old certainties that have failed and the new understanding that has not yet formed.

Individually, this between-worlds experience intensifies at midlife but can occur at any significant transition. The retirement that dissolves a career identity. The divorce that dissolves a relational identity. The spiritual crisis that dissolves a worldview. The death of a parent that dissolves the last link to childhood. In each case, the person finds themselves in liminal space — having left one shore but not yet arrived at the other.

Liminality is deeply uncomfortable because the ego demands clarity, direction, and control — and liminal space provides none of these. The old answers have stopped working, and new answers have not yet formed. The temptation is to grab the first available certainty — a new ideology, a new relationship, a new career, a self-help programme that promises to replace the lost map. But premature closure aborts the transformation that liminality makes possible.

The gift of between-worlds living is precisely the dissolution of false certainties. The maps that have failed were always incomplete; their failure creates the possibility of a more truthful orientation. But this possibility can only be realised by enduring the disorientation rather than fleeing it.

## How to Apply
1. **Name the liminality.** "I am between worlds. The old map has failed. The new one has not yet appeared. This is where I am." Naming creates a small island of consciousness in the disorientation.
2. **Resist premature closure.** Do not grab the first available answer. The anxiety of not-knowing is the price of genuine transformation. Let the questions live longer than is comfortable.
3. **Attend to what is emerging.** In liminal space, the psyche is working on something. Dreams become vivid. Unexpected interests arise. Old certainties feel hollow. These are signs of the new orientation forming — not in the ego's preferred clarity but in the unconscious's symbolic language.
4. **Find companions for the passage.** Liminality is not meant to be endured alone. A therapist, a trusted friend, a community of honest seekers — relationship provides the holding environment that the dissolved structures no longer offer.

## Examples
**Situation:** A man who has defined himself through his career for thirty years is facing involuntary retirement. He feels as though the floor has disappeared. Friends say "Enjoy your freedom!" but he feels only terror and disorientation.
**Application:** His friends are offering first-adulthood advice to a second-adulthood crisis. "Enjoy your freedom" assumes that the self exists independently of the career identity — but for this man, the career was the identity. Its removal is not liberation but dissolution. He is between worlds: the world of the professional self has ended, and the world of whoever he is about to become has not yet formed. The work is to endure the gap. Not to rush into volunteering, consulting, or golf as identity replacements, but to let the question sit: who am I when I am not what I do? The answer will come — but only to the person who can tolerate the not-knowing long enough for it to form.

## Anti-Patterns
**Don't:** Treat liminality as a problem to be solved quickly.
**Why:** The ego wants a new map immediately. But the liminal period is when the psyche does its most important work — dissolving the old identity and composting it into the new. Rushing this process produces a new persona rather than a new self.

**Don't:** Romanticise the disorientation.
**Why:** Liminality is not glamorous. It is frightening, lonely, and often depressing. Romanticising it avoids the genuine difficulty. Acknowledge the pain while trusting the process.
